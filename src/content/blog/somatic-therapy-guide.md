A new ache, persistent tension, or difficulty moving comfortably deserves thoughtful attention, not guesswork. Somatic therapy can help you explore how physical sensations and emotional experiences may influence one another, but it cannot identify the medical cause of pain. That distinction matters, especially when symptoms are new, worsening, or affecting your daily activities. A clinician may use conversation, body awareness, or gentle grounding practices to support emotional wellbeing and help you notice personal patterns. You remain in control of the pace and can ask to change or stop an exercise. Here’s what to know before considering somatic therapy as part of your care.

## Key Takeaways

* **Somatic therapy adds body awareness to psychotherapy**: It can help you observe how stress and emotions relate to physical sensations, without treating those sensations as a diagnosis.
* **Choose a pace and approach that feel manageable**: Ask about exercises in advance, share health or mobility concerns, and pause or adjust activities whenever needed.
* **Coordinate therapy with your care team**: Somatic therapy may complement medical, rehabilitation, or mental health care, but new or worsening symptoms still need medical assessment.

## What Is Somatic Therapy?

### Connect the body and mind

Somatic therapy is a body-centered form of psychotherapy that combines conversation with attention to physical sensations, movement, and breathing. A therapist may help you notice how stress or emotion shows up physically, such as a tight jaw, shallow breathing, or tense shoulders. The goal is to build awareness and explore ways to respond to these cues with more steadiness.

This approach recognizes that mental and physical experiences can influence each other. It does not mean that trauma is literally stored in a particular muscle or joint, or that every physical symptom has an emotional cause. A [guide to somatic therapies](https://www.contemporaryrehabservices.com/post/what-are-somatic-therapies-a-clear-guide-for-2026) describes the approach as bringing bodily experience into psychotherapy to help address emotional distress.

### Compare it with talk therapy and physical rehabilitation

Talk therapy often explores thoughts, feelings, relationships, and past experiences through conversation. Somatic therapy may address those same topics, while also asking you to notice what is happening in your body as you talk. A therapist might ask what a sensation feels like, or whether it changes when you shift your attention or try a grounding exercise.

Somatic therapy is different from physical rehabilitation. Physical therapy typically works toward goals such as improving strength, balance, mobility, or function after an injury or illness. Somatic therapy focuses on emotional wellbeing and the relationship between physical experience and mental health. It is not a substitute for medical assessment or rehabilitation. The [Trauma Therapist Institute’s overview](https://www.traumatherapistinstitute.com/trauma-therapist-institute-blog/what-is-somatic-therapy) explains how somatic psychotherapy combines awareness of the body with traditional therapeutic approaches.

### See how stress and trauma affect sensation, movement, and daily function

Stress can affect sleep, breathing, muscle tension, concentration, and how comfortable you feel moving through daily life. These experiences may overlap with chronic pain, reduced mobility, or other health concerns. It is important not to assume that a physical symptom has a psychological cause. A qualified medical professional can assess new, persistent, or worsening symptoms.

In somatic therapy, you may practice noticing physical responses without judgment and explore ways to feel more grounded. The goal is not to push through discomfort or interpret every sensation as a sign of trauma. Instead, the work can help you recognize patterns and choose how to respond. This reflects the [mind-body perspective](https://www.chicagointegrativepsychotherapy.com/does-somatic-therapy-really-work-common-myths-explained) that mental and physical health can affect each other.

## How Does Somatic Therapy Work?

Somatic therapy brings attention to body sensations as you explore emotions, stress, or difficult experiences. Instead of focusing only on talking about past events, a therapist may invite you to notice what is happening in the present: perhaps your jaw feels tight, your breathing changes, or your shoulders relax. These sensations can help you observe your responses, but they do not have one fixed meaning and cannot diagnose a health condition.

A session may involve conversation, quiet reflection, or a simple exercise. Your therapist should explain what they suggest and work with you to find a pace that feels manageable. You can ask questions, decline an activity, or pause. Approaches vary, and somatic therapy is not a replacement for medical care, physical rehabilitation, or other mental health treatment when those are appropriate. If you have chronic pain, mobility limitations, or a health condition that may affect an exercise, discuss it with your clinician before trying it.

### Track body sensations and nervous-system cues

A therapist may invite you to notice physical sensations without trying to change them right away. You might check whether your shoulders feel tense, your breathing feels quick, or your hands feel warm or cool. The aim is to observe with curiosity, not to assume that a sensation has a particular emotional cause.

You may also notice whether sensations shift as you discuss a stressful topic, then compare them with how you feel during a neutral or reassuring moment. Over time, this practice may help you recognize your personal responses and consider what feels supportive. Somatic therapy uses body awareness alongside conversation to explore emotional distress, as described in this overview of [somatic therapy and the body](https://joyfullivingbh.com/trauma-and-the-body-exploring-somatic-therapy/).

### Try grounding, orienting, breathwork, and body scans

Some somatic therapy sessions include exercises that bring your attention to the present. Grounding might involve feeling your feet on the floor or noticing the chair supporting you. Orienting means looking around and taking in details in your surroundings. A body scan guides you to notice sensations in different areas, without needing to judge or change them.

A therapist may also suggest gentle breath awareness or guided imagery. You do not need to force a deep breath or make a sensation disappear. Instead, you can notice how an exercise feels and tell your therapist if you want to adjust or stop. Common practices include [grounding, breathwork, body scans, and gentle movement](https://joyfullivingbh.com/trauma-and-the-body-exploring-somatic-therapy/).

### Use gentle movement, resourcing, and gradual pacing

Some sessions include small, comfortable movements, such as shifting your weight or stretching your hands. Your therapist may also help you identify a resource, something that feels supportive or reassuring. It could be a calming image, a person you trust, a steady breath, or the feeling of sitting in a comfortable place. These supports can give you something to focus on while exploring difficult emotions.

The work should proceed at a pace that feels manageable to you. You might focus on a brief moment, take a pause, or return to a topic another time. Somatic therapy can combine awareness of physical sensations with conversation and conscious movement. You can learn more about this [mind-body approach to therapy](https://www.traumatherapistinstitute.com/trauma-therapist-institute-blog/what-is-somatic-therapy).

### Expect consent, choice, and the option to pause

Somatic therapy should be collaborative. A therapist may invite you to notice a sensation or try an exercise, but you can ask questions, decline, or request another approach. You should not feel pressured to share details or continue an activity that feels uncomfortable. Discussing your goals and agreeing on how to pause can help make expectations clear before you begin.

Tell your therapist about pain, mobility concerns, sensory sensitivities, or health conditions that could affect movement or breathing exercises. You can also ask what an exercise involves before trying it. A client-led approach supports choice and lets you move at your own pace, as described in this guide to [somatic therapy and collaborative care](https://elisplace.org/healing-the-mind-and-body-understanding-somatic-therapy/).

## Who May Benefit From Somatic Therapy?

Somatic therapy may be worth discussing if you want to understand how stress, emotions, and physical sensations affect one another. It can support work on trauma-related concerns, anxiety, or body awareness, but it is not a replacement for medical or mental health care. A qualified clinician can help you decide whether it fits your needs, health history, and comfort level.

### Address trauma-related concerns, including PTSD

Trauma can affect more than thoughts and memories. You may notice changes in breathing, posture, muscle tension, or your sense of safety. Somatic therapy can help you pay attention to these responses at a manageable pace and practice ways to feel grounded. It may be included in care for trauma-related concerns, including post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), when guided by a qualified mental health professional.

A supportive therapist should explain each exercise, respect your boundaries, and give you the option to pause. You do not have to share details of a traumatic experience before you feel ready. For more on how trauma may show up physically, read this overview of [somatic therapy and the mind-body connection](https://elisplace.org/healing-the-mind-and-body-understanding-somatic-therapy/).

### Manage anxiety, stress, and emotional regulation challenges

If anxiety or stress leaves you feeling tense, overwhelmed, or disconnected from your body, somatic therapy may help you notice those patterns and practice responding to them. A therapist might guide you through grounding, gentle breath awareness, or noticing sensations in the present moment. The goal is not to force relaxation or revisit difficult experiences before you feel ready.

Instead, sessions can help you identify what feels manageable and develop coping skills over time. If you find it difficult to regulate emotions or recover after stressful situations, ask a mental health professional whether this approach may suit you. This overview explains how somatic therapy may support [awareness and emotional regulation](https://www.chicagointegrativepsychotherapy.com/does-somatic-therapy-really-work-common-myths-explained).

### Build awareness of chronic pain, tension, and physical cues

People living with chronic pain or persistent tension may find it useful to explore how physical sensations and emotional stress influence each other. Somatic therapy does not determine the medical cause of pain or replace an evaluation by a healthcare professional. It may help you notice patterns, such as when your muscles tense, how your breathing changes, or which situations affect how you feel.

That awareness can give you useful information to share with your care team as part of a broader treatment plan. If pain is new, worsening, or interfering with daily activities, seek medical advice. Learn more about how [mental and physical health can interact](https://therapygroupdc.com/therapist-dc-blog/strengths-and-weaknesses-of-somatic-therapy).

### Consider age, mobility, sensory, and health changes

Somatic therapy should be adapted to your mobility, sensory needs, health conditions, and comfort. You can ask about seated exercises, clear verbal instructions, a quieter space, or extra time to work through an activity. If balance, hearing, vision, or transportation is a concern, discuss possible adjustments before starting.

Age alone does not determine whether therapy is appropriate. Share relevant health information with your clinician, and check with your medical team if you are unsure how an exercise may affect you. Older adults have varied needs, and the [National Center for PTSD’s guidance on older adults](https://www.ptsd.va.gov/professional/treat/specific/assess_tx_older_adults.asp) identifies mobility and sensory changes as considerations when planning care.

## Weigh Potential Benefits and Limitations

Somatic therapy may help you notice how emotions, stress, and physical sensations interact. Its usefulness depends on your needs, the approach your clinician uses, and how comfortable you feel with the process. Consider both potential benefits and practical limits before adding it to your care plan.

### Build body awareness, coping skills, and emotional regulation

Somatic therapy encourages you to notice cues such as muscle tension, shallow breathing, or restlessness. With a clinician’s guidance, you can practice responding with grounding, gentle movement, or other strategies that feel manageable. Recognizing these cues earlier may help you choose how to respond when stress rises.

This approach may be worth exploring if distress often shows up in your body or if talk therapy alone has not addressed that part of your experience. It should not mean assuming that pain or other symptoms have a psychological cause. A qualified clinician can help you explore sensations while remaining attentive to your overall health. Read more about the [strengths and limitations of somatic therapy](https://therapygroupdc.com/therapist-dc-blog/strengths-and-weaknesses-of-somatic-therapy) as you consider whether it fits your goals.

### Review the research and its limits

Research suggests that some forms of somatic therapy may ease symptoms associated with post-traumatic stress disorder, anxiety, and depression. However, somatic therapy includes different methods, and the evidence is still developing. Findings from one approach or group of participants may not apply to every person, so results can vary.

Ask a prospective clinician what method they use, what outcomes they track, and how they will adapt sessions to your needs. Be cautious of claims that promise a cure or a particular result. Somatic therapy is one option to discuss with a licensed mental health professional, who can explain how it may fit with other forms of care. The [available overview of benefits and limitations](https://therapygroupdc.com/therapist-dc-blog/strengths-and-weaknesses-of-somatic-therapy) can help you prepare questions for that conversation.

### Consider discomfort, emotional intensity, access, and varied progress

Focusing on physical sensations can feel uncomfortable, particularly when a sensation brings up distressing memories or emotions. Closing your eyes, sitting in silence, or doing a body scan may not feel right for everyone. You should be able to ask for changes, keep your eyes open, or pause an exercise. A trauma-informed clinician will check in and respect your choices. [Guidance on somatic techniques for trauma clinicians](https://clinicalevents.org/somatic-therapy-techniques) explains why mindfulness practices are not automatically comfortable or appropriate for every trauma survivor.

Progress varies, too. You may need time to feel comfortable with a practice, and some strategies may suit you better than others. Cost, provider availability, mobility, and sensory preferences can also affect access. Before starting, share any concerns and ask how sessions can be adapted to your comfort and physical abilities.

### Use somatic therapy alongside medical and mental health care

Somatic therapy can complement care from a physician, physical therapist, or mental health professional. It should not replace medical assessment for new, worsening, or unexplained symptoms. This is especially important if you experience chronic pain, numbness, balance changes, or reduced mobility, as these symptoms may need medical evaluation.

Ask your care team how somatic therapy could fit with your treatment plan and day-to-day goals. Body awareness practices may support rehabilitation, while mental health care can address emotional concerns that affect daily life. Some clinicians combine somatic and cognitive approaches when treating complex trauma. A [guide to somatic therapies](https://www.contemporaryrehabservices.com/post/what-are-somatic-therapies-a-clear-guide-for-2026) discusses this approach. Choose qualified providers, and ask whether they can coordinate with your other clinicians when appropriate.

## Integrate Somatic Therapy With Rehabilitation and Medical Care

Somatic therapy may complement medical care by helping you notice how pain, stress, and movement feel in your body. It does not replace medical assessment, physical therapy, or mental health treatment. If you are managing chronic pain, mobility changes, or another health concern, discuss somatic practices with your care team and connect them to goals that matter to you, such as walking more comfortably or returning to a favorite activity.

### Pair body awareness with physical therapy, pain management, and mobility goals

Share with your physical therapist or medical provider which movements feel difficult, what brings relief, and whether symptoms change with stress or fatigue. This information can add context to assessments of strength, balance, and mobility. During an exercise, a clinician might invite you to notice your breathing or muscle tension and adjust the activity if needed. The aim is to support safe, functional movement, not to push through pain.

Somatic approaches can bring attention to nervous-system responses alongside movement and other rehabilitation measures. Read [The Embody Lab’s guide to somatic therapy training for physical therapists](https://theembodylab.com/blogs/articles/somatic-therapy-training-for-physical-therapists-complete-guide), then ask your provider how a practice fits your treatment plan.

### Coordinate care with qualified, trauma-informed clinicians

If you see both a mental health professional and a medical or rehabilitation provider, let them know what information you are comfortable sharing. With your permission, coordination may help keep care aligned with your goals. A trauma-informed clinician should explain exercises, respect your boundaries, and offer choices, including changing or stopping an activity.

Somatic practices may include grounding, mindful movement, breathwork, or noticing physical sensations. When paired with psychotherapy, these approaches can connect reflection with awareness of the body, as [CASAT OnDemand explains](https://casatondemand.org/2025/06/12/bridging-the-mind-and-body-the-evolving-landscape-of-psychotherapy-through-somatic-integration). Ask about a clinician’s training and experience, and how they adapt sessions to your health, mobility, and comfort.

### Ask about training, safety, pacing, and home practices

Before starting, ask what training the practitioner has, what a session involves, and how they respond if an exercise brings up discomfort or strong emotions. You can also ask whether they have experience supporting people with chronic pain, mobility changes, or other health conditions. A qualified clinician should answer clearly and help you understand your choices without pressure.

Agree on how to pause or adjust an exercise, and discuss a pace that feels manageable. If you receive home practices, ask how often to do them, what sensations to expect, and when to stop and contact your care team. The [Trauma Therapist Institute’s overview of somatic therapy](https://www.traumatherapistinstitute.com/trauma-therapist-institute-blog/what-is-somatic-therapy) recommends asking whether a therapist’s training and approach suit your needs and safety.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is somatic therapy the same as somatic exercises or physical therapy?**\
No. Somatic therapy is a form of psychotherapy that includes awareness of physical sensations and may use grounding, breath awareness, or gentle movement. Physical therapy focuses on improving movement and function. A medical professional can help determine which type of care fits your needs.

**Can somatic therapy help with chronic pain?**\
It may help you notice how stress and emotions interact with your experience of pain, but it cannot identify or treat the medical cause. Tell your healthcare provider about new, worsening, or unexplained pain, and discuss somatic therapy as one possible part of a broader care plan.

**What should I expect during a first session?**\
The therapist will usually ask about your goals, health history, and comfort with different practices. You may talk, notice a physical sensation, or try a simple grounding exercise. You can ask questions, decline an activity, or pause at any time.

**How do I choose a somatic therapist?**\
Ask about the therapist’s qualifications, the method they use, and their experience with concerns like trauma, chronic pain, or mobility changes. It is also reasonable to ask how they adapt exercises and respond if you feel uncomfortable.

**Can I try somatic therapy if I have limited mobility or a health condition?**\
Possibly. Practices can often be adjusted, for example by using seated exercises or focusing on sensations without movement. Share relevant health information with the therapist, and check with your medical team if you are unsure whether an exercise is suitable.
