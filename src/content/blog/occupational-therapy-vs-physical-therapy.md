Staying independent often depends on more than strength or mobility alone. A sore joint can affect how you walk, but it may also make bathing, dressing, or preparing food harder. That’s why it can be useful to understand occupational therapy vs physical therapy before choosing where to begin. PT typically addresses movement, balance, strength, and joint function. OT focuses on taking part in everyday activities, sometimes by practicing tasks or finding practical ways to adapt them. Read on to learn how each therapist can support your goals, what an evaluation may include, and how OT and PT can work together when your needs overlap.

## Key Takeaways

* **OT supports daily activities**: An occupational therapist can help you manage tasks like dressing, cooking, using your hands, and staying safe at home.
* **PT addresses movement and physical function**: A physical therapist can assess strength, balance, walking, joint mobility, and pain to create a plan around your goals.
* **Start with what you want to do more easily**: Describe your challenges to a clinician, who can help determine whether OT, PT, or both may fit your needs.

## How Do OT and PT Differ?

Occupational therapy (OT) and physical therapy (PT) both help people improve function and participate in daily life, but they focus on different parts of that process. OT looks at the activities a person needs or wants to do, and how to make them safer, easier, or more manageable. PT focuses more directly on movement and the physical abilities that support tasks such as walking, climbing stairs, and maintaining balance.

The distinction can be useful when pain, weakness, or changes in mobility affect more than one part of life. For example, someone with knee pain might work with a physical therapist on walking and leg strength, while an occupational therapist helps them get dressed or prepare meals with less strain. These approaches can complement one another, and a person may benefit from one or both depending on their circumstances.

Neither therapy is limited to a single diagnosis or age group. A therapist considers a person’s symptoms, abilities, environment, and priorities when shaping care. Both disciplines can help people work toward greater independence and participation, as [UCLA Health explains in its comparison of OT and PT](https://medschool.ucla.edu/news-article/occupational-therapy-vs-physical-therapy). A clinician can help determine which evaluation or combination of services may fit a person’s needs.

### OT: daily activities, independence, and participation

Occupational therapy helps people develop, regain, or adapt the skills they use in everyday activities. An OT may work with someone on bathing, dressing, cooking, managing household tasks, or returning to a hobby. The focus is not only on what the body can do, but also on how a person can complete meaningful tasks in their home and community.

For adults experiencing joint pain, reduced hand function, or changes in stamina, an OT may suggest a different way to perform a task, practice a specific skill, or use adaptive equipment. The plan reflects the person’s goals and daily routines. Husson University’s overview of occupational therapy describes its role in helping people take part in daily activities and live more independently.

### PT: movement, strength, balance, and physical function

Physical therapy focuses on movement and physical abilities that affect how a person gets around and carries out activities. A PT may assess strength, flexibility, balance, walking, joint motion, and pain. Treatment may include guided exercises, mobility practice, balance training, and strategies for moving more comfortably and safely.

For adults over 50, PT may be considered when stiffness, weakness, balance changes, or pain make walking, climbing stairs, or staying active more difficult. The therapist builds a plan around the person’s health, abilities, and goals, then adjusts it as needs change. [Raleigh Orthopaedic’s explanation of physical therapy](https://www.raleighortho.com/blog/physical-therapy/physical-therapy-vs-occupational-therapy-what-is-the-difference/) describes PT as addressing movement and physical problems that limit mobility.

### What “occupation” means in OT

In occupational therapy, “occupation” does not refer only to paid work or a career. It means the meaningful activities that make up a person’s day, from personal care and cooking to driving, gardening, writing, and spending time with family. These activities differ from person to person, so an OT considers what someone wants or needs to do, not just their diagnosis.

For instance, if hand stiffness makes it difficult to button a shirt or hold a gardening tool, an OT can look at the task and explore ways to make it more manageable. Husson University’s guide to OT explains how everyday activities are central to the profession. This focus connects therapy to a person’s routines and priorities.

### Shared goals: pain relief, mobility, recovery, and staying active

OT and PT can both support recovery, mobility, and participation in daily life. Their roles may overlap, but each brings a different perspective. A physical therapist might work on leg strength and balance, while an occupational therapist helps someone apply those abilities to tasks such as getting in and out of the shower or carrying groceries.

Pain can affect both movement and everyday routines. Depending on its cause and the person’s needs, a therapist may help identify ways to move or complete tasks with greater comfort, while coordinating with other clinicians when appropriate. The aim is to work toward functional goals that matter to the individual. [UCLA Health’s comparison of OT and PT](https://medschool.ucla.edu/news-article/occupational-therapy-vs-physical-therapy) notes that both therapies tailor care to each person’s needs and can support greater independence.

## How Do OT and PT Assess and Treat Different Needs?

Occupational therapy (OT) and physical therapy (PT) both help you take part in daily life with greater comfort and confidence. OT focuses on how your health affects everyday activities, while PT focuses on movement and physical function. Your needs and goals will help determine whether one approach or both may be useful.

### OT assessment: routines, self-care, hand function, cognition, and home barriers

An occupational therapist will ask about your routines and the activities you want to do more easily. These might include dressing, bathing, cooking, managing medications, using your hands, or enjoying a hobby. Your OT may also look at fine motor skills, memory, attention, and how your home setup affects safety and independence.

The assessment helps identify specific barriers. Hand pain, for example, may make it hard to fasten buttons, while balance concerns can make getting in and out of the shower feel less secure. Your therapist may watch you perform a task and ask what feels challenging. This [overview of occupational therapy](https://www.raleighortho.com/blog/physical-therapy/physical-therapy-vs-occupational-therapy-what-is-the-difference/) explains how care can support daily activities. Sharing your priorities helps your OT focus on what matters most to you.

### PT assessment: movement, strength, balance, walking, and physical impairments

A physical therapist assesses how you move and what may be limiting your mobility. Depending on your concerns, they may check joint motion, strength, flexibility, posture, balance, walking, and transitions such as standing up from a chair. They’ll also ask about your health history, pain, and the activities you find difficult.

The assessment helps your PT understand how physical changes affect your daily function. If you feel unsteady while walking, for example, they may look at your balance, leg strength, and walking pattern. The findings help shape a plan around your needs, whether you’re managing joint problems, recovering from surgery, or hoping to move more comfortably. This [PT assessment overview](https://medschool.ucla.edu/news-article/occupational-therapy-vs-physical-therapy) describes how therapists evaluate movement and physical impairments. Tell your PT which activities you want to return to, such as climbing stairs or walking outdoors.

### OT treatments: task practice, adaptive equipment, routines, and home modifications

OT treatment often includes practicing activities that matter to you. Your therapist may break a task into steps, teach a different way to complete it, or help you create a routine that supports safety and conserves energy. If arthritis makes gripping uncomfortable, for instance, you might practice using kitchen tools with larger handles.

Your OT may also recommend adaptive equipment or home changes, such as grab bars or moving frequently used items within easy reach. The right options depend on your abilities, goals, and surroundings. OT treatment approaches can address daily tasks, hand skills, and other factors that affect participation. Let your therapist know which activities are most important to you, so you can work on strategies that fit your life.

### PT treatments: exercise, gait and balance training, and mobility work

PT treatment may include therapeutic exercise, hands-on techniques, and practice with movements you use every day. Your therapist might work with you on joint mobility, leg strength, balance, or walking. If rising from a chair is difficult, for example, your PT may help you practice the movement while building the strength and control it requires.

Your plan should reflect your abilities, symptoms, and goals. Exercises may feel challenging, but your therapist can adjust them to your response. Speak up if a movement causes pain, feels unsafe, or is hard to repeat. [Common physical therapy treatments](https://medschool.ucla.edu/news-article/occupational-therapy-vs-physical-therapy) include exercise and gait or balance training to support mobility. Your PT can help connect each part of treatment to activities you want to do, from walking around the neighborhood to managing stairs more comfortably.

### Set goals, track progress, and adjust care

OT and PT are active partnerships between you and your therapist. You’ll set goals based on the activities you want to manage more comfortably. A goal might be preparing a meal with less difficulty, walking farther, or feeling steadier while getting dressed. Tracking progress helps you and your therapist see what is improving and where you may need more support.

Your plan can change as your symptoms, abilities, or priorities shift. Between appointments, notice what feels easier and what remains challenging, then share that information with your therapist. Your therapist guides and teaches you, while your participation helps put those strategies into practice. [UCLA’s overview of OT and PT](https://medschool.ucla.edu/news-article/occupational-therapy-vs-physical-therapy) describes therapy as a collaborative process. Ask how an exercise or daily-living strategy connects to your goals if you’re unsure.

### Clear up misconceptions: OT isn’t just job training, and PT isn’t just for athletes

In occupational therapy, “occupation” means the meaningful activities that fill your day, not only paid work. OT can support self-care, household tasks, hobbies, and participation in your community. If a health condition makes it harder to button a shirt, prepare food, or return to a favorite activity, an OT can help you find practical ways to take part.

PT is not just for athletes. People also work with physical therapists to address pain, balance concerns, joint stiffness, walking difficulties, and recovery after surgery. You don’t have to be an athlete to benefit from an assessment, and your treatment should match your needs and abilities. Tell your therapist if an activity causes discomfort so you can discuss adjusting it. ## Which Therapy Fits Your Needs?

The best place to start is with the activities you want to do more comfortably, safely, or independently. If dressing, preparing meals, using your hands, or managing routines has become difficult, occupational therapy (OT) may help you adapt daily tasks and your surroundings. If walking, balance, strength, or joint movement is limiting you, physical therapy (PT) may be a better fit.

These needs often overlap. For example, knee pain may make walking harder and also make bathing or getting dressed more challenging. A therapist can assess how your symptoms affect your daily life and recommend a plan based on your goals, abilities, and health history.

Before an appointment, note which activities are difficult, when symptoms tend to occur, and what you would like to do more easily. Being specific can help your clinician identify whether OT, PT, or both may be useful. A plan should reflect what matters to you, whether that means moving around your home with confidence, returning to a hobby, or keeping up with family and friends.

### When OT can help with dressing, cooking, hand use, memory, or home safety

OT may help when pain, stiffness, weakness, or changes in memory affect everyday routines. An occupational therapist can look at how you manage activities such as dressing, bathing, cooking, using your hands, or keeping track of daily tasks. Depending on your needs, they may practice an activity with you, suggest a different technique, or recommend adaptive equipment and changes to your home.

For example, if arthritis makes it difficult to open jars, an OT may suggest tools or hand positions that make the task easier. If you feel unsteady while getting dressed, they can help you consider safer ways to do it and arrange your space. [Occupational therapy focuses on daily activities](https://www.raleighortho.com/blog/physical-therapy/physical-therapy-vs-occupational-therapy-what-is-the-difference/) and can tailor strategies to your priorities.

### When PT can help with walking, joint movement, strength, balance, or mobility-related pain

PT may be a good fit when pain or physical changes affect how you move. A physical therapist can assess your walking, joint movement, strength, flexibility, and balance, then recommend treatment based on your goals. Sessions may include guided exercises, gait or balance practice, and advice on moving safely.

Consider asking about an evaluation if knee stiffness makes stairs difficult, back pain limits your walks, or balance concerns make you less confident going out. PT may also be part of recovery after an injury or surgery. The plan can focus on practical goals, such as standing more comfortably, walking farther, or returning to an activity you enjoy. Learn more about how [physical therapy addresses movement and mobility](https://www.raleighortho.com/blog/physical-therapy/physical-therapy-vs-occupational-therapy-what-is-the-difference/).

### How chronic pain, joint problems, neuropathy, or surgery recovery shape therapy needs

A diagnosis by itself doesn’t determine which therapy is right for you. What matters is how your symptoms affect movement and everyday activities. Chronic pain or joint problems may make walking, reaching, or dressing difficult. Neuropathy can affect sensation and balance, while recovery after surgery may involve rebuilding strength and learning how to manage daily tasks safely.

Your clinician can help match therapy to your needs. PT may focus on movement, strength, or balance; OT may help you adapt tasks and your home setup. Some people benefit from both, with therapists coordinating their work around shared goals. The [UCLA guide to OT and PT](https://medschool.ucla.edu/news-article/occupational-therapy-vs-physical-therapy) outlines how the two therapies can address different aspects of function and recovery.

### Support independence, meaningful activities, and social participation as you age

Therapy goals should connect to the parts of life you want to maintain. You may want to prepare meals, tend a garden, meet friends, travel, or move around your home with greater confidence. OT and PT can both support these aims, though they often take different approaches: OT focuses on participating in daily activities, while PT focuses on movement and physical function.

Tell your therapist about specific priorities, not just symptoms. For example, explain that you want to walk to a nearby café or fasten buttons more easily. Clear goals help shape practical treatment and give you a way to track changes that matter to you. [Occupational therapy supports participation in everyday activities](https://westcoastuniversity.edu/blog/difference-between-ot-and-pt), including activities that help people stay independent and connected.

### Ask a clinician about evaluation and treatment plans

If you’re unsure where to start, ask your primary care clinician or specialist whether an OT or PT evaluation makes sense. Describe what has changed, which activities are difficult, and whether your symptoms vary throughout the day. You can also ask if both types of therapy may be helpful, what treatment may involve, and how your progress will be reviewed.

Therapy works best when you take an active role. Your therapist can guide and teach you, while your feedback helps shape the plan. Ask what you can practice safely between visits, how often you should meet, and when the plan might be adjusted. The [UCLA overview of occupational and physical therapy](https://medschool.ucla.edu/news-article/occupational-therapy-vs-physical-therapy) explains how patient participation is part of both therapies.

## How Do OT and PT Work Together in Rehabilitation?

OT and PT can work as a team to help you move more safely and use your abilities in daily life. Your therapists may coordinate treatment around what matters most to you, from getting around your home to returning to favorite activities.

### Pair movement gains with daily-living strategies

PT may work on balance, walking, or the strength to stand from a chair. OT can help you use those abilities during everyday tasks, such as dressing, bathing, cooking, or moving around your home. After a fall, for example, PT may practice walking and balance while OT looks at how you manage stairs and self-care. This combination connects therapy exercises with the activities you want to do more safely and independently. Your therapists can also suggest practical changes, such as using a grab bar or rearranging items to make daily routines easier. [See how OT and PT can complement each other after a fall](https://alexandriahc.com/resources/short-term-rehab-pt-vs-ot-differences/).

### Coordinate goals and share progress across the care team

Your OT and PT may work toward related goals, such as walking safely to the kitchen and preparing a meal without becoming overly tired. By sharing observations, they can identify what is improving and where you may need more support. Your feedback helps shape that plan: tell your therapists which tasks feel difficult and what you hope to do again. Both therapies involve active participation, with therapists teaching and guiding you as you practice skills and strategies. The [UCLA Medical School overview of OT and PT](https://medschool.ucla.edu/news-article/occupational-therapy-vs-physical-therapy) explains the importance of patient involvement. Coordinated goals help keep sessions connected to your daily priorities.

### Involve family or caregivers when appropriate

With your permission, a family member or caregiver can take part in therapy discussions or learn ways to support practice at home. They might help you follow safety strategies, provide the right level of assistance, or share details about routines that are difficult. Therapists can also discuss home setup, such as keeping frequently used items within reach or reducing trip hazards. OT may address cognitive, sensory, and emotional factors that affect participation, in addition to physical tasks. The [University of the Cumberlands overview of OT](https://www.ucumberlands.edu/blog/occupational-therapy-vs-physical-therapy) describes these broader areas of care. Involvement should match your wishes and encourage you to do as much as you safely can.

### Adapt therapy as symptoms, abilities, and priorities change

Pain, fatigue, balance, strength, and confidence can change during recovery. Your therapists can adjust activities to reflect how you feel and what you can manage, while keeping safety in focus. If joint pain makes dressing difficult, for example, your OT may recommend a different technique or an adaptive tool, while your PT works on movement and strength. Let both therapists know if symptoms change, an activity becomes harder, or your goals shift. They can reassess your needs and update the plan. [Therapists adapt activities to each person’s needs](https://alexandriahc.com/resources/short-term-rehab-pt-vs-ot-differences/), helping keep treatment relevant as your abilities and priorities evolve.

## Where Do OTs and PTs Work?

Occupational therapists (OTs) and physical therapists (PTs) work in many settings, including hospitals, outpatient clinics, rehabilitation centers, and patients’ homes. The setting often depends on a person’s health needs, goals, and stage of recovery. For example, a PT may help someone rebuild walking strength after surgery, while an OT may help them manage everyday tasks safely as they return home.

Both therapists can contribute to a person’s care, sometimes as part of the same team. Their services may look different from one setting to another, but treatment should connect to what matters in your daily life: moving with more confidence, managing routines, or staying involved in activities you enjoy. [UCLA’s overview of OT and PT](https://medschool.ucla.edu/news-article/occupational-therapy-vs-physical-therapy) describes the range of settings where these professionals work.

### Hospitals and rehabilitation centers

In a hospital, OTs and PTs may help people regain function after surgery, illness, or injury. A PT may practice walking, transfers, strength, or balance. An OT may work on everyday activities such as getting dressed, bathing, or preparing for a safe return home.

Rehabilitation centers provide more structured therapy for people who need ongoing support before returning to their usual routines. A care team may include both professions, with therapists coordinating around shared priorities such as safety, mobility, and independence. The length and type of therapy depend on a person’s needs and the services available at the facility.

### Outpatient clinics and sports medicine facilities

Outpatient clinics provide therapy through scheduled appointments, without an overnight stay. People may seek care for joint pain, reduced strength, balance concerns, or recovery after surgery. PTs in these settings may guide exercises and movement practice to support daily activities or a return to recreation.

OTs also work in outpatient clinics. They may address hand function, daily tasks, or ways to adapt activities when pain or limited movement gets in the way. For adults who want to stay active, therapy goals might include walking more comfortably, gardening, or managing household routines. [UCLA’s guide](https://medschool.ucla.edu/news-article/occupational-therapy-vs-physical-therapy) lists outpatient clinics and sports medicine facilities among common PT work settings.

### Homes and community settings

Home-based therapy lets people practice skills where they use them every day. An OT may observe how someone manages dressing, bathing, or moving around the kitchen, then recommend strategies or equipment to make tasks easier and safer. A PT may work on walking, transfers, strength, and balance within the home.

Therapists may also work in skilled nursing facilities, home health agencies, and community programs. These settings can help address practical concerns, such as stairs, limited space, or the need for caregiver support. The [University of the Cumberlands’ overview](https://www.ucumberlands.edu/blog/occupational-therapy-vs-physical-therapy) notes that OTs and PTs work across a variety of care and community settings.

### Schools and mental health settings for OT

OTs may work in schools to help students take part in learning and school routines. Depending on a student’s needs, support may involve classroom tasks, handwriting, organization, or using tools and equipment. The focus is on helping students participate in everyday school activities as independently as possible.

OTs also work in mental health settings. They may help people build routines, manage self-care, and take part in meaningful activities. The therapist’s role depends on the setting and the person’s goals. These settings may be less familiar to adults seeking physical rehabilitation, but they show how occupational therapy can support participation in daily life. The [University of the Cumberlands explains](https://www.ucumberlands.edu/blog/occupational-therapy-vs-physical-therapy) that OT work can take place in schools and mental health facilities.

### How care settings shape treatment

A therapist’s setting shapes what they can assess and practice with you. In a clinic, sessions may focus on exercises, strength, range of motion, or balance. At home, a therapist can see how you manage daily tasks in your actual living space and suggest practical changes. In a hospital or rehabilitation center, care may focus on immediate safety and preparing for the next stage of recovery.

When discussing therapy, share what you want to do more comfortably or confidently, whether that means walking, managing household routines, or returning to a favorite activity. Ask how treatment in that setting can support your goals and whether your therapists will coordinate with other clinicians. As your needs change, your care plan may need to change with them.

## What Education and Licenses Do OTs and PTs Need?

Occupational therapists (OTs) and physical therapists (PTs) follow different education paths, but both must pass a professional exam and meet state licensing rules before practicing. If you’re choosing a therapist, confirming their license is a useful first step. You can also ask about their experience with your goals, such as managing joint pain, improving balance, or returning to everyday activities.

### OT education, certification, and state licensure

In the United States, occupational therapists complete an entry-level master’s or doctoral degree from a program accredited by the [Accreditation Council for Occupational Therapy Education](https://acoteonline.org/). Their education includes supervised fieldwork, where they learn to help people take part in daily activities, adapt tasks, and build independence at home and in the community.

After graduation, candidates must pass the [NBCOT certification exam](https://www.nbcot.org/), known as the Occupational Therapist Registered (OTR) exam. They then apply for a license in the state where they plan to practice. Each state sets its own requirements, which may include additional documentation or a background check. When considering OT, ask whether the therapist has experience with your needs, such as hand function, home safety, or managing daily routines.

### PT education, licensure, and continuing education

Physical therapists earn a Doctor of Physical Therapy (DPT) degree from a program accredited by the Commission on Accreditation in Physical Therapy Education. Their training combines academic coursework with supervised clinical education. This prepares them to assess and treat concerns involving movement, strength, balance, and physical function.

After earning a DPT, candidates must pass the [National Physical Therapy Examination](https://www.fsbpt.org/Our-Services/Candidate-Services/National-Exam-NPTE) and meet their state’s licensing requirements. Most states also require continuing education to renew a license, though the rules and required hours vary. If you’re seeking help with walking, joint movement, or recovery after surgery, ask whether the PT regularly works with people who have similar concerns and how they’ll track your progress.

### Compare career paths, work settings, and specialties

OTs and PTs may both work in hospitals, outpatient clinics, rehabilitation centers, and home health. Their settings often reflect their different areas of focus. OTs also commonly work in schools, mental health services, and long-term care, where they help people take part in daily activities. PTs may work in sports medicine and orthopedic clinics, focusing on movement and physical function.

Both professions offer opportunities to develop expertise in specific areas. An OT may focus on hand therapy or adapting daily tasks, while a PT may specialize in orthopedics, balance, or sports rehabilitation. A therapist’s setting or specialty can offer useful context, but the key is whether their experience fits your goals. Husson University’s overview of OT and PT outlines common work settings for each profession.

### Check a therapist’s credentials

Before beginning care, confirm that the therapist has an active license in the state where you’ll receive treatment. State licensing boards typically offer online tools to check a professional’s license status. You can ask the clinic for the therapist’s full name, professional designation, and license information to help with your search.

OTs who pass the NBCOT exam may use the OTR designation. PTs must pass the NPTE and meet state licensing requirements. These credentials confirm that a therapist has met important professional standards, but they don’t show how much experience they have with a particular concern. Ask about their work with chronic pain, neuropathy, balance changes, or returning to everyday activities. The [West Coast University guide to OT and PT requirements](https://westcoastuniversity.edu/blog/difference-between-ot-and-pt) explains the exams and licensing steps for both professions.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can I receive occupational therapy and physical therapy at the same time?**\
Yes. If your needs involve both movement and daily activities, your care team may recommend OT and PT together. For example, PT may address balance while OT helps you apply safer strategies during household routines.

**How do I know which therapy to ask about first?**\
Think about what you want to do more comfortably. If walking, strength, or balance is the main concern, ask about a PT evaluation. If self-care, hand use, or managing tasks at home is difficult, ask about OT. A clinician can help you decide if both make sense.

**Do I need a doctor’s referral for OT or PT?**\
Requirements depend on your state, insurance plan, and clinic. Contact your insurer and the provider before scheduling to ask whether you need a referral or authorization.

**Can OT or PT help with chronic pain?**\
A therapist may help you manage how pain affects movement or everyday tasks. The right approach depends on the cause of your pain, your health, and your goals, so discuss treatment options with a clinician.

**What should I share at my first therapy appointment?**\
Describe the activities you find difficult, when symptoms occur, and what you hope to do more easily. Bring relevant medical information and mention any changes in your health, medications, or symptoms.
