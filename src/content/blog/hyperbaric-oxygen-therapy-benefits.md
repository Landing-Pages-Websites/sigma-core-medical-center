Hyperbaric oxygen therapy is sometimes promoted for everything from faster recovery to improved energy and longevity. But strong claims deserve careful questions, especially when a treatment involves medical risks, repeated sessions, and significant cost. HBOT has established uses for certain serious conditions, including decompression sickness and some hard-to-heal wounds. That does not mean it treats everyday aches, slows aging, or helps everyone feel more energetic. If you are considering it, start with your diagnosis and the evidence for your specific situation. This guide explains how the therapy works, when clinicians may recommend it, and what to ask before committing to treatment.

## Key Takeaways

* **HBOT treats specific medical conditions:** A qualified clinician can assess whether it fits your diagnosis and care plan.
* **Benefits and treatment schedules vary:** Ask what outcomes to expect, how progress will be measured, and when your plan will be reviewed.
* **Check safety and costs before starting:** Share your health history and medications, discuss possible side effects, and confirm fees and insurance coverage.

## What Is Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy, and How Does It Work?

Hyperbaric oxygen therapy, or HBOT, is a medical treatment in which you breathe concentrated oxygen inside a pressurized chamber. A clinician may recommend it for certain conditions that affect wound healing or tissue health. It is not a one-size-fits-all treatment: whether it’s appropriate depends on your diagnosis, medical history, and care plan.

### How pressure and concentrated oxygen deliver more oxygen to tissues

During HBOT, pressure inside the chamber rises above normal air pressure as you breathe medical-grade oxygen. The added pressure allows more oxygen to dissolve into your blood plasma, which carries it throughout the body. Depending on the chamber, you may breathe oxygen through a mask or hood, or the oxygen may fill the chamber.

Normal air contains about 21% oxygen; medical HBOT uses 100% oxygen at a prescribed pressure. The treatment protocol varies based on the condition being treated. Pressure matters because HBOT is designed to increase oxygen delivery beyond what you get by breathing oxygen at normal pressure. [University Hospitals explains how pressure supports oxygen delivery](https://www.uhhospitals.org/blog/articles/2022/09/the-healing-power-of-hyperbaric-oxygen-therapy).

### How oxygen may help heal specific wounds and injuries

Some wounds and injuries receive too little oxygen to support normal tissue repair. For eligible patients, HBOT may help the body respond by supporting the growth of new blood vessels and connective tissue. Extra oxygen may also help white blood cells fight infection. These effects can be useful in particular medical situations, but they do not mean HBOT is suitable for every wound or injury.

Care teams assess the cause and severity of a wound, along with factors such as circulation, infection, and blood sugar. HBOT, when recommended, is usually one part of a broader treatment plan that may include wound care and other medical treatment. Learn more about [hyperbaric oxygen therapy for wound healing](https://www.tuftsmedicine.org/services-treatments/wound-healing/hyperbaric-oxygen-therapy).

### How medical-grade chambers differ from mild-pressure wellness chambers

Medical HBOT uses a chamber and treatment protocol designed to deliver specific oxygen and pressure levels. Some chambers are pressurized with oxygen; others use pressurized air while oxygen is delivered through a mask or hood. Qualified staff supervise treatment and monitor patients throughout the session.

Mild-pressure wellness chambers may operate at lower pressure and may not provide the same oxygen concentration or clinical protocol. They should not be assumed to offer the same treatment as medical HBOT. If you’re considering a chamber, ask about its pressure and oxygen levels, who oversees sessions, and whether the treatment is appropriate for your diagnosis. The Cleveland Clinic’s guide to HBOT describes how medical treatment delivers oxygen under pressure.

## Which Conditions Can Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy Treat?

Hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) has specific medical uses, but it is not a general treatment for pain, reduced mobility, or age-related changes. A clinician considers the diagnosis, the severity of the condition, and whether HBOT is supported for that situation. The treatment may be recommended on its own in some circumstances or as part of a broader care plan.

### Established uses: decompression sickness, gas embolism, and carbon monoxide poisoning

HBOT is used for decompression sickness, which can happen when a diver surfaces too quickly and gas bubbles form in the body. It may also treat an air or gas embolism, when bubbles obstruct blood flow in a blood vessel. In these emergencies, treatment in a pressurized chamber can help reduce the bubbles and deliver oxygen to affected tissues.

Carbon monoxide poisoning is another established use. Carbon monoxide interferes with the blood’s ability to carry oxygen, potentially harming the brain, heart, and other organs. HBOT may be recommended as part of urgent medical care, depending on the person’s symptoms and clinical assessment. The Mayo Clinic’s overview of hyperbaric oxygen therapy describes these uses and other conditions for which clinicians may consider treatment.

### Other accepted uses: diabetic foot ulcers, crush injuries, severe infections, and radiation tissue damage

Clinicians may consider HBOT for certain hard-to-heal wounds, including some diabetic foot ulcers, particularly when standard wound care has not been enough. It may also be used for selected crush injuries, severe soft-tissue infections, gangrene, and tissue damage caused by radiation therapy. In these situations, the aim is to support oxygen delivery to injured tissue as part of an overall treatment plan.

HBOT is not appropriate for every wound or infection. The cause, location, severity, circulation, and response to other treatments all matter. A diabetic foot ulcer, for example, usually calls for careful wound care and management of diabetes; HBOT does not replace those steps. [University Hospitals lists conditions in which clinicians may use HBOT](https://www.uhhospitals.org/blog/articles/2022/09/the-healing-power-of-hyperbaric-oxygen-therapy), including diabetic ulcers, crush injuries, and radiation-related tissue damage.

### Additional uses: selected bone infections and compromised grafts or flaps

In some cases, HBOT may be considered for osteomyelitis, a bone infection that can be difficult to treat. It may also be used when a skin graft or flap is compromised because the tissue is not getting enough oxygen. Other possible uses include certain burns, arterial insufficiency ulcers, and gangrene.

These conditions call for an individual assessment. A clinician may consider the affected tissue, its blood supply, whether infection is present, what treatments have already been tried, and the potential risks of HBOT. The therapy may be combined with wound care, medication, or surgery, depending on the person’s needs. [Tufts Medicine describes HBOT’s use for selected bone infections and compromised grafts or flaps](https://www.tuftsmedicine.org/services-treatments/wound-healing/hyperbaric-oxygen-therapy). Ask your care team why they recommend it for your condition and what outcomes they will monitor.

### How diagnosis, clinical criteria, and medical evaluation determine eligibility

A condition that may be treated with HBOT does not automatically make someone a suitable candidate. A medical team first confirms the diagnosis and evaluates whether the person’s specific situation meets clinical criteria. This assessment may include a review of health history, symptoms, previous treatments, medications, and factors that could affect treatment safety.

The team should also explain the goal of therapy, how they will track progress, and what other options are available. Some conditions require a physician’s order, and treatment decisions should be based on a medical evaluation rather than a general wellness claim. [Tufts Medicine notes that a care team evaluates each person and condition before treatment](https://www.tuftsmedicine.org/services-treatments/wound-healing/hyperbaric-oxygen-therapy). Before beginning, ask your clinician how they determined HBOT may be appropriate and when they will reassess whether it is helping.

### How HBOT complements standard treatment

HBOT is often used alongside other medical care, not as a replacement for it. Depending on the condition, a treatment plan may also include wound cleaning and dressings, antibiotics, surgery, blood sugar management, or care to address circulation. For carbon monoxide poisoning or decompression sickness, HBOT may be part of urgent care after immediate assessment and stabilization.

Your clinician can explain which treatments should continue and how HBOT fits into the plan. They can also set a time to review your response and decide whether treatment should continue. The Cleveland Clinic explains that HBOT may be used with medication or surgery. Tell your care team about changes in symptoms or concerns during treatment so they can review your plan and make adjustments when needed.

## What Are the Benefits of Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy?

Hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) can help deliver extra oxygen to certain tissues, but its benefits depend on the condition being treated. It is used for specific medical problems, not as a proven general remedy for aging, low energy, or everyday aches. If you have a wound, injury, or tissue damage, a clinician can assess whether HBOT may be appropriate and what results are realistic.

### Potential benefits for eligible wounds, injuries, and radiation-related tissue damage

For some wounds and injuries, HBOT may support the body’s healing process. Pressurized oxygen increases the amount of oxygen carried in the blood, which may help it reach tissue with a poor blood supply. This can support the growth of new blood vessels and connective tissue, help the body fight infection, and reduce swelling.

HBOT is used for certain conditions, including some diabetic foot ulcers, crush injuries, severe infections, compromised skin grafts or flaps, and radiation-related tissue damage. It is usually part of a broader treatment plan, not a replacement for wound care, medication, surgery, or managing conditions such as diabetes. [University Hospitals describes how HBOT may support tissue healing](https://www.uhhospitals.org/blog/articles/2022/09/the-healing-power-of-hyperbaric-oxygen-therapy), and [Tufts Medicine outlines accepted uses](https://www.tuftsmedicine.org/services-treatments/wound-healing/hyperbaric-oxygen-therapy).

### What evidence shows about healing, pain, mobility, and recovery

The best-supported benefits relate to healing in specific conditions for which HBOT is recommended. Extra oxygen may help the body form new skin, blood vessels, and connective tissue, while supporting white blood cells as they fight infection. The evidence varies by diagnosis, so results seen with one type of wound do not necessarily apply to another. HBOT cannot guarantee healing or rule out the need for other treatments.

Pain, mobility, and recovery may improve if the underlying wound or injury heals, but HBOT is not a general pain treatment. Evidence for broad improvements in movement or athletic recovery remains limited. A [Mayo Clinic discussion of HBOT for diabetic wounds](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=QfdwLtPj5EY\&t=3) describes better improvement in some treated groups, but those findings apply to a specific condition. Ask your clinician which outcomes are relevant to your diagnosis and how progress will be measured.

### How timing, treatment length, and the broader care plan affect results

When to begin HBOT depends on the diagnosis, the severity of the condition, and other treatments you may need. A clinician can assess whether you are a suitable candidate and coordinate care. For example, treatment may take place alongside wound care, infection management, surgery, or steps to improve circulation and manage blood sugar. HBOT should not delay urgent assessment or standard treatment for a serious wound or injury.

Treatment typically involves repeated sessions. Some conditions may require 20 to 40 sessions over several weeks, with each session lasting about 90 minutes to two hours. Your schedule will depend on your condition and response to treatment. Cleveland Clinic explains how session length and frequency can vary. Your care team can monitor the target condition, review your progress, and adjust the plan if needed.

### What research supports—and doesn’t support—about anti-aging, energy, and general wellness

HBOT is sometimes promoted for anti-aging, increased energy, or general wellness, but these claims go beyond its established medical uses. Researchers continue to study possible applications, yet current evidence does not show that HBOT reliably reverses aging, improves everyday energy, or provides proven benefits for healthy people seeking general wellness. The fact that oxygen delivery changes under pressure does not, on its own, demonstrate a meaningful health benefit.

Before considering treatment for a non-approved or unproven use, ask what specific outcome the provider expects, what evidence supports it, and how risks will be managed. Be cautious of promises of broad or guaranteed results. Cleveland Clinic notes that evidence is insufficient for several promoted uses. You can also use [Harvard Health’s overview of evidence-based uses and unproven claims](https://www.health.harvard.edu/healthy-aging-and-longevity/hyperbaric-oxygen-therapy-evidence-based-uses-and-unproven-claims) to prepare questions for a qualified clinician.

## What Are the Risks and Side Effects of Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy?

Hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) is generally well tolerated when it’s appropriate for a person’s health needs and delivered by a qualified medical team. Still, the pressure changes and high oxygen levels can cause side effects, and rare complications can be serious. A careful pre-treatment assessment helps your care team weigh potential benefits against risks and prepare for your individual needs.

### Common effects: ear or sinus pressure, temporary vision changes, and claustrophobia

As pressure rises inside the chamber, you may feel fullness or discomfort in your ears or sinuses, much like during takeoff or landing on a plane. Swallowing, yawning, or using an ear-clearing technique recommended by staff may help. Let your care team know if discomfort persists, rather than trying to tolerate it without support.

Some people experience temporary blurred vision or a shift toward nearsightedness, particularly after multiple sessions. These changes usually improve after treatment ends, but report them to your provider. The enclosed space may also feel uncomfortable if you’re prone to claustrophobia. Mention this before your first session so staff can explain what to expect and discuss ways to help you feel more at ease. The Cleveland Clinic’s guide to HBOT side effects describes these possible reactions.

### Less common serious risks: oxygen toxicity, lung injury, and pressure-related injury

Serious complications are uncommon, but can include oxygen toxicity, which may cause a seizure, and lung injury, including a collapsed lung. Pressure changes can also damage the eardrum or sinuses. Your care team determines the treatment pressure and oxygen exposure based on your clinical needs, then monitors you during the session.

Tell staff right away if you have severe ear or chest pain, trouble breathing, or suddenly feel unwell during or after treatment. Breathing problems need prompt medical attention. Your personal risk depends on your health and treatment plan, so ask your clinician which symptoms to watch for and whom to contact. [Harvard Health’s review of HBOT](https://www.health.harvard.edu/healthy-aging-and-longevity/hyperbaric-oxygen-therapy-evidence-based-uses-and-unproven-claims) discusses uncommon risks such as lung injury and eardrum damage.

### How providers screen for untreated pneumothorax and other health conditions

An untreated pneumothorax, or collapsed lung, is a major safety concern because pressure changes may worsen it. Before treatment, your clinician will review your medical history and assess whether HBOT is suitable. Share any history of lung disease, chest surgery, trouble equalizing ear pressure, or recent illness. Mention other health conditions, too, even if they seem unrelated.

Depending on your symptoms and history, the care team may recommend an examination or additional testing before deciding whether to proceed. The assessment also helps confirm that HBOT is appropriate for your diagnosis and that your health is stable enough for treatment. [UVA Health’s overview of HBOT evaluation](https://www.uvahealth.com/treatments/hyperbaric-oxygen-therapy) explains why clinicians assess each patient before starting therapy.

### How providers assess medications, blood sugar, and fire safety

Bring a current list of your prescription medicines, over-the-counter drugs, supplements, and topical products to your evaluation. Some medications may affect whether or how HBOT is provided, so your care team may coordinate with the clinician who prescribed them. This is especially important if you have received or are receiving cancer treatment. Don’t stop or change medication without medical advice.

If you have diabetes, ask your team whether to adjust the timing of meals or medication and whether your blood sugar should be checked before or after a session. Oxygen can also make some materials ignite more easily, so staff will explain what you can wear or bring into the chamber. You may need to avoid certain lotions, oils, or hair products. Follow the facility’s instructions and ask if you’re unsure whether a product is safe. [Tufts Medicine’s HBOT guidance](https://www.tuftsmedicine.org/services-treatments/wound-healing/hyperbaric-oxygen-therapy) highlights medication review, while the Cleveland Clinic outlines safety considerations.

## What Happens During a Hyperbaric Oxygen Therapy Session?

### How providers screen and prepare you and select a chamber

Before treatment, your care team will review your diagnosis, medical history, medications, and treatment goals to decide whether hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) is appropriate. Depending on your health and the condition being treated, screening may include a physical exam, blood tests, imaging, heart or lung checks, or oxygen measurements. Tell your provider about all medications and supplements you take, and don’t change a medication unless your care team advises you to. [Tufts Medicine outlines tests providers may consider](https://www.tuftsmedicine.org/services-treatments/wound-healing/hyperbaric-oxygen-therapy).

The team will select a chamber based on your treatment plan and health needs. Ask why that type of chamber is recommended, who will supervise your sessions, and what preparation is needed. Your provider can also explain what to wear and which personal items must stay outside the treatment area.

### How staff manage pressurization, ear equalization, oxygen delivery, and monitoring

A technician gradually increases the chamber pressure, a process called compression. As the pressure changes, your ears may feel full or pop, similar to the sensation during air travel. Staff can show you ways to equalize pressure, such as swallowing or yawning. Let them know if you have ear pain or trouble clearing your ears, rather than trying to push through the discomfort. [Tufts Medicine describes what patients may feel during compression](https://www.tuftsmedicine.org/services-treatments/wound-healing/hyperbaric-oxygen-therapy).

During treatment, you breathe concentrated oxygen in a pressurized environment. How oxygen is delivered depends on the chamber and your care plan. Staff monitor you throughout the session, and you can communicate with them if you feel anxious, uncomfortable, or unwell. [UVA Health explains how pressure and oxygen work during HBOT](https://www.uvahealth.com/treatments/hyperbaric-oxygen-therapy).

### How long sessions last, how often treatments occur, and how providers track progress

A session often lasts about 90 minutes to two hours, but the length and frequency depend on the condition being treated. Some treatment plans involve sessions once a day, several days a week, over the course of multiple weeks. The total number of sessions varies, so ask your clinician to explain the recommended schedule and how it relates to your goals. [University Hospitals describes common HBOT schedules](https://www.uhhospitals.org/blog/articles/2022/09/the-healing-power-of-hyperbaric-oxygen-therapy).

Your care team will track changes relevant to your condition. For a wound, they may measure its size and assess how it looks. For an injury or mobility concern, they may review symptoms and function. Ask when your progress will be evaluated and what findings might lead the team to adjust or end treatment. Your plan should reflect both your response to HBOT and your overall care.

### What to ask about provider qualifications, costs, and insurance coverage

Before you begin, ask who will prescribe and supervise treatment, what training the clinical team has, and how the facility monitors patients and responds to complications. Discuss your personal health risks, including possible ear or sinus pain and temporary vision changes. [Harvard Health reviews potential HBOT side effects and the evidence behind its uses](https://www.health.harvard.edu/healthy-aging-and-longevity/hyperbaric-oxygen-therapy-evidence-based-uses-and-unproven-claims).

Request a written estimate for the full treatment course, including follow-up visits and any additional fees. Insurance coverage depends on your plan and diagnosis. Medicare and some other insurers may cover HBOT for certain conditions, but coverage is not automatic. Contact your insurer and the treatment center to confirm whether your care is covered, what documentation is required, and what you may owe.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can hyperbaric oxygen therapy help with chronic pain or joint problems?**\
HBOT is not a general treatment for chronic pain, arthritis, or reduced mobility. It may be considered when a specific condition, such as certain wounds or radiation-related tissue damage, meets clinical criteria. Ask your clinician whether your diagnosis is one for which HBOT is supported and how it would fit with your current care.

**Is HBOT a proven way to improve energy or slow aging?**\
No. Current evidence does not establish HBOT as a reliable way to increase everyday energy or reverse age-related changes. If a provider recommends it for a wellness goal, ask what evidence supports that use, what outcome they plan to measure, and what risks or costs are involved.

**How can I tell whether a treatment center offers medical HBOT?**\
Ask whether a clinician has evaluated your diagnosis and prescribed the treatment, what oxygen and pressure levels the protocol uses, and who supervises sessions. Also ask how staff monitor patients and handle side effects. A chamber marketed for wellness should not be assumed to provide medically prescribed HBOT.

**What should I tell my provider before treatment?**\
Share your full medical history, including lung or ear problems, past chest surgery, medications, supplements, and any concerns about enclosed spaces. If you have diabetes, ask whether you need to adjust meal or medication timing and check your blood sugar around sessions. Follow the facility’s instructions about clothing and personal care products.

**Will insurance cover hyperbaric oxygen therapy?**\
Coverage depends on your diagnosis, insurer, and plan requirements. Before starting, contact your insurer and the treatment center to confirm whether the proposed treatment is covered, what documentation is needed, and what you may pay out of pocket. Ask for a written estimate for the full course of care.
