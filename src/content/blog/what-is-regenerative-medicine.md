What is regenerative medicine? It is a broad term for approaches intended to replace, repair, or support the function of cells, tissues, or organs affected by injury or disease. It describes a field, not one procedure or a guaranteed result. For adults who want to preserve strength, mobility, and independence, the useful starting point is to learn what a specific treatment involves and what is known about its proposed use.

## What does regenerative medicine mean?

Regenerative medicine is an umbrella term for methods intended to replace or regenerate human cells, tissues, or organs to restore or establish function. The [UK Health Research Authority overview](https://www.hra.nhs.uk/planning-and-improving-research/policies-standards-legislation/regenerative-medicine/) uses this broad definition. It covers a range of research and clinical approaches; the phrase alone does not tell a patient which method is being offered, how strong the evidence is, or whether it fits a particular health concern.

That distinction matters when you are considering care for movement, recovery, or persistent symptoms. A headline may use the same broad label for very different products and procedures. The materials, preparation, delivery, intended use, regulatory status, and evidence can all differ. Ask for the exact name of the proposed intervention instead of relying on a general term.

Regenerative medicine is also not synonymous with “new tissue growth.” The intended goal may be to support function or address a specific problem, but that does not mean tissue will regrow or that a condition will resolve. A theory about how a treatment might work is not the same as evidence that patients experience a meaningful benefit.

## What kinds of approaches may be called regenerative?

Several approaches may be grouped under the regenerative medicine label. They are not interchangeable, and research on one method cannot automatically be applied to another. In musculoskeletal care, patients may hear about platelet-rich plasma (PRP), biologic products, or cell-based procedures. Other regenerative medicine research involves tissue engineering and methods to replace or support damaged cells or structures.

* **PRP:** A preparation made from a person’s blood that concentrates platelets. The preparation and protocol matter, as does the condition for which it is being considered. Ask what evidence applies to that exact use.
* **Cell-based procedures:** These can involve different cell sources and processing methods. The label by itself does not establish what cells are present, that a product regenerates tissue, or that it is suitable for a particular condition.
* **Biologic products:** This term can refer to products made from or involving biological materials. Ask what the product contains, how it is handled, and what its status is for the proposed use.
* **Tissue engineering:** This area may combine cells, materials, or biological signals. Many approaches are studied in research settings, and findings do not automatically establish that a treatment is available or beneficial for an individual patient.

The scientific literature also discusses how regenerative approaches may develop over time. A review of [current therapies and future directions in regenerative medicine](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4664309/) describes the field's breadth and ongoing evolution. Research findings need to be considered in context: what population was studied, which product or procedure was used, what outcome was measured, and how long participants were followed.

## What can regenerative medicine do—and what can’t the name promise?

The aim of a specific regenerative approach may be to support repair or function. Possible goals and outcomes depend on the intervention, the condition, and the individual. The word “regenerative” does not promise that damaged tissue will regrow, that symptoms will disappear, or that an underlying disease will be cured. It should not substitute for a clear description of the treatment and its limits.

Evidence is specific. A study may examine a particular preparation, dose, delivery method, comparison group, and diagnosis. If a treatment being discussed differs from the research protocol, the findings may not transfer. Even when research is relevant, it may have limitations, such as a small study group, short follow-up, or outcomes that do not match the goals most important to you.

Ask how the proposed plan relates to evidence for people with a similar condition and health history. Find out whether the expected outcome is a change in a symptom, a functional measure, or something else. Ask how progress would be assessed, what would count as a meaningful change for you, and what the clinician would recommend if the plan does not meet that goal.

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration cautions consumers that regenerative medicine therapies have not been approved for orthopedic conditions such as osteoarthritis, tendonitis, or disc disease. Its [patient information on regenerative medicine therapies](https://www.fda.gov/vaccines-blood-biologics/consumers-biologics/important-patient-and-consumer-information-about-regenerative-medicine-therapies) is a resource for questions about regulatory status and proposed use. Availability or marketing does not establish FDA approval for a particular condition. Do not assume all products described with the same broad term have identical status.

## Why does evaluation come before choosing a treatment?

An evaluation connects a broad health concern to a specific person. Symptoms may have more than one possible contributor, and a treatment label cannot establish the underlying cause. A clinician may ask when the issue began, whether it has changed, what activities it affects, what has already been tried, and what other health conditions or medications may be relevant.

For example, a person may be interested in a procedure after noticing discomfort during walking. The useful discussion is not simply whether a regenerative treatment is “for pain.” It is whether the concern has been assessed, what the functional goal is, what other factors affect the plan, and whether the evidence relates to that situation. A different person with the same symptom may have different priorities and medical considerations.

Bring a practical account of your day-to-day function. Note whether the concern affects sleep, household tasks, work, stairs, driving, or an activity you want to continue. Describe how often symptoms occur, what makes them better or worse, and what you have already tried. If you have relevant imaging or medical records, ask whether they would help the conversation.

Share your medications and supplements, allergies, relevant diagnoses, and prior procedures. Do not stop or change prescribed care based on a web article. An assessment may lead to more discussion, further evaluation, a different kind of care, or no procedure. For joint or pain concerns, non-surgical options may be considered alongside or instead of a regenerative approach depending on the findings. ## What questions should you ask about evidence and safety?

A careful consultation should leave room for questions and answers in plain language. Consider bringing this list and writing down the responses:

* What is the exact name of the product or procedure, and what materials does it involve?
* What condition or functional goal is it intended to address in my case?
* What evidence supports this use, and how closely does that evidence match my situation?
* What is uncertain about the expected benefit, and what risks or side effects should I understand?
* What alternatives are reasonable, including non-procedural care, another approach, or monitoring?
* What preparation, procedure steps, and follow-up would be involved?
* How will we review progress, and when might the plan be reconsidered?
* What regulatory status applies to this specific product and proposed use?
* What charges should I expect, what do they include, and what are the payment terms?

Listen for answers that distinguish established information from uncertainty. You should be able to understand what a treatment is intended to do without being told that a result is certain. Be cautious if a claim suggests one procedure works for many unrelated conditions, guarantees an outcome, or replaces a careful evaluation. Ask who is responsible for each part of care and whether the proposed treatment is part of a clinical study or clinical care.

It is reasonable to take time before deciding. If important questions remain, ask for clarification or a written explanation. A second conversation can help you compare the proposal with your priorities and other available options. You should not feel pressured to agree to a procedure before you understand its purpose, risks, uncertainties, and costs.

## How can you compare regenerative care with other options?

Compare the actual plans rather than comparing labels. A consultation can help you understand the differences among a proposed regenerative approach, other non-surgical care, and monitoring. The prompts below are not a recommendation for a specific treatment; they can help structure a discussion around function and decision-making.

| Comparison point  | What to clarify                                                                   |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Purpose           | What symptom, function, or clinical finding is the plan intended to address?      |
| Evidence          | What research supports this use, and what limitations or uncertainties apply?     |
| Procedure details | What product or method is involved, and what happens before, during, and after?   |
| Risks             | What risks are known, and what uncertainties matter for my health history?        |
| Alternatives      | Could non-surgical care, another approach, or continued monitoring be reasonable? |
| Follow-up         | How will we check whether the plan is meeting my functional goals?                |
| Practical details | What preparation, time, follow-up, and payment arrangements should I expect?      |

If PRP is being discussed, ask specifically how it is prepared and what evidence applies to your condition and goal. Regenerative and integrative services may be offered on a cash-pay basis, and coverage depends on the service and plan. Sigma Core Medical Center is a cash-pay practice. Do not assume insurance will cover a procedure because it is offered in a medical setting or described as regenerative care. Ask for the anticipated charges before making a decision, including what is included, whether follow-up is part of the plan, and what payment or cancellation terms apply.

When comparing options, ask whether the discussion includes separate charges for evaluation, procedure, supplies, or follow-up. Do not rely on a general price found online to estimate your own costs. The exact plan and financial terms should be explained directly by the practice. Cost is one factor to consider alongside the intervention’s purpose, evidence, risks, and alternatives.

## How can you prepare for a consultation?

Preparation can make a high-stakes conversation clearer. Before the visit, write down your top priorities and a few questions. Be concrete: perhaps you want to remain comfortable while preparing meals, keep up with household tasks, or continue a hobby. Describe what is difficult today, what you have changed because of it, and what improvement would matter to you. This gives the clinician a functional context rather than a single symptom label.

Gather relevant records if available, such as prior evaluations, imaging reports, and a current medication list. Note allergies, supplements, previous procedures, and any treatment that did or did not help. You do not need to arrive with a conclusion about which procedure you want. The purpose is to understand the concern and options, not to confirm a decision you have already made.

During the conversation, ask the clinician to explain unfamiliar terms. You might say, “Can you describe what the procedure involves in everyday language?” or “Which part of the evidence applies to my situation?” If the answer is unclear, ask again. Consider taking notes or bringing a trusted support person who can listen and help you remember the discussion.

Afterward, review what you heard. Check that you understand the proposed goal, the uncertainty, the alternatives, and the practical and financial details. If you need more time, say so. A well-informed choice can include deciding not to proceed, requesting more information, or discussing another option. The appropriate next step depends on your health and goals, not on a broad category name.

## How do other care options fit into the picture?

Regenerative approaches are only one part of a broader care discussion. Depending on the health concern and evaluation, a clinician may discuss other non-surgical care or recommend additional assessment. The useful comparison is not “new” versus “old”; it is which approach is relevant to the concern, what evidence and risks apply, and how each option aligns with a person’s functional goals.

For example, someone with persistent discomfort may ask whether activity changes, rehabilitation-focused care, or a procedure should be considered, and how each would be monitored. Another person may need further evaluation before any treatment decision. There is no single sequence that fits every person. Ask why a particular option is being considered and what would lead the clinician to recommend a different direction.

It may also be useful to clarify what is outside the scope of a proposed treatment. A procedure intended to address one problem should not be assumed to address unrelated symptoms or overall health. If you have several concerns, list them separately and ask which need evaluation first. ## Frequently asked questions

### Is regenerative medicine one specific treatment?

No. It is a broad term for different methods, products, and research areas. Ask what exact intervention is proposed, what it is intended to address, and what evidence relates to that use.

### Does regenerative medicine mean new tissue will grow?

No. The term does not guarantee tissue regrowth or a particular outcome. The intended goal and evidence depend on the specific treatment, condition, and individual circumstances.

### Are PRP and cell-based procedures the same?

No. PRP is prepared from blood to concentrate platelets, while cell-based procedures can involve different materials and processing. The labels are not interchangeable; ask for details about the procedure being discussed.

### Is a regenerative treatment automatically FDA-approved?

No. Regulatory status depends on the specific product and proposed use. Ask about that status directly and review FDA patient information when evaluating claims.

### What is a sensible next step if I am considering this type of care?

Start with an evaluation that reviews your health history, goals, and options. Ask about evidence, risks, alternatives, regulatory status, follow-up, and costs before deciding.
