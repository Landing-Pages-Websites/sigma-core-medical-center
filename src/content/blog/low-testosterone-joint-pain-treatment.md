A sore knee or stiff shoulder can have many causes, especially after years of work, sports, or daily wear and tear. Low testosterone may affect muscle and bone health, but it isn’t a proven explanation for every ache, and testosterone therapy isn’t a general joint pain remedy. If pain comes with ongoing fatigue, reduced libido, or declining strength, it may be worth asking a clinician whether hormone testing is appropriate. The right approach to low testosterone and joint pain treatment begins with identifying what’s behind each symptom. This guide explains what the evidence can and can’t tell you, how clinicians evaluate both concerns, and which questions to ask about your care.

## Key Takeaways

* **Confirm low testosterone with a clinician**: Symptoms can have many causes, so diagnosis generally requires relevant symptoms and at least two low morning blood tests.
* **Assess joint pain separately**: Low testosterone may affect muscle and bone health, but it is not established as a direct cause of joint pain or a reliable treatment for it.
* **Build a care plan around your needs**: Ask about the cause of your joint symptoms, whether TRT is appropriate, and how benefits, risks, and follow-up will be assessed.

## What Is Low Testosterone?

Low testosterone, also called testosterone deficiency or hypogonadism, means testosterone levels are consistently below the healthy range and are linked with symptoms. It is more than a single low reading: levels can change, and symptoms may have other causes. A clinician assesses both before recommending treatment.

### How testosterone affects muscle, bone, energy, and sexual health

Testosterone helps maintain muscle mass and strength, supports bone health, and plays an important role in sexual desire and erectile function. It also contributes to sperm production. When levels are low, some men experience reduced libido, fewer spontaneous erections, or changes in muscle strength and energy. These effects vary from person to person, and fatigue or weakness alone does not point to a hormone problem. Testosterone is one factor in bone and muscle health, alongside age, physical activity, nutrition, medications, and other medical conditions. The [Prostate Cancer Foundation’s overview of testosterone-related effects](https://www.pcf.org/patient-support/side-effects/hormone-therapy) describes several ways changes in testosterone can affect the body.

### How low testosterone differs from age-related changes

Testosterone levels may decrease gradually as men age, but this change does not mean everyone develops testosterone deficiency or needs treatment. Clinicians look for both relevant symptoms and consistently low blood levels. Changes such as lower energy, reduced strength, or slower recovery can also relate to poor sleep, chronic health conditions, medications, or changes in activity. Sexual symptoms can have multiple causes, too. Rather than attributing every change to aging or testosterone, a clinician can review your health history and consider other explanations. If treatment is being considered, potential benefits and risks should be discussed for your individual circumstances, as outlined in the Cleveland Clinic’s guide to testosterone replacement therapy.

### Why symptoms or one test can’t confirm a diagnosis

Symptoms such as fatigue, low mood, reduced strength, or joint pain can occur for many reasons, so they cannot confirm low testosterone on their own. A single blood test is not enough either: testosterone levels vary throughout the day and may be affected by illness and other factors. Diagnosis generally involves symptoms plus at least two low morning blood test results, interpreted by a clinician. Joint pain also needs its own assessment because it may stem from arthritis, injury, or another condition. Research has found a possible association between low testosterone and pain, but it has not established that low testosterone causes joint pain. [Evidence reviewed by Hims](https://www.hims.com/blog/testosterone-and-joint-pain) highlights this distinction.

## What Are Common Signs of Low Testosterone?

Low testosterone can affect sexual health, energy, mood, muscle, and bone health. Changes may develop gradually, so it can be tempting to chalk them up to aging, stress, or a busy schedule. But these symptoms aren’t specific to testosterone, and some people with low levels notice few changes. Symptoms can raise a question, but they can’t confirm the cause on their own.

Look for persistent changes from your usual baseline. You might notice less interest in sex, fatigue that doesn’t improve with rest, or reduced strength during activities you normally manage comfortably. Consider when the changes started, whether they’re getting worse, and how they affect sleep, exercise, and everyday tasks. That information can help a clinician assess what may be contributing.

It’s also worth noting that several symptoms can occur together, while others may be absent. A clinician will consider your symptoms alongside your medical history, medications, and appropriate test results. If you’re experiencing ongoing changes, particularly ones that affect your quality of life, arrange an evaluation rather than assuming they’re an inevitable part of getting older or trying testosterone products without medical guidance.

### Reduced libido or fewer spontaneous erections

A persistent drop in sexual desire can be associated with low testosterone. Some men may also notice fewer spontaneous erections, including erections during sleep or on waking. Testosterone contributes to sexual desire and male sexual function, but erection changes can also be related to circulation, diabetes, medication effects, stress, or other health concerns.

The [Prostate Cancer Foundation’s overview of hormone therapy effects](https://www.pcf.org/patient-support/side-effects/hormone-therapy) describes how reduced testosterone can affect sexual desire and erectile function. Its information focuses on hormone therapy, so it should not be taken to mean that changes in sexual function automatically indicate low testosterone. If you’ve noticed a lasting change, tell your clinician when it began and mention any medications or other symptoms. They can help assess possible causes and decide whether testing is appropriate.

### Fatigue, low mood, or trouble concentrating

Ongoing fatigue, lower mood, or difficulty concentrating may occur with low testosterone, but each can have many causes. Sleep problems, emotional stress, anemia, thyroid conditions, medication effects, and other health concerns can affect energy and focus. Consider whether the change is persistent and whether it’s making exercise, work, or routine tasks more difficult.

The [Prostate Cancer Foundation’s guide to hormone therapy side effects](https://www.pcf.org/patient-support/side-effects/hormone-therapy) lists fatigue and mood changes among possible effects of testosterone loss. These symptoms alone don’t identify the cause. A clinician can review your health history, sleep, medications, and other symptoms before recommending tests or treatment. If you feel unusually tired despite getting enough rest, or your mood or concentration has changed for a sustained period, bring it up at an appointment instead of assuming testosterone is responsible.

### Loss of muscle, strength, or exercise capacity

A gradual decline in muscle or strength may make familiar activities feel harder. You might find that carrying groceries, climbing stairs, or recovering after exercise takes more effort than before. Testosterone supports normal processes related to muscle, but strength can also change with reduced activity, nutrition, illness, pain, and age-related muscle loss.

If you notice a lasting change, keep track of what feels different and when it began. Note whether joint pain has limited your activity, or whether weakness affects one area more than others. This can help your clinician consider different explanations. The [Prostate Cancer Foundation’s information on testosterone loss](https://www.pcf.org/patient-support/side-effects/hormone-therapy) includes decreased muscle mass among potential effects. However, a loss of strength or muscle by itself does not confirm low testosterone, and a clinician can help determine whether further assessment is needed.

### Changes in body composition or bone density

Some people with low testosterone experience a decrease in muscle, an increase in body fat, or changes in weight distribution. Testosterone also plays a role in bone health, and persistently low levels may be associated with reduced bone density. However, body composition and bone health can also be influenced by activity, nutrition, medications, other conditions, and aging.

Bone loss often has no noticeable symptoms until a fracture occurs. Changes in weight or appearance can’t show whether your bone density has declined, so a clinician may assess your risk and decide whether bone-density testing is appropriate. The Mayo Clinic’s overview of testosterone therapy in older adults explains why potential benefits and risks need careful consideration. If you’re concerned about changes in body composition or bone health, ask about an evaluation rather than starting testosterone or supplements on your own.

### Why other conditions can cause these symptoms

Low libido, fatigue, reduced strength, and mood changes can overlap with symptoms of other health conditions. Medication side effects, obstructive sleep apnea, thyroid problems, diabetes, and depression are among the possible causes. Joint pain may also limit activity, which can contribute to changes in strength, energy, or body composition over time.

That overlap is why clinicians consider your overall health rather than relying on one symptom. The Mayo Clinic’s discussion of testosterone therapy notes that several conditions can cause symptoms similar to those associated with low testosterone. At an appointment, share when your symptoms started, how they affect daily life, and any medications or supplements you take. This context helps your clinician decide whether to investigate testosterone levels, another possible cause, or both.

## Can Low Testosterone Contribute to Joint Pain?

Low testosterone may play a role in muscle strength, bone health, and how the body responds to inflammation. Those factors can affect movement and comfort, but joint pain alone does not show that testosterone is low. Research has found possible links between hormone levels and pain, yet it has not established that low testosterone directly causes arthritis or that testosterone treatment reliably relieves joint pain.

If you have joint pain alongside symptoms such as reduced libido, ongoing fatigue, or loss of strength, tell your clinician about both. They can assess possible hormone-related symptoms while also investigating the joint itself. The [research on testosterone and joint pain](https://www.hims.com/blog/testosterone-and-joint-pain) offers useful context, but it should not replace an individual evaluation.

### How reduced strength and bone health can affect joint function

Testosterone helps maintain muscle and bone in men. When levels are low, some men lose muscle mass or strength, which can make tasks such as climbing stairs or standing from a chair more difficult. Muscles support and stabilize joints during movement, so changes in strength may affect how comfortably you move. This is one possible indirect connection between low testosterone and joint function.

Low testosterone may also be associated with lower bone density. That does not mean reduced bone density automatically causes joint pain, but bone health can influence mobility and overall physical function. Age, activity, nutrition, medications, and other health conditions also affect muscles and bones. A [discussion of testosterone and joint health](https://spineina.com/blog/joint-pain-and-testosterone-the-link-you-didnt-know-about) describes bone health as one possible factor. Your clinician can help decide whether you need an assessment of muscle strength or bone density.

### What research says about testosterone, arthritis, inflammation, and pain

Testosterone may influence inflammation, which plays a part in many conditions that affect joints. Researchers have examined whether low testosterone is associated with inflammation, cartilage changes, arthritis, or greater pain. Some studies report connections, but a connection does not prove that low testosterone causes arthritis or that increasing testosterone will prevent joint damage.

Joint pain can stem from several different conditions, including osteoarthritis, inflammatory arthritis, and injuries. Each can have its own causes and treatment needs. The [available research on low testosterone and arthritis](https://www.hims.com/blog/testosterone-and-joint-pain) is largely observational, so the findings are not proof of cause and effect. If pain or stiffness persists, a clinician should assess the joint directly, even if you also have symptoms that could point to low testosterone.

### What observational studies can and can’t show

Observational studies look for patterns among groups of people. For example, researchers may compare pain reports among men with different testosterone levels. These findings can help identify possible links, but they cannot show by themselves that low testosterone caused someone’s pain. Age, weight, activity, sleep, and chronic health conditions may influence both hormone levels and joint symptoms.

This distinction is important when considering treatment. If men with lower testosterone report more pain, that does not establish that testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) will ease the pain. Researchers need stronger studies to account for other factors and determine whether treatment changes joint symptoms. Because the evidence remains limited, clinicians assess testosterone alongside symptoms and blood tests rather than using joint pain alone to diagnose a deficiency.

### Why evidence for TRT relieving joint pain remains limited

Some reports describe reduced joint pain in men receiving TRT. One [report on a study of men treated with TRT](https://gamedaymenshealth.com/blog/joint-pain-vs-low-t) notes that fewer participants reported joint pain after several months. That finding may be worth further study, but improvement over time does not prove that TRT caused it.

Pain can change naturally, and participants may also receive other care or make lifestyle changes during a study. Results from one study may not apply to someone whose pain comes from arthritis, an injury, or another condition. TRT is generally considered for men with confirmed testosterone deficiency and related symptoms, not as a standard treatment for joint pain. A clinician can explain what benefits and limitations may apply to your health history and diagnosis.

### Other common causes of joint pain after 50

Joint pain after 50 can have many causes besides low testosterone. Osteoarthritis, past injuries, tendon or bursa problems, inflammatory arthritis, and repetitive strain can all lead to pain or stiffness. Sometimes pain felt near a joint comes from a muscle, nerve, or the spine. Where the pain occurs, how long it has lasted, and what makes it better or worse can help guide an evaluation.

A clinician may ask about swelling, warmth, stiffness, injuries, and how pain affects daily activities. They may recommend an examination or tests based on your symptoms. If joint pain occurs alongside reduced libido, persistent fatigue, or loss of strength, mention those symptoms too. They may support checking testosterone, but they cannot confirm a hormone deficiency on their own. Assessing both the joint and your broader health helps identify the right next steps.

## How Do Clinicians Evaluate Low Testosterone and Joint Pain?

Low testosterone and joint pain can occur at the same time, but one does not necessarily cause the other. A clinician will assess hormone-related symptoms and joint pain separately, then consider whether they may share an underlying cause. This helps ensure that care addresses the reason for your symptoms rather than focusing on a single test result.

### Review symptoms, medical history, medications, and daily function

Your clinician may ask when your symptoms began, whether they have changed, and how they affect everyday activities such as walking, sleeping, exercising, or getting dressed. Mention concerns such as reduced sexual desire, ongoing fatigue, changes in strength, or pain that limits movement. A clear picture of your symptoms helps guide which evaluations may be appropriate.

You may also be asked about past injuries or surgeries, chronic health conditions, sleep, and any medications or supplements you take. Some health conditions and treatments can affect energy, sexual function, hormone levels, or pain. Bring a current medication list and describe what makes your joint symptoms better or worse. This context helps your clinician tailor the evaluation to your health and daily needs.

### Confirm low testosterone with repeat morning blood tests

Symptoms alone can’t confirm low testosterone. Your clinician will usually measure testosterone with a blood test, often in the morning when levels tend to be higher. Since levels can vary, a low result is typically confirmed with a second morning test on a separate day before a diagnosis or treatment decision is made.

Your clinician may also consider whether illness, sleep, or the timing of the test could affect the result. If repeat tests show low levels and your symptoms are consistent, additional tests may help clarify the cause. The [Endocrine Society’s clinical guideline](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/testosterone-therapy) recommends confirming low testosterone with repeat morning measurements. Don’t rely on one test or a symptom checklist to decide whether treatment is right for you.

### Check for underlying causes and related health conditions

When tests confirm low testosterone, your clinician may look for a reason. Low levels can be associated with chronic illness, certain medications, past surgery or injury, or changes in how the body produces hormones. Age may be relevant, but it doesn’t explain every case. Depending on your symptoms and medical history, your clinician may order additional blood tests to assess hormone signals and related health factors.

This step matters because an underlying condition may also contribute to fatigue, reduced strength, or slower recovery. Your clinician may ask about sleep, weight changes, and other health concerns, as well as review medications that can affect hormone levels. Identifying these factors can help shape a care plan that addresses more than the lab result alone. The Mayo Clinic’s overview of testosterone therapy explains why clinicians consider potential causes and health risks before recommending treatment.

### Assess joint pain separately with an exam and appropriate tests

Joint pain deserves its own evaluation, even if testosterone levels are low. Your clinician may ask where the pain occurs, when it began, and whether you have stiffness, swelling, warmth, or reduced movement. They may examine the affected joint and assess your movement, strength, or gait for signs of arthritis, injury, or another condition.

Depending on what the exam shows, imaging or blood tests may help investigate a possible cause. These tests aren’t necessary for everyone. Low testosterone can be relevant to overall muscle and bone health, but it doesn’t establish why a particular joint hurts. A [joint pain evaluation](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/joint-pain/) can help direct care toward the cause identified, rather than assuming testosterone treatment will relieve the pain.

## What Are the Treatment Options for Low Testosterone and Joint Pain?

Treatment depends on what is causing your joint pain and whether repeat testing confirms low testosterone. These concerns can occur together, but treating one may not resolve the other. A clinician can help you weigh options based on your symptoms, health history, activity goals, and test results.

### Treat the diagnosed cause of joint pain alongside hormone symptoms

Joint pain may come from osteoarthritis, an injury, an inflammatory condition, nerve problems, or another cause. A clinician can assess your symptoms and recommend care for the specific problem, such as physical therapy, changes to certain activities, medication, or other joint-focused treatment. At the same time, fatigue, reduced libido, or loss of strength may warrant a separate evaluation for low testosterone.

Research has explored links between testosterone, inflammation, and cartilage health. However, an association between lower testosterone and osteoarthritis does not prove that low testosterone caused your pain. The [Hims overview of testosterone and joint pain](https://www.hims.com/blog/testosterone-and-joint-pain) discusses this potential connection, but it does not establish TRT as a treatment for joint damage. Addressing joint symptoms and hormone concerns separately helps ensure each gets the right assessment.

### Determine when testosterone replacement therapy may be appropriate

Testosterone replacement therapy (TRT) may be an option when you have symptoms of testosterone deficiency and repeat blood tests confirm consistently low levels. Symptoms such as fatigue, reduced strength, or low mood can have many causes, so they do not confirm low testosterone on their own. Testing is generally done in the morning, when testosterone levels tend to be higher.

If results confirm low testosterone, your clinician can review possible causes, overall health, and whether treatment’s potential benefits outweigh its risks. TRT is meant to treat diagnosed testosterone deficiency, not joint pain by itself. The [Endocrine Society’s clinical guidance](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/testosterone-therapy) outlines the need to confirm the diagnosis and consider a person’s health and treatment goals before starting therapy.

### Weigh TRT options, potential benefits, and limits for joint pain

TRT is available in several forms, including gels, injections, and patches. They differ in how often they are used, how they are applied, and what precautions are needed. For example, testosterone gel can transfer through skin contact, so users need to follow instructions about application and covering the treated area. Ask your clinician which option fits your preferences and health needs.

Some men report less joint discomfort after starting TRT, but current evidence does not show that it reliably treats joint pain. [Gameday Men’s Health describes reported changes in joint pain during TRT](https://gamedaymenshealth.com/blog/joint-pain-vs-low-t), but findings like these cannot predict your response or prove that testosterone caused the change. TRT should not replace evaluation or treatment for arthritis, injury, or another joint condition.

### Consider TRT’s side effects, health risks, and reasons to avoid it

Possible side effects of TRT include acne, fluid retention, breast tenderness or swelling, and worsening sleep apnea. Treatment can also raise red blood cell levels, which is one reason clinicians may check blood counts during follow-up. Risks vary from person to person, so discuss your medical history, medications, and existing health conditions before making a decision.

TRT may not be appropriate for some people or may require extra caution. Ask how it could affect your prostate health and any conditions you already manage, and find out which symptoms should prompt a call to your clinician. [Harvard Health’s review of testosterone therapy safety](https://www.health.harvard.edu/mens-health/is-testosterone-therapy-safe-take-a-breath-before-you-take-the-plunge) outlines potential side effects and the need for individualized medical advice. Don’t start, stop, or adjust hormone treatment without guidance from a qualified clinician.

### Discuss fertility, goals, and follow-up before starting treatment

If you may want to have children, discuss fertility before starting TRT. Testosterone therapy can reduce sperm production, which may make it harder to conceive. Your clinician can explain how treatment could affect fertility and discuss other options if preserving sperm production is important to you.

It also helps to agree on clear goals, such as improving libido, energy, or strength, and decide how you’ll assess progress. Follow-up may include reviewing symptoms, testosterone levels, blood counts, side effects, and whether treatment remains appropriate. TRT can raise red blood cell levels, so ask how often your clinician plans to monitor your blood count. The Voy overview of TRT side effects also discusses this issue. A follow-up plan helps you and your clinician decide whether to continue, adjust, or stop treatment.

## How Do Clinicians Monitor Testosterone Therapy?

Monitoring helps your clinician assess whether testosterone therapy is addressing the symptoms it was prescribed for and remains a good fit for your health and goals. Follow-up typically includes a discussion of symptoms and treatment effects, along with blood tests and other checks when appropriate. Your schedule depends on your treatment, medical history, and individual risk factors.

### Track symptoms and testosterone levels

At follow-up visits, describe changes in energy, sexual health, strength, mood, and daily function. If joint pain is one of your concerns, mention whether it has changed, too. Improvement alone doesn’t show that testosterone was the cause: joint pain has many possible explanations, and evidence that testosterone therapy directly relieves it remains limited. Your clinician can consider your symptoms alongside your [testosterone test results](https://gamedaymenshealth.com/blog/joint-pain-vs-low-t).

Blood tests help determine whether treatment is bringing testosterone into an appropriate range. The timing of each test depends on the medication or formulation you use, so follow your clinician’s instructions. Don’t change your dose based on symptoms or a single test result without discussing it with your care team.

### Monitor blood counts and prostate health

Testosterone therapy can increase red blood cell counts. Your clinician may check a complete blood count, including hematocrit, before treatment and at follow-up. If the count becomes too high, they may reassess your dose or treatment plan. The right response depends on your results and overall health.

Prostate monitoring is another topic to discuss, with checks based on your age, symptoms, medical history, and personal risk. Your clinician may ask about urinary changes and consider prostate-specific antigen testing when appropriate. Share any history of prostate concerns, significant urinary symptoms, or heart disease before starting treatment. [Harvard Health’s review of testosterone therapy safety](https://www.health.harvard.edu/mens-health/is-testosterone-therapy-safe-take-a-breath-before-you-take-the-plunge) explains why care decisions should account for individual risks.

### Review side effects, response, and ongoing need

Tell your clinician about new or worsening symptoms, even if you’re unsure they relate to treatment. Report persistent swelling, shortness of breath, marked mood changes, or significant changes in urination promptly. Your provider can help determine whether these symptoms need urgent attention or a change in care. Keeping notes between appointments can help you describe patterns and bring clear questions to each visit.

At follow-ups, you and your clinician can review how you feel, side effects, test results, and whether therapy continues to support your goals. The plan may need adjustment, or treatment may no longer be appropriate. Regular, open conversations help guide those decisions; the [testosterone side effects guide](https://gamedaymenshealth.com/blog/side-effects-of-testosterone) also highlights the value of reporting symptoms.

## How Can You Treat Joint Pain Beyond TRT?

TRT is not a general treatment for joint pain, and it may not be right for everyone with low testosterone. A clinician can help identify what is causing your pain and build a plan around your health, symptoms, and daily activities. Depending on the cause, care may involve physical therapy, medication, or treatment for a specific joint condition.

### Tailor physical therapy and strength or mobility work to your ability

A physical therapist can adapt a plan to your current mobility, pain level, and goals. That may include gentle range-of-motion exercises, balance work, or strength training to help with everyday activities such as walking, getting up from a chair, or climbing stairs.

Begin at a level you can manage and increase activity gradually with professional guidance. Tell your therapist which movements hurt and whether symptoms change during or after exercise. Sharp or worsening pain is a reason to pause and ask for advice, rather than push through. If reduced muscle strength is affecting how you move, strengthening may support joint function, but it does not diagnose or treat the cause of the pain on its own.

### Consider pain medicines and other clinician-guided options

The right approach to pain relief depends on the affected joint, your medical history, and the medicines you already take. A clinician may discuss topical or oral anti-inflammatory medicines, other pain relievers, or an injection for a specific condition. These options have different risks. Some medicines, for example, may not be suitable for people with kidney, stomach, heart, or blood-pressure concerns. Check with a clinician or pharmacist before starting a new medication.

Heat or cold, activity adjustments, and a brace may also help in some situations. Ask what an option is intended to address, how long to try it, and when to follow up. Evidence that TRT relieves joint pain is limited, so review what is known about [TRT and joint pain](https://gamedaymenshealth.com/blog/joint-pain-vs-low-t) with your clinician before considering it for that purpose.

### Treat arthritis, injury, or another diagnosed joint condition

Joint pain may be related to osteoarthritis, an inflammatory condition, an injury, or problems with nearby muscles and tendons. Treatment should fit the cause. Depending on your assessment, a clinician may recommend targeted exercise, medication, changes to certain activities, or referral to a specialist. Imaging or other tests may be useful when your symptoms and exam suggest they are needed.

Low testosterone and joint problems can occur at the same time, but that does not show that one caused the other. Some research has explored links between testosterone, inflammation, and cartilage, but much of it is observational. An association between low testosterone and arthritis does not prove that TRT will prevent or resolve arthritis, as [research on testosterone and joint pain](https://www.hims.com/blog/testosterone-and-joint-pain) explains. Ask your clinician what may be causing your pain and which options are appropriate for that diagnosis.

### Coordinate care for overlapping joint and hormone symptoms

If joint pain occurs alongside low libido, ongoing fatigue, or reduced strength, mention all of your symptoms during a medical visit. A primary care clinician or endocrinologist can assess whether testosterone testing is appropriate. A physical therapist, rheumatologist, or orthopedist may help evaluate the joint problem, depending on your symptoms and exam.

Before your appointment, note when the pain began, what activities affect it, what eases it, and whether it disrupts sleep or daily tasks. Bring a list of medicines and supplements, along with relevant test results. This information helps your care team consider possible connections without assuming low testosterone explains every symptom. If testing confirms low testosterone, discuss the goals and follow-up for hormone treatment separately from the plan for your joint condition.

## Can Lifestyle Changes Support Testosterone and Joint Health?

Healthy habits can support strength, mobility, sleep, and overall well-being. They may help you stay active while addressing joint pain or possible low testosterone, but they cannot diagnose or treat an underlying condition on their own. Choose changes that fit your abilities and health needs, and discuss persistent symptoms with a clinician.

### Adapt resistance and aerobic exercise to your ability

Regular movement can help preserve muscle strength, endurance, and joint function. Stronger muscles support you during everyday activities, while aerobic exercise can make tasks like walking and climbing stairs feel more manageable. Start with an activity that suits your current fitness and joint comfort, such as walking, stationary cycling, or water exercise. Add light resistance work if it’s appropriate, and increase activity gradually. Stop and seek guidance if exercise causes sharp or worsening pain. A physical therapist can adapt movements for arthritis, an injury, or mobility limits. Exercise supports overall health, but it should not be treated as a proven way to correct low testosterone. The [CDC’s physical activity guidance for older adults](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/guidelines/older-adults.html) offers a useful starting point.

### Eat well and manage your weight

A balanced eating pattern can support muscle, bone, and cardiovascular health. Include protein-rich foods, vegetables, fruit, whole grains, and healthy fats, adjusting your choices to fit any medical conditions or dietary needs. If weight loss is appropriate for you, gradual changes may reduce stress on weight-bearing joints and make movement more comfortable. Avoid restrictive diets or products that promise quick changes in testosterone. Research has found associations between lower testosterone and osteoarthritis, but an association does not prove that one causes the other. A [review of the possible link between testosterone and joint pain](https://www.hims.com/blog/testosterone-and-joint-pain) can provide background, but it cannot determine what is causing your symptoms.

### Sleep consistently and assess for sleep disorders

A regular sleep and wake schedule can support energy, mood, and recovery. If you snore loudly, wake up gasping, or feel unusually sleepy during the day, talk with a clinician about sleep apnea or another sleep disorder. These symptoms deserve attention, especially if you are considering testosterone therapy, since treatment may worsen sleep-related breathing problems for some people. [Harvard Health explains the connection between testosterone therapy and sleep apnea](https://www.health.harvard.edu/mens-health/is-testosterone-therapy-safe-take-a-breath-before-you-take-the-plunge). Share any sleep concerns with the clinician overseeing your care before starting treatment. Don’t change a prescribed therapy on your own; ask about the benefits, risks, and appropriate follow-up for your situation.

### Limit excess alcohol and avoid unproven testosterone supplements

Heavy alcohol use can affect sleep, recovery, and overall health. If you drink, consider reducing your intake and ask your clinician whether alcohol could affect your symptoms, medications, or treatment plan. Be cautious with supplements marketed as testosterone boosters. Ingredients and quality can vary, and some products may interact with prescription medicines or health conditions. “Natural” does not automatically mean safe or effective. Before taking a supplement, show your healthcare professional its name and ingredient list. Testosterone can interact with other drugs and substances, so it’s important to review everything you take with your care team. [Kaiser Permanente’s guidance on hormone therapy risks](https://mydoctor.kaiserpermanente.org/mas/structured-content/Treatment_Risks_and_Side_Effects_of_Masculinizing_Hormone_Therapy.xml?co=%2Fregions%2Fmas) also underscores the importance of discussing possible interactions.

### Pair lifestyle changes with medical care

Lifestyle changes can complement medical care, but ongoing joint pain or symptoms such as low libido, persistent fatigue, or reduced strength warrant an evaluation. A clinician can review your health history, assess possible causes, confirm low testosterone with appropriate testing, and examine the joint separately. If low testosterone is diagnosed, ask whether treatment is right for you, what benefits are realistic, and how your health will be monitored. Testosterone therapy should not be assumed to relieve joint pain, and it does not replace care for arthritis, injury, or another joint condition. The [Endocrine Society’s clinical guidance on testosterone therapy](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/testosterone-therapy) can help you prepare questions for a conversation with your clinician.

## When Should You Seek Medical Evaluation?

### Get evaluated for persistent pain or limits on daily activities

Make an appointment if joint pain lasts more than a few weeks, keeps returning, or interferes with daily activities. Trouble walking, climbing stairs, getting dressed, sleeping, or exercising is worth discussing with a clinician, even if the pain feels manageable. An evaluation can help identify causes such as arthritis, an injury, medication effects, or another health condition.

Don’t assume ongoing joint pain is caused by low testosterone. Research has explored possible links between testosterone, inflammation, and osteoarthritis, but an association does not prove that low testosterone is causing your symptoms. The research on testosterone and joint pain is still developing. A clinician can assess your joint and consider your health history before recommending next steps.

### Seek evaluation for joint pain with low libido, weakness, or ongoing fatigue

Joint pain accompanied by reduced libido, fewer spontaneous erections, persistent fatigue, or loss of strength is a good reason to speak with a healthcare professional. These symptoms can occur with low testosterone, but they may also relate to sleep problems, depression, thyroid conditions, medication effects, or other health issues. Note when the symptoms began and how they affect your daily life, so you can share those details during your appointment.

A clinician can determine whether hormone testing is appropriate and assess your joint symptoms separately. Some studies have reported changes in joint pain among men receiving testosterone therapy, but this does not establish TRT as a general pain treatment. Read [information on low testosterone and joint pain](https://gamedaymenshealth.com/blog/joint-pain-vs-low-t) as background, and discuss your specific symptoms and options with a qualified healthcare professional.

### Ask about testing, treatment options, and monitoring

If you’re concerned about low testosterone, ask how it will be confirmed and what other causes of your symptoms should be considered. Diagnosis generally requires compatible symptoms and consistently low testosterone levels, confirmed with repeat morning blood tests. A single result may not be enough to determine whether treatment is appropriate. The [Endocrine Society’s clinical guidance](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/testosterone-therapy) explains why both test results and clinical context matter.

If TRT is being considered, ask about potential benefits, risks, and whether it fits your health history and goals. Find out how your symptoms and testosterone levels will be monitored, and which follow-up tests you may need. Make sure to discuss fertility plans and any medications or health conditions. Joint pain also needs its own care plan, since hormone treatment may not address the underlying cause.

### Seek prompt care for sudden severe pain, marked swelling, fever, or inability to bear weight

Seek urgent medical care if joint pain begins suddenly and is severe, the joint becomes very swollen or hot, you develop a fever, or you can’t put weight on the affected leg. These symptoms can point to a problem, such as an infection or fracture, that needs prompt assessment. Don’t wait for a routine appointment or assume the symptoms are related to testosterone.

For less urgent symptoms, contact a clinician if pain worsens, swelling persists, or movement becomes increasingly limited. The Mayo Clinic’s guidance on joint pain can help you understand when to seek care. Tell your care team about any recent injury, new medication, or hormone treatment, as these details may help guide your evaluation.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can joint pain be a sign of low testosterone?**\
Joint pain alone does not point to low testosterone. If it occurs with changes such as reduced libido, ongoing fatigue, or loss of strength, mention all of your symptoms to a clinician. They can assess the joint and decide whether hormone testing is appropriate.

**Will testosterone therapy relieve joint pain?**\
It may not. Some men report changes in pain while receiving testosterone therapy, but current evidence does not show that it reliably treats joint pain or arthritis. Treatment should be based on a confirmed diagnosis and your individual health needs.

**How is low testosterone confirmed?**\
A clinician considers relevant symptoms alongside blood test results. Testing usually includes at least two low morning readings, since testosterone levels can vary. Your clinician may also check for other causes of your symptoms.

**What should I discuss before starting testosterone therapy?**\
Ask about potential benefits, side effects, fertility, and how treatment will be monitored. Share your medical history and all medications or supplements you take. These details help your clinician decide whether therapy fits your health and goals.

**When should joint pain be checked promptly?**\
Seek urgent medical care for sudden, severe pain, a hot or very swollen joint, fever, or difficulty bearing weight. For pain that persists or limits daily activities, arrange a medical evaluation to identify its cause.
