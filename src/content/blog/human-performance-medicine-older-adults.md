You may be exercising regularly but still feel limited by joint discomfort, fatigue, or a long recovery after activity. Or perhaps you want to become more active but are unsure which movements are safe for your health. Human performance medicine for older adults can help bring those concerns into one practical conversation. Rather than relying on a standard routine, a clinician can consider your health conditions, medications, daily function, and priorities when discussing next steps. Exercise, nutrition, recovery, and medical guidance may all play a role. This approach cannot promise to cure pain or reverse aging, but it can help you explore realistic ways to support strength, mobility, and well-being.

## Key Takeaways

* **Set goals around everyday function**: Tell your care team which activities matter to you, along with your symptoms, health conditions, and medications.
* **Build activity gradually**: Combine strength, aerobic, and balance exercises with nutrition and recovery, adjusting your routine with professional guidance.
* **Ask clear questions before treatment**: Discuss evidence, risks, alternatives, and how you’ll measure progress, particularly for hormone and regenerative therapies.

## What Is Human Performance Medicine for Older Adults?

Human performance medicine is an individualized approach to helping people maintain or improve how they move, feel, and function. For older adults, that could mean building the strength to carry groceries, improving balance on stairs, or finding ways to stay active while managing joint pain. The approach brings together physical activity, nutrition, recovery, and medical guidance to support daily function and long-term health.

It is not one specific treatment, and it cannot guarantee a particular result. A thoughtful plan considers your health history, medications, current abilities, symptoms, and personal priorities. It also changes as your needs change. A person returning to exercise after surgery may need a different starting point from someone who walks regularly but wants to improve strength.

The goal is to support healthspan, the years you live in good health and can take part in the activities that matter to you. That may involve improving strength or mobility, addressing barriers to movement, or supporting recovery. Some age-related changes and health conditions cannot be prevented or reversed, but practical, evidence-informed care can help you make progress safely and with realistic expectations.

### Combine Exercise, Nutrition, Recovery, and Evidence-Informed Care

Exercise is a central part of healthy aging, but it works best alongside other habits and appropriate medical care. Strength and aerobic activity support physical function, while balanced meals, enough fluids, sleep, and recovery help you stay consistent. Your clinician can help adapt these foundations to health concerns such as heart disease, diabetes, osteoporosis, or ongoing pain.

Public health guidance recommends that older adults work toward at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity each week, or 75 minutes of vigorous activity, and do muscle-strengthening activities on two or more days. These are general targets, not a requirement to start at full volume. The [CDC’s physical activity guidance for older adults](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/guidelines/older-adults.html) also recommends activities that support balance. If you have been inactive or have health concerns, ask a health professional how to start and increase activity safely.

### Prioritize Strength, Mobility, Independence, and Healthspan

A useful plan connects movement to everyday life. Instead of focusing only on weight or a fitness score, consider what you want to do more comfortably: rise from a chair, walk farther, carry a bag, garden, or play with grandchildren. Those goals can guide decisions about strength, mobility, balance, and endurance.

It is possible to build strength later in life, though progress and the right starting point vary from person to person. Walking, tai chi, dance, and adapted yoga can help you practice movement, balance, and coordination. Strength exercises can also be adjusted to your ability and experience. Track changes that matter to you, such as feeling steadier or completing a daily task more easily, and share them with your care team. Small, consistent steps can help you build a routine that fits your life.

### Set Realistic Goals Without Promising a Cure

Human performance care should come with clear, practical expectations. A plan may help improve strength, function, or confidence, but results vary. No approach can promise to cure chronic pain, reverse every age-related change, or prevent all illness. Before starting a treatment, ask what it is intended to do, what evidence supports it, what risks it carries, and how you and your clinician will measure progress.

Well-being includes mental health, too. Grief, stress, chronic illness, and major life changes can affect mood and daily functioning, but persistent depression or anxiety should not be dismissed as a normal part of aging. If low mood, worry, loneliness, or changes in thinking interfere with daily life, talk with a health professional. The National Institute on Aging’s guidance on mental and emotional health explains signs to watch for and ways to seek support.

## What Can Human Performance Medicine Help Address?

Human performance medicine can help assess the factors affecting how you move, feel, and recover, then guide care around your health history and goals. For adults over 50, that may include age-related muscle loss, joint discomfort, changes in balance, low energy, or difficulty staying active. A plan may combine supervised exercise with guidance on recovery, nutrition, and coordination with other clinicians.

The focus is on function in everyday life: walking with more confidence, getting up from a chair, carrying groceries, or returning to activities you enjoy. Your starting point matters. Health conditions, medications, previous injuries, sleep, and current fitness all affect which activities are appropriate and how gradually to progress.

Some symptoms need medical assessment before you change your activity. New or worsening pain, numbness, dizziness, balance problems, or unusual fatigue should be discussed with a healthcare professional. Human performance care cannot promise to cure every condition, but it can help you understand your options and work toward realistic, measurable goals.

### Address Age-Related Muscle Loss, Reduced Strength, and Slower Recovery

Muscle mass and strength can decline with age, making everyday tasks such as climbing stairs or standing from a chair more challenging. Still, meaningful gains are possible later in life. With consistent, appropriately tailored resistance training, older adults can build muscle and improve strength. A [discussion of strength training for older adults](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZDP_qHwJwPY) notes that some people may see gains within a few months, although results vary.

A clinician or qualified exercise professional can help you start at a suitable level and adjust your program as your strength changes. They can also account for recovery time by modifying the intensity, frequency, and rest between sessions. The goal is steady progress, not pushing through unusual pain or fatigue.

### Manage Joint Pain, Neuropathy, Balance Changes, and Reduced Mobility

Joint pain, numbness or tingling, and balance changes can make movement less comfortable and affect confidence. A personalized plan starts by reviewing your symptoms and determining whether they need further medical assessment. If exercise is appropriate, strength, mobility, and balance activities can be adapted to your needs. They should support care for the underlying issue, not take the place of diagnosis or treatment.

Balance practice may include supported single-leg standing, walking in a straight line, or tai chi, as described in this [exercise discussion for older adults](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZDP_qHwJwPY). If you feel unsteady or have fallen before, ask a healthcare professional how to practice safely. Using a stable support or working with a professional may be appropriate when you begin.

### Address Chronic Conditions, Low Energy, and Inactivity

Diabetes, heart disease, and other chronic conditions can affect energy and stamina, while pain, sleep problems, medication effects, and inactivity may add to the challenge. Rather than treating low energy as an inevitable part of aging, a care team can review your symptoms, health conditions, and current treatments to identify suitable ways to build activity gradually.

Chronic illness can also affect emotional health. The [University of Utah School of Medicine](https://medicine.utah.edu/psychiatry/clinics/geriatric-psychiatry/facts) notes that chronic conditions in later life can increase the risk of depression. If low mood or loss of interest persists, mention it to a healthcare professional. Considering physical and emotional well-being together can help clarify what support may be useful.

### Support Mental Health Through Grief, Stress, and Cognitive Changes

Grief, chronic pain, health concerns, and changes in family or work roles can affect mental well-being. Depression and anxiety do not always show up as sadness or worry. Changes in sleep, appetite, energy, concentration, or physical comfort may also be signs worth discussing with a healthcare professional, especially when they persist or interfere with daily life.

Mental health concerns are not an inevitable part of aging, and support is available. The [University of Utah School of Medicine’s overview of older adult mental health](https://medicine.utah.edu/psychiatry/clinics/geriatric-psychiatry/facts) explains that untreated conditions can affect daily function and quality of life. Human performance care can be part of a broader conversation about well-being, but it should complement mental health care when needed. Seek prompt help if you or someone close to you may be at risk of harm.

### Challenge the Myth That Older Adults Must Lift Heavy to Build Strength

Strength training does not require lifting the heaviest weight you can manage. Resistance bands, body-weight movements, and lighter weights can all challenge your muscles when chosen and progressed appropriately. The right level depends on your strength, joint health, balance, medical history, and experience with exercise.

A [discussion of strength training for older adults](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZDP_qHwJwPY) explains that lighter resistance can be effective when it challenges the working muscles. You do not need to train to exhaustion or ignore discomfort to make progress. A clinician or qualified exercise professional can help select movements, set a manageable starting point, and adjust the program over time. Stop and seek guidance if an exercise causes sharp pain, dizziness, unusual shortness of breath, or other concerning symptoms.

## How Do You Build a Personalized Plan?

A personalized plan starts with your health, your daily life, and what you want to be able to do. You might want to walk farther, climb stairs with less discomfort, return to gardening, or feel steadier on your feet. These goals help shape a plan around meaningful changes, rather than a generic routine based on age alone.

Your clinician can review your medical history, current treatments, symptoms, activity level, and everyday function. Together, you can discuss which steps may be appropriate, such as supervised exercise, nutrition support, recovery strategies, or a referral to a specialist. Your plan should account for health conditions, medications, pain, balance, and other factors that affect safety.

Progress may involve more than getting stronger or moving faster. Sleeping better, feeling more confident during daily activities, and managing symptoms with fewer disruptions can matter just as much. Set a manageable starting point, agree on how you’ll track changes, and check in regularly. Before starting a new exercise routine or changing medication or supplements, ask a qualified clinician for guidance. A thoughtful plan can adapt as your needs and abilities change.

### Review Health History, Medications, Pain, Fitness, and Daily Function

A useful assessment looks beyond a diagnosis or a single fitness measure. Share your medical history, surgeries, medications, supplements, pain patterns, sleep habits, and any recent changes in energy, strength, or balance. Describe tasks that feel difficult, such as standing from a chair, carrying groceries, or walking on uneven ground. These details help your care team understand how your health affects daily life.

Include emotional well-being in the conversation. Stress, grief, or low mood can affect sleep, motivation, and activity, but may be overlooked when an appointment focuses on physical symptoms. The [SAMHSA guide to depression treatment in older adults](https://e4center.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/12/Treatment-of-Depression-in-Older-Adults-2011.pdf) highlights the importance of supporting personal recovery goals and participation in everyday life. Sharing the full picture gives your clinician more context for recommendations.

### Identify Personal Goals, Barriers, and Safety Needs

Start with what you want to do more comfortably or confidently. You might aim to walk around your neighborhood, garden for longer, or feel steadier getting in and out of the shower. Specific goals give your care team a practical way to tailor recommendations and help you notice changes that matter to you.

Next, discuss anything that could make the plan difficult to follow. Pain, fatigue, transportation, cost, fear of falling, or limited access to equipment can all affect what is realistic. Mention concerns about mood, stress, or isolation, too. Older adults may focus on physical symptoms and miss changes in emotional well-being, as [Nursing@Georgetown explains](https://online.nursing.georgetown.edu/blog/mental-health-aging-americans). By identifying barriers and safety needs early, you and your clinician can choose appropriate activities, support, and a starting level that feels manageable.

### Set Measurable Goals and Track Strength, Mobility, and Well-Being

Choose a few simple measures that relate to your goals. With your clinician, you might track how long you can walk comfortably, how easily you stand from a chair, how often pain interrupts an activity, or how confident you feel on stairs. Repeat the measures at agreed intervals to spot changes over time.

Progress can vary from week to week. Illness, changes in sleep, medication, or pain may affect how you feel and move. Keep notes on these factors alongside your activity, and tell your care team about setbacks or new symptoms. Your measures can also include sleep, mood, and confidence, not just strength or endurance. When appropriate, care may combine medical treatment, behavioral support, and social strategies. [Collaborative care approaches for older adults](https://www.thesupportivecare.com/blog/mental-health-interventions-for-seniors-with-chronic-illnesses) can bring different forms of support together to address related needs.

### Coordinate With Current Clinicians and Refer for Specialized Care

Make sure your care team understands your goals and the parts of the plan you are following. With your permission, your primary care clinician, physical therapist, specialist, and other providers can share relevant information about your symptoms, medications, restrictions, and progress. Coordination is especially helpful when you have multiple health conditions or are considering a new treatment alongside existing care.

Ask who is responsible for each part of the plan and whom to contact if your symptoms change. If a concern calls for expertise beyond your current team, ask about a referral. For example, mental health support may help when grief, stress, or depression affects daily activities or your ability to take part in care. The [American Medical Association’s guide to behavioral health integration](https://www.ama-assn.org/system/files/bhi-older-adults-practice-guide.pdf) describes how behavioral health support can be incorporated into care for older adults. Clear communication helps keep recommendations consistent and suited to your needs.

### Reassess and Adapt the Plan Regularly

Schedule check-ins to discuss what is working, what feels challenging, and whether your priorities have changed. Your clinician may adjust activity levels, exercise choices, recovery time, or referrals based on your response and overall health. If an activity causes lasting pain, dizziness, unusual shortness of breath, or another concerning symptom, stop and seek medical advice instead of pushing through.

Your plan should also reflect changes in mood, motivation, and support needs. Tell your care team if you feel persistently anxious, low, isolated, or overwhelmed, since emotional health can affect daily function and quality of life. The [University of Utah’s overview of mental health and aging](https://medicine.utah.edu/psychiatry/clinics/geriatric-psychiatry/facts) outlines why mental health concerns in older adults deserve attention. Regular reassessment helps keep your plan relevant, safe, and connected to what matters to you.

## Which Exercises Help Older Adults Maintain Strength and Mobility?

A balanced routine can support the strength, stamina, and steadiness you need for everyday activities, from carrying groceries to getting up from a chair. The best exercises depend on your health, fitness level, pain, and goals. Start with movements you can do comfortably and safely, then build gradually. If you have a chronic condition, recent injury, or concerns about falling, ask a healthcare professional how to tailor your routine.

### Build Toward 150 Minutes of Moderate Aerobic Activity, as Appropriate

Walking, stationary cycling, swimming, and water exercise can build cardiovascular fitness and make daily activities feel less tiring. A widely used goal is at least 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity each week, spread across several days. At a moderate pace, your breathing and heart rate increase, but you can still talk.

You don’t need to reach that target immediately. Begin with a few minutes at a time, then add time or sessions as your stamina improves. The [Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans](https://health.gov/our-work/nutrition-physical-activity/physical-activity-guidelines/current-guidelines) recommend that older adults include aerobic activity, muscle strengthening, and balance activities. If walking worsens joint pain, consider a lower-impact option and ask a clinician or physical therapist what may work for you.

### Strength Train at Least Twice a Week and Progress Gradually

Strength training helps maintain the muscle you use to stand, climb stairs, carry bags, and move around independently. Aim to work the major muscle groups at least twice a week, with recovery time between challenging sessions. Chair stands, wall push-ups, resistance-band rows, and supervised weight training are possible options.

If you’re new to strength exercise, start with a few controlled repetitions and focus on your form. When an exercise feels manageable, add repetitions or resistance gradually. It’s not too late to begin: older adults can gain strength through consistent training. If you have pain, balance concerns, or a health condition that affects exercise, ask a healthcare professional to help you choose appropriate movements and a safe pace of progression.

### Use Weights, Resistance Bands, or Body-Weight Exercises at the Right Challenge

Heavy weights aren’t required to build strength. Resistance bands, light dumbbells, and body-weight exercises can all be useful when they provide enough challenge. Choose a level of resistance that lets you move with control and maintain good form. The last few repetitions should feel difficult, but not painful or so strenuous that you need to hold your breath or swing your body.

Try a few repetitions of a supported chair stand or band row to assess how it feels. If you can complete them comfortably, gradually add repetitions or resistance. If the exercise is too difficult, reduce the resistance or range of motion. A physical therapist or qualified exercise professional can help you adjust movements and check your technique.

### Practice Balance, Flexibility, and Functional Movements

Balance practice can help you feel steadier as you move through daily life. Try supported heel-to-toe standing, side steps along a counter, or standing on one leg while holding a sturdy chair. Stay close to stable support, and ask someone to be nearby if you’re concerned about falling.

Tai chi, gentle yoga, and Pilates can combine controlled movement with balance and flexibility practice. Functional exercises, such as standing up from a chair or stepping onto a low platform, rehearse movements you use at home. Choose activities that feel safe, and use support when needed. The [CDC’s fall-prevention guidance](https://www.cdc.gov/falls/about/index.html) includes exercise as one way to support strength and balance.

### Adapt Activity for Pain, Joint Conditions, Fall Risk, and Recovery

Exercise should challenge you, but sharp or worsening pain is a reason to stop and reassess. If a movement bothers a joint, try a smaller range of motion, lighter resistance, or a lower-impact alternative. With guidance from a clinician or physical therapist, you may be able to begin with isometric exercises, which involve gently tightening a muscle without moving the joint, then progress as tolerated.

If you’re at risk of falling, practice balance near a counter or with professional supervision. Allow time to recover between sessions, especially when starting a routine or returning after illness. Mild soreness can follow unfamiliar activity, but persistent pain, swelling, dizziness, or unusual shortness of breath warrants medical advice. Ask your care team how to adjust activity to your health and recovery needs.

## How Do Nutrition, Recovery, and Social Connection Support Healthspan?

Staying strong and active as you age takes more than exercise alone. What you eat and drink, how well you recover, and the support you get from other people can all shape your energy and ability to manage daily activities. These habits work together: nutritious meals help fuel movement, rest gives your body time to recover, and social connections can make healthy routines easier to maintain.

There is no single plan that fits everyone. Health conditions, medications, appetite, mobility, and personal goals all matter. Choose changes you can maintain, and ask your care team for help adapting them to your needs. If pain, fatigue, sleep problems, or changes in mood make it difficult to stay active, mention them to a clinician rather than trying to push through on your own.

### Choose Protein-Rich, Nutrient-Dense Foods and Drink Enough Water

Protein helps your body maintain and repair muscle. Include a protein source in meals and snacks, such as eggs, fish, poultry, beans, tofu, yogurt, or nuts. Pair these foods with vegetables, fruits, whole grains, and healthy fats to support an overall balanced eating pattern. A simple first step is adding yogurt or eggs to breakfast, or including beans or fish with lunch.

Fluid needs vary with activity, temperature, health conditions, and medications. Keep water nearby if that helps you drink regularly, but follow any fluid limits prescribed for heart, kidney, or other conditions. If your appetite, thirst, or energy changes, or you are unsure what foods or fluids suit your health needs, ask a clinician or registered dietitian for advice.

### Pair Protein With Resistance Training to Support Muscle

Protein supports muscle, but it is only one part of maintaining strength. Resistance training challenges muscles through activities such as chair stands, wall push-ups, resistance-band exercises, or lifting weights. Begin with a level that feels manageable, then increase the challenge gradually with guidance if needed. Older adults can build strength, even if they have not exercised regularly before.

A small eight-week study in women found greater muscle gains in the group that ate more protein while following the same resistance-training program. Some experts use a target of 1.2 to 1.6 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day for people seeking muscle gains, while the general recommended dietary allowance is about 0.8 grams per kilogram. These are general figures, not personal prescriptions. A clinician or dietitian can help determine what is appropriate, particularly if you have kidney disease. The National Institute on Aging’s strength-training guidance includes examples for older adults.

### Tailor Food, Fluids, and Supplements to Health Conditions

A nutrition plan that works well for one person may not suit another. Kidney, heart, and digestive conditions can affect food and fluid needs. Medications may also influence appetite, hydration, or nutrient levels. Before taking a supplement, check with a clinician or pharmacist. Supplements can interact with prescription medicines, so bring a list of everything you take, including vitamins, herbal products, and over-the-counter medicines, to appointments.

Alcohol can also affect health and recovery, especially when combined with medication. The body may respond to alcohol differently with age, and certain combinations can increase the risk of side effects. The [Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration’s guidance for older adults](https://www.samhsa.gov/sites/default/files/state-ta-supporting-mental-health-older-adults.pdf) describes concerns about alcohol and some medications, including medicines used to treat mental health conditions. Ask your care team about the risks that apply to you.

### Support Recovery With Sleep, Rest, and Appropriate Activity

Your body needs time to recover from exercise and everyday demands. Build rest into your routine, especially when you start a new activity or increase its intensity. Depending on your symptoms and medical guidance, gentle walking or light mobility exercises may feel better than staying still. If pain becomes sharp, worsens, or continues after activity, pause and ask a clinician how to adjust your plan.

Sleep supports energy, mood, and recovery. Regular activity may benefit overall well-being, but it does not replace addressing ongoing sleep problems. Try keeping a consistent sleep schedule, and talk with a clinician if pain, breathing issues, or other symptoms repeatedly interrupt your rest. You can track how you feel after exercise by noting sleep quality, soreness, and energy. Resting heart rate may offer one useful point of context, but it is best considered alongside symptoms and other measures, not as a stand-alone assessment.

### Connect With Others Through Exercise, Classes, Volunteering, and Regular Contact

Social connection can make healthy habits more enjoyable and easier to keep up. Consider joining a walking group or community class, volunteering, or scheduling regular calls with friends and family. If you are returning to exercise, look for an instructor who can offer modifications and a pace that feels comfortable. Choose activities that fit your interests, mobility, and schedule so they feel sustainable.

Research links social relationships with health outcomes, though those findings describe patterns across groups and cannot predict what will happen for any one person. Regular contact can still be a meaningful part of a healthspan plan. If leaving home is difficult, start with a short phone call or invite someone to join you for a brief, comfortable activity. If you feel isolated, tell someone you trust or ask your clinician about local groups and other sources of support.

### Seek Help for Persistent Loneliness, Anxiety, Depression, or Grief

Loneliness, anxiety, depression, and grief can affect anyone. When these experiences persist or interfere with daily life, support may help. Notice whether you are withdrawing from others, losing interest in activities, feeling worried or low much of the time, or having difficulty sleeping. You do not need to wait until symptoms feel severe to bring them up. A primary care clinician can discuss what you are experiencing, review possible health or medication factors, and suggest next steps.

Mental health conditions are not an inevitable part of aging, and support is available. The [World Health Organization’s overview of mental health in older adults](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/mental-health-of-older-adults) discusses depression and anxiety among the concerns older people may experience. Consider speaking with a mental health professional, especially if symptoms continue or affect relationships, self-care, or activities you value. If you feel unsafe or think you may harm yourself, contact emergency services or a crisis line for immediate help.

## What Should Older Adults Know About Medical and Regenerative Treatments?

Medical and regenerative treatments may help address specific health concerns, but no treatment can guarantee that you will feel or function like you did when you were younger. The best option depends on your diagnosis, health history, medications, symptoms, and personal goals. Before starting care, ask what condition the treatment is intended to address, what evidence supports its use for someone like you, and what other options are available.

Be cautious of claims that a therapy will reverse aging, eliminate pain, or restore function for everyone. Evidence differs across treatments, and research may be limited for some procedures marketed as regenerative care. [Balance Medical Integration’s overview of performance and longevity care](https://balancemedicalintegration.com/conditions-we-treat/performance-and-longevity/) notes that evidence varies by therapy. You can also check the FDA’s information on regenerative medicine products to learn about products that may not be approved for a particular use.

Before treatment begins, agree with your clinician on what improvement would look like and how you will track it. Changes in pain, strength, mobility, energy, or everyday activities can help you assess whether a plan is working. Regular follow-up gives your care team a chance to review your response, discuss side effects, and adjust care when needed.

### Use Hormone Therapy Only for Appropriate Diagnoses After Individual Evaluation

Hormone therapy should address a confirmed medical need, not be prescribed simply because aging can affect energy, strength, sleep, or body composition. These symptoms can have many causes, so a clinician should review your health history and decide whether testing is appropriate before recommending treatment. Growth hormone deficiency, for example, is uncommon and requires specialized evaluation. [Harvard Health explains the limits of growth hormone use](https://www.health.harvard.edu/diseases-and-conditions/growth-hormone-athletic-performance-and-aging), including why it is not a routine treatment for age-related changes.

Ask what diagnosis the therapy is meant to treat, which test results support that diagnosis, and whether other approaches may help. Your clinician should also consider other health conditions and medications that could contribute to your symptoms. An individual assessment helps distinguish a treatable deficiency from changes that can occur with age, and keeps decisions grounded in your health rather than broad promises.

### Understand the Risks and Limits of Testosterone and Other Hormone Treatments

Testosterone and other hormone treatments may help in specific situations, but they can also cause side effects and may not address symptoms when no deficiency is present. If testosterone is being considered, ask how your diagnosis was established, what benefits are realistic, and what monitoring you will need. The [Endocrine Society’s patient guidance on testosterone therapy](https://www.endocrine.org/patient-engagement/endocrine-library/testosterone-therapy) explains why treatment decisions should follow a clinical evaluation, rather than relying on age or one symptom alone.

Growth hormone also carries potential risks. Harvard Health notes that people treated for growth hormone deficiency may experience fluid retention, joint or muscle pain, carpal tunnel syndrome, or high blood sugar. Tell your clinician about new or worsening symptoms after starting treatment. Ask how often you will need follow-up visits or tests, and consult your care team before changing a dose or stopping a prescribed therapy.

### Know That Growth Hormone Is Not a Proven Anti-Aging Treatment

Growth hormone is sometimes promoted as a way to maintain muscle, energy, or a youthful appearance. However, research does not support using it as an anti-aging treatment for healthy older adults. A review discussed by [Harvard Health](https://www.health.harvard.edu/diseases-and-conditions/growth-hormone-athletic-performance-and-aging) found no significant improvements in several measures, including cholesterol, aerobic capacity, bone density, or fasting blood sugar and insulin levels. Potential side effects are also important to weigh when benefits are uncertain.

Growth hormone may be prescribed for certain diagnosed conditions under specialist care. That is different from taking it to counter normal aging. If a clinic recommends it for longevity or performance, ask what diagnosis supports the treatment, whether the research applies to your health profile, and how potential risks will be monitored. Be wary of claims that promise the same results for everyone.

### Ask About Evidence, Safety, and Realistic Outcomes for Regenerative Procedures

“Regenerative treatment” can refer to different procedures and products, so ask your clinician to explain exactly what is being proposed. Find out what evidence supports its use for your specific condition, whether it is approved for that use, and whether studies show meaningful changes in pain or function. The FDA’s consumer alert on regenerative medicine products explains that some products marketed for these purposes may not be FDA-approved.

Ask about possible complications, the clinician’s experience, the full cost, and what alternatives are available. You can also ask how long it may take to assess results and what the next step would be if the treatment does not help. A responsible clinician should explain uncertainty clearly, avoid guaranteeing outcomes, and discuss established options such as rehabilitation when appropriate.

### Monitor Your Response and Adjust Care With Clinical Guidance

Before starting a treatment, agree with your clinician on what you hope to improve and how you will measure progress. Depending on your goals, useful measures might include walking distance, stair climbing, pain levels, sleep, or your ability to manage everyday tasks. Keep brief notes about changes and any side effects so you can share a clear picture at follow-up visits.

Recovery and treatment response vary from person to person, and older adults may need more time between demanding activities. If symptoms worsen, progress stalls, or new concerns arise, contact your care team rather than pushing through or making treatment changes on your own. Your clinician can review your response, consider other factors, and adjust the plan when appropriate. Regular check-ins help keep care focused on your safety and the activities that matter to you.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is human performance medicine only for people who already exercise?**\
No. It can be useful whether you are active, returning to movement after a break, or unsure where to start. A clinician can consider your health and current abilities when helping you choose manageable next steps.

**Can I start exercising if I have joint pain or a chronic condition?**\
Often, activity can be adapted, but the right approach depends on what is causing your symptoms and your overall health. Ask a healthcare professional for guidance, especially if pain is new or worsening, or you have dizziness, numbness, or balance concerns.

**How do I know if my plan is working?**\
Look for changes that connect to your daily goals, such as walking more comfortably, feeling steadier on stairs, or finding it easier to stand from a chair. Your care team can help choose ways to track progress and decide when to adjust the plan.

**Are hormone or regenerative treatments right for everyone?**\
No. These treatments may be appropriate for certain diagnoses, but benefits, risks, and supporting evidence vary. Ask what condition a treatment is intended to address, what evidence applies to your situation, and what alternatives are available.

**When should I talk with a clinician about low energy or changes in mood?**\
If low energy, poor sleep, anxiety, low mood, or loss of interest persists or affects daily life, bring it up with a healthcare professional. These changes can have several causes, including health conditions or medication effects, and deserve individual attention.
