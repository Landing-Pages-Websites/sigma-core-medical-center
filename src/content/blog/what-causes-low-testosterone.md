Low testosterone can affect libido, erections, energy, strength, mood, body composition, and recovery. But these symptoms can also come from sleep disorders, thyroid problems, medication effects, chronic illness, stress, or changes in activity and nutrition. Understanding **what causes low testosterone in men** is therefore a starting point for an appropriate evaluation, not a reason to diagnose yourself from symptoms or begin treatment on your own.

This educational guide explains common causes, the difference between a temporary low result and persistent testosterone deficiency, and what to discuss with a qualified clinician. It does not diagnose a hormone condition or recommend a specific treatment for any individual.

## Key Takeaways

- Low testosterone may involve the testicles, the hypothalamus and pituitary gland, medications, a broader health condition, or several factors at once.
- Symptoms alone do not confirm testosterone deficiency. A clinician considers the symptom pattern and appropriately timed laboratory testing together.
- A single low result can occur during illness, poor sleep, undernutrition, or other temporary stress. Repeat testing may be needed when clinically appropriate.
- Age, body composition, sleep, chronic disease, and medications can influence hormone levels, but age alone does not establish a diagnosis.
- Evaluation should include personal goals, fertility plans, current medicines, sleep, and other health risks before any treatment discussion.

## What Is Low Testosterone in Men?

Testosterone is produced mainly in the testicles in response to signals from the hypothalamus and pituitary gland. It supports sexual function, sperm production, muscle and bone maintenance, red blood cell production, and aspects of energy and mood. Levels naturally vary between people and within the same person over time.

The term **hypogonadism** describes a condition in which the body does not produce enough testosterone for its needs. It may result from a problem in the testicles, a problem in the brain signals that regulate the testicles, or a combination of factors. Some men have symptoms with a clear hormonal cause; others have similar symptoms while testosterone levels are within the expected range.

A lab report should be interpreted using the laboratory’s reference range, the timing of the sample, the person’s symptoms, and the broader health history. A number by itself is not a complete diagnosis.

### Symptoms that may lead someone to seek an evaluation

Possible symptoms include reduced interest in sex, erectile changes, fewer spontaneous erections, low energy, reduced strength, loss of muscle, increased body fat, lower motivation, depressed or irritable mood, difficulty concentrating, or slower recovery from activity. Some men may also have reduced body hair, breast tenderness, hot flashes, infertility, or decreased bone density.

These symptoms are not specific to low testosterone. Sleep apnea, anemia, depression, thyroid disorders, diabetes, chronic pain, medication effects, and reduced conditioning can look similar. Sexual symptoms and changes in physical function deserve attention, but they should be evaluated in context rather than attributed automatically to hormones.

## What Causes Low Testosterone in Men?

Causes generally fall into two broad patterns. **Primary hypogonadism** begins in the testicles. **Secondary hypogonadism** involves the hypothalamus or pituitary gland, which normally coordinate the signals that stimulate testosterone production. Medications, serious illness, sleep disruption, body-composition changes, and other conditions may affect either pattern or temporarily suppress hormone signaling.

The categories can overlap. A man may have age-related changes along with sleep apnea, medication effects, metabolic disease, or a prior testicular injury. Identifying the contributors is more useful than assuming there is one explanation.

### Testicular conditions and injuries

Testosterone production may be affected by conditions or damage involving the testicles. Possible contributors include:

- Genetic or chromosomal conditions, including Klinefelter syndrome
- Testicles that did not descend normally during childhood
- Testicular injury, torsion, surgery, or loss of testicular tissue
- Certain infections, including mumps-related inflammation
- Testicular tumors or other direct disease
- Injury associated with chemotherapy or radiation treatment

The same conditions can affect sperm production and fertility. A history of testicular injury, surgery, cancer treatment, infertility, or delayed puberty is important information to share during an evaluation.

### Hypothalamic and pituitary conditions

The hypothalamus and pituitary gland regulate the signals that tell the testicles to produce testosterone and sperm. A pituitary tumor, elevated prolactin, prior head injury, brain surgery, radiation near the pituitary region, or an uncommon genetic signaling disorder may interfere with that communication.

Severe headaches, vision changes, loss of peripheral vision, unusual breast discharge, delayed puberty, reduced sense of smell, or fertility concerns may provide clues that a broader evaluation is needed. These symptoms do not identify a cause on their own, but they should be discussed promptly with a clinician.

### Sleep disorders, illness, and physical stress

Poor sleep and obstructive sleep apnea can affect energy, sexual function, recovery, and hormone patterns. A low result may also occur during a serious illness, hospitalization, major inflammation, or another period of physical stress. Restrictive dieting, rapid weight loss, and inadequate calorie or protein intake can affect the body’s reproductive hormone signals as well.

A person who snores loudly, wakes gasping, has witnessed breathing pauses, or remains unrefreshed after sleep should mention those symptoms. Treating a sleep or health problem may be an important part of understanding the hormone picture, whether or not testosterone treatment is ultimately considered.

### Body composition and metabolic health

Higher body fat, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome can be associated with lower testosterone or changes in the proteins that carry testosterone in the blood. These relationships are complex and can run in both directions: reduced activity and loss of muscle may worsen metabolic health, while metabolic conditions may affect energy, sexual function, and hormone signaling.

Body weight is only one part of an evaluation. A clinician may consider waist measurement, activity, nutrition, blood sugar, sleep, medications, and other health factors rather than treating a single measurement as the explanation.

### Chronic disease and endocrine conditions

Chronic kidney or liver disease, inflammatory illness, HIV, cancer, thyroid disorders, and other endocrine conditions may affect hormone production, hormone signaling, or the way symptoms are experienced. Chronic pain and reduced mobility can also lower activity and disrupt sleep, creating symptoms that overlap with low testosterone.

The relevant evaluation depends on the history and examination. It may be appropriate to assess other health conditions rather than focusing only on testosterone.

### Medications and substance exposure

Some medicines and substances can affect testosterone production or the signals that regulate it. Examples may include opioid pain medicines, long-term corticosteroids, certain medicines used in cancer care, and anabolic steroid use followed by withdrawal. Alcohol and recreational substances may also affect sleep, general health, or hormone signaling.

Never stop or change a prescription on your own. A clinician can review the medication, dose, timing, risks, and alternatives. For some medicines, the health benefit may outweigh a possible effect on hormone levels, and the safest plan may involve monitoring rather than an abrupt change.

## How Does Aging Affect Testosterone?

Testosterone levels often change gradually with age, but the amount and clinical significance vary widely. Sleep, body composition, activity, chronic disease, medications, and overall health can influence levels as people get older. A new decline in energy or sexual function should not automatically be labeled a normal part of aging.

Age-related symptoms can also arise from arthritis, cardiovascular disease, sleep apnea, depression, thyroid disease, anemia, or deconditioning. An evaluation can help distinguish a hormone concern from another condition that may need attention.

Free testosterone and total testosterone can tell different parts of the story because binding proteins change with age and health. A clinician decides which measurements are relevant and how to interpret them together.

## Can Lifestyle Factors Lower Testosterone?

Lifestyle factors may contribute to low or changing testosterone, although they are rarely the only explanation. Factors that can be relevant include:

- Short or fragmented sleep and untreated sleep apnea
- Long periods of inactivity and loss of muscle conditioning
- Excessive training without enough recovery or nutrition
- Restrictive diets, rapid weight loss, or inadequate nutrient intake
- Heavy alcohol use, nicotine, or recreational substances
- Ongoing stress and limited recovery time
- Changes in body fat, insulin resistance, or metabolic health

Regular movement, adequate nutrition, consistent sleep, and a sustainable approach to strength and aerobic activity may support overall health and physical performance. They are not a substitute for evaluation when symptoms persist, and they should be adapted to a person’s health and ability.

## How Is Low Testosterone Evaluated?

Evaluation starts with a discussion of symptoms, timing, goals, medical history, medicines, supplements, sleep, activity, fertility plans, and relevant family history. The clinician may also ask how symptoms affect exercise, work, relationships, and daily function.

When testing is appropriate, testosterone is usually measured in the morning because levels follow a daily pattern. A clinician may recommend repeat testing when an initial result is low or does not fit the overall picture. Additional tests can help distinguish a testicular cause from a pituitary or hypothalamic cause and look for other explanations. Depending on the history, this may include luteinizing hormone, follicle-stimulating hormone, prolactin, thyroid markers, blood counts, metabolic testing, or other targeted tests.

Testing during a major acute illness, after a severely disrupted night of sleep, or during significant undernutrition may not represent a person’s usual state. The timing and interpretation of tests belong in a clinician-guided evaluation.

### Information to bring to an appointment

A short symptom timeline can make the conversation more useful. Note when changes began, whether they are progressing, and how they affect libido, erections, sleep, strength, mood, exercise, and daily activities. Bring a complete list of prescriptions, over-the-counter products, supplements, alcohol or substance use that may be relevant, recent lab reports, and a record of major illnesses or injuries.

Also bring your goals. Someone focused on fertility may need a different conversation from someone focused on energy, strength, sexual function, or recovery. Questions may include:

- Could another health issue explain these symptoms?
- Which tests are appropriate, and how should the results be interpreted?
- Could a medicine, sleep problem, or recent illness be contributing?
- How would fertility goals affect the options under discussion?
- What follow-up and monitoring would be needed?

## What Are the Treatment Considerations?

Treatment depends on the cause, the person’s symptoms and goals, health risks, and whether the findings consistently support a hormone deficiency. It may involve addressing sleep, nutrition, activity, body composition, a medication effect, a chronic condition, or another identified cause. Some men may have a treatment discussion that includes testosterone therapy; that decision requires individualized review of potential benefits, limitations, risks, fertility goals, and monitoring.

Testosterone therapy is not appropriate for everyone, and it should not be started based on symptoms or one lab result alone. It can affect sperm production and may require attention to blood counts, prostate-related considerations, sleep apnea, cardiovascular health, and other individual factors. A qualified clinician can explain what is relevant for a particular patient.

## When Should You Seek Medical Evaluation?

Consider scheduling an evaluation when low libido, erectile changes, persistent fatigue, reduced strength, loss of muscle, mood changes, infertility, or reduced physical function continue or interfere with daily life. An appointment is especially worthwhile when several symptoms occur together or when there is a history of testicular injury, pituitary disease, cancer treatment, chronic illness, sleep-disordered breathing, or medicines that may affect hormone signaling.

Seek prompt medical attention for sudden severe headache, new vision changes, a new testicular lump, sudden severe testicular pain, or other urgent symptoms. These concerns need appropriate medical assessment and should not be self-managed as a testosterone problem.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does fatigue mean I have low testosterone?**  
No. Fatigue can be related to sleep disorders, stress, anemia, thyroid disease, depression, medication effects, chronic illness, inadequate nutrition, and many other causes. Persistent fatigue deserves an evaluation rather than a self-diagnosis.

**Can one low testosterone test confirm a deficiency?**  
Usually, no. The result needs to be interpreted with symptoms, timing, the laboratory’s reference range, recent illness, medications, and other health information. A clinician may recommend repeat morning testing when appropriate.

**Is low testosterone a normal part of aging?**  
Testosterone and other aspects of health can change with age, but age alone does not establish a hormone deficiency. Sleep, body composition, chronic disease, medications, and activity can also affect symptoms and test results.

**Can low testosterone affect fertility?**  
Low testosterone and the conditions that cause it may affect sperm production and fertility. Testosterone medication can also reduce sperm production in some men. Fertility goals should be discussed before any hormone treatment decision.

**Should I stop a medicine if I think it is lowering testosterone?**  
No. Do not stop a prescription without medical guidance. Ask the prescribing clinician to review the medicine, possible alternatives, and whether follow-up testing is appropriate.

**What is a safe first step?**  
Write down persistent symptoms, their effect on your daily function, your medicines and supplements, and your goals. Then schedule a consultation with a qualified clinician who can decide whether testing or another evaluation makes sense.
