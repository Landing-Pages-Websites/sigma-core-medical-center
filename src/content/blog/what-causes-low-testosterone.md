Low testosterone can affect the way you feel, move, exercise, recover, and connect with your partner. Some men notice fewer spontaneous erections or lower libido first. Others become concerned about fatigue, muscle loss, reduced motivation, or a decline in strength. These changes are real, but they do not always point to a hormone deficiency. Learning **what causes low testosterone in men** helps separate possible contributors, from sleep apnea and obesity to chronic disease, prescription medications, testicular injury, and pituitary conditions. Ahead, you’ll learn how clinicians investigate these causes and how treatment decisions are made around your health, goals, and fertility plans.

## Key Takeaways

* **Diagnosis depends on patterns, not one result**: Ongoing symptoms should be paired with two properly timed morning testosterone tests and additional labs when needed.
* **The cause guides care**: Sleep disorders, metabolic conditions, medications, testicular problems, pituitary disorders, and lifestyle factors can affect testosterone and may need separate treatment.
* **Treatment should match your health goals**: Discuss fertility, prostate health, cardiovascular concerns, sleep apnea, medications, and follow-up testing before considering testosterone therapy.

## What Is Low Testosterone in Men?

Low testosterone means the body does not produce enough testosterone for its needs. The condition is also called male hypogonadism. Testosterone is made mainly in the testicles under the direction of signals from the hypothalamus and pituitary gland in the brain. Levels vary from one man to another and may change with age, sleep, illness, body composition, medications, and overall health.

Some men notice sexual changes first. Others experience fatigue, reduced strength, slower recovery, mood changes, or difficulty maintaining muscle. These symptoms can affect exercise, mobility, and independence, particularly for men who want to remain active as they age.

Low testosterone is not diagnosed from symptoms alone. Many of the same concerns can result from sleep apnea, thyroid disease, depression, diabetes, medication effects, or chronic illness. Diagnosis usually requires symptoms that fit the condition and consistently low testosterone on properly timed blood tests. A thorough evaluation can also identify whether the problem starts in the testicles, the pituitary gland, or another part of the body’s hormone system.

### Testosterone’s role in libido, erections, fertility, muscle, bone, mood, energy, and recovery

Testosterone supports much more than sex drive. It contributes to libido, erectile function, sperm production, muscle development, bone density, red blood cell production, mood, energy, and general well-being. It also helps the body maintain muscle tissue, which can influence strength, movement, and recovery from physical activity.

The effects of low testosterone vary. One man may notice less interest in sex, while another may experience declining strength, increased body fat, or slower recovery after exercise. Testosterone is also involved in sperm production, but testosterone medication can reduce sperm production in some men. Fertility plans should therefore be part of any treatment discussion. The [UNC Men’s Health Program explains testosterone’s role](https://www.med.unc.edu/urology/mens-health/2018/12/low-testosterone-male-hypogonadism/) in sexual function, muscle, bones, fertility, and overall health.

### Hypogonadism versus one temporary low result

A single low testosterone result does not always mean a man has hypogonadism. Testosterone can temporarily fall during an acute illness, after poor sleep, during severe calorie restriction, or while taking certain medications. Testing later may produce a different result once the temporary factor has improved.

A diagnosis usually requires both related symptoms and repeatedly low morning testosterone levels. The clinician will also look for the cause by reviewing medications, sleep, health conditions, and other hormone results. Conditions such as obesity, diabetes, sleep apnea, and pituitary disorders may contribute to low testosterone or create similar symptoms.

This distinction matters because treatment should address the reason for the abnormal result whenever possible. The Cleveland Clinic’s overview of low testosterone explains why symptoms and laboratory findings need to be considered together.

### The 300 ng/dL benchmark and lab-specific ranges

In adults, clinicians commonly use a total testosterone level below 300 nanograms per deciliter as a general reference point for low testosterone. It is not a diagnosis by itself. Laboratories use different testing methods and reference ranges, and results can vary with the time of day, recent illness, sleep quality, and testing conditions.

Testosterone is usually highest in the morning, so testing is generally performed early in the day. If the first result is low, the test is often repeated on another morning. When total testosterone is near the lower end of the range, a clinician may also assess free testosterone and sex hormone-binding globulin, or SHBG. These results can provide more information about how much testosterone is available to tissues. The [Endocrine Society’s clinical guidance](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/testosterone-therapy) outlines the importance of consistent results and related symptoms before treatment begins.

### Sexual symptoms: low libido, erectile changes, and fewer spontaneous erections

Sexual symptoms are among the more specific signs associated with low testosterone. They may include reduced interest in sex, fewer morning or spontaneous erections, and difficulty getting or keeping an erection. Some men also notice changes in orgasm or ejaculation, although these concerns often have more than one cause.

Blood vessel disease, diabetes, high blood pressure, medication effects, stress, depression, relationship concerns, and prostate conditions can all affect sexual function. Erectile changes may sometimes signal a circulation problem rather than a testosterone problem. A gradual change deserves medical attention, especially when it occurs alongside fatigue, loss of strength, or reduced body hair. Testing and a health history can help identify the factors involved.

### Physical symptoms: fatigue, reduced strength, muscle loss, body fat, and slower recovery

Men with low testosterone may feel persistently tired, lose strength, or find that familiar activities require more effort. Some experience reduced muscle mass, increased body fat, or difficulty maintaining physical performance despite regular activity. Recovery after exercise may also feel slower, although inadequate sleep, low protein intake, joint pain, inflammation, and poor cardiovascular fitness can cause similar changes.

These symptoms affect more than appearance. Loss of muscle and strength can make it harder to climb stairs, carry groceries, protect balance, or remain independent. An evaluation can show whether testosterone is contributing or whether another concern, such as anemia, thyroid disease, poor sleep, or chronic illness, needs attention. Harvard Health discusses the connection between testosterone, [fatigue, muscle changes, and body composition](https://www.health.harvard.edu/mens-health/why-testosterone-levels-drop-and-when-to-consider-treatment).

### Mood and cognitive symptoms: irritability, low motivation, depressed mood, and poor focus

Hormonal changes may affect mood, motivation, and mental clarity. Possible symptoms include irritability, low motivation, depressed mood, reduced confidence, poor concentration, and difficulty remembering information. Some men feel less interested in activities they once enjoyed or have less motivation to exercise and socialize.

These symptoms are not specific to testosterone deficiency. Depression, anxiety, chronic pain, grief, sleep apnea, medication effects, and thyroid disorders can cause similar changes. Mood symptoms deserve attention rather than being dismissed as a normal part of aging or treated with hormones without further evaluation. A clinician can consider mental health, sleep, medications, and hormone levels together. The [UNC Men’s Health Program describes mood and cognitive changes](https://www.med.unc.edu/urology/mens-health/2018/12/low-testosterone-male-hypogonadism/) that may occur with low testosterone.

### Other signs: body-hair loss, smaller testicles, breast tenderness, hot flashes, anemia, and bone loss

Low testosterone may cause physical changes that are easy to overlook. Possible signs include reduced pubic or armpit hair, smaller testicles, breast tenderness or enlargement, hot flashes, and increased body fat. Some men develop anemia, which can add to fatigue and reduce exercise tolerance. Long-standing deficiency may also contribute to lower bone density and a higher risk of fractures.

Not every man develops these changes, and some have no obvious physical signs. During an evaluation, a clinician may examine body composition, body hair, testicular size, breast tissue, and other features. Unexplained nipple discharge, a new breast lump, sudden testicular pain, or a rapidly changing testicle requires prompt medical attention rather than routine hormone testing.

### Why sleep disorders, thyroid problems, depression, and chronic disease can cause similar symptoms

Low energy, low libido, erectile changes, poor focus, and reduced strength do not point to testosterone deficiency alone. Obstructive sleep apnea can disrupt sleep and hormone patterns while causing daytime fatigue. Thyroid disorders, depression, diabetes, obesity, chronic kidney or liver disease, and persistent inflammation can also affect energy, sexual function, muscle, and mood.

These conditions may contribute to low testosterone, resemble its symptoms, or occur at the same time. Excess body fat and insulin resistance can affect hormone regulation, while chronic pain can interfere with sleep, activity, and sexual interest. A complete evaluation helps separate these possibilities and guides treatment toward the underlying cause. Harvard Health identifies [sleep apnea, obesity, and type 2 diabetes](https://www.health.harvard.edu/mens-health/why-testosterone-levels-drop-and-when-to-consider-treatment) as common factors that can overlap with low testosterone symptoms.

## What Causes Low Testosterone in Men?

Low testosterone, also called male hypogonadism, can develop for several reasons. The problem may begin in the testicles, where testosterone is produced, or in the hypothalamus and pituitary gland, which coordinate the signals that control production. Aging, excess body fat, poor sleep, chronic illness, certain medications, and prolonged physical stress can also affect testosterone levels.

Identifying the cause matters because treatment should address the underlying problem, not just the lab result. A man with low testosterone related to untreated sleep apnea may need a different plan from someone with testicular injury, medication-related suppression, or a pituitary disorder. The evaluation should consider sexual health, energy, strength, recovery, sleep, medications, and metabolic health.

Low testosterone can also occur alongside other conditions that produce similar symptoms. For example, fatigue and reduced motivation may relate to poor sleep, depression, anemia, thyroid disease, chronic pain, or cardiovascular problems. A careful assessment helps separate these possibilities and determine whether low testosterone is temporary, persistent, or part of a broader health concern.

### Primary versus secondary hypogonadism

Doctors generally classify low testosterone as either **primary** or **secondary hypogonadism**. Primary hypogonadism begins in the testicles. The brain may send appropriate hormonal signals, but the testicles cannot produce enough testosterone in response. Possible causes include testicular injury, infection, genetic conditions, cancer treatment, or loss of testicular tissue.

Secondary hypogonadism begins in the hypothalamus or pituitary gland. These areas of the brain direct testosterone production, so a problem with their signaling can prevent the testicles from receiving enough stimulation. In this case, the testicles may still be capable of producing testosterone.

Blood tests for luteinizing hormone, or LH, and follicle-stimulating hormone, or FSH, can help distinguish these patterns. The Cleveland Clinic’s overview of male hypogonadism explains how clinicians evaluate primary and secondary forms.

### How the testicles, hypothalamus, and pituitary regulate testosterone

Testosterone production depends on communication between the brain and the testicles. The hypothalamus releases gonadotropin-releasing hormone, which signals the pituitary gland. The pituitary then releases LH, which travels through the bloodstream to the testicles. In response, Leydig cells in the testicles produce testosterone.

The testicles also contain cells involved in sperm production, which is regulated partly by FSH. Testosterone levels and fertility are connected, but they are not the same. A man may have low testosterone, reduced sperm production, or both.

When testosterone levels rise, the body normally reduces some of the signals that stimulate production. This feedback system helps keep hormone levels within a healthy range. Problems at any point in this pathway can affect testosterone, which is why clinicians may check several hormones instead of relying on one result. [Harvard Health explains this hormone pathway](https://www.health.harvard.edu/mens-health/why-testosterone-levels-drop-and-when-to-consider-treatment).

### Temporary suppression versus lasting hormone deficiency

A single low testosterone result does not always indicate a permanent deficiency. Testosterone may fall temporarily during an acute illness, after significant sleep loss, during severe calorie restriction, or when the body is under major physical stress. Some medications can also suppress production for a period of time.

Testosterone levels naturally change throughout the day, so the timing of a blood test matters. Clinicians typically confirm a low result with two separate early-morning tests, especially when the first finding is unexpected. Testing in the morning helps account for normal daily variation.

The result should also be considered alongside persistent symptoms and relevant medical findings. A diagnosis generally requires consistently low blood testosterone and related clinical concerns. The Endocrine Society’s clinical guideline recommends confirming the result before treatment and evaluating the cause.

### Age, lifestyle, medical conditions, and medications

Testosterone often declines gradually with age, but age is only one factor. Excess body fat, insulin resistance, Type 2 diabetes, poor sleep, chronic stress, and limited physical activity can affect hormone production or the body’s response to testosterone. Heavy alcohol use and rapid weight loss may also contribute.

Medical conditions can influence hormone levels as well. Chronic kidney or liver disease, inflammatory illness, thyroid disorders, cancer, and persistent pain may alter hormone signaling. Medications, including opioid pain medicines and corticosteroids, can suppress testosterone in some men. Anabolic steroid use can also reduce the body’s own production, sometimes for an extended period after stopping.

A clinician should review your complete health history and medication list before recommending treatment. Do not stop a prescribed medication without medical guidance. This [review of common low-testosterone causes](https://atlanticurologyclinics.com/blog/9-causes-of-low-t/) provides additional context on lifestyle, health, and medication-related factors.

### Why causes often overlap after age 50

After age 50, low testosterone often reflects several connected changes rather than one isolated problem. Reduced activity can contribute to muscle loss and increased body fat. More body fat may worsen insulin resistance, while poor sleep or obstructive sleep apnea can further affect hormone signaling, energy, and recovery.

Chronic pain can create another cycle. Pain may limit exercise, interrupt sleep, and lead to medication use, all of which can influence testosterone and physical performance. A man may then notice lower strength, slower recovery, reduced motivation, and changes in sexual function at the same time.

These overlapping factors should not be dismissed as normal aging. Instead, they call for a broad evaluation that considers sleep quality, metabolic health, medication effects, physical activity, and other endocrine conditions. Addressing one contributor may support improvement in other areas, but the care plan should be based on appropriate testing and medical supervision.

### Why symptoms alone cannot reveal the cause

Low libido, erectile changes, fatigue, reduced strength, and low mood can occur with low testosterone, but they are not specific to it. Sleep apnea, depression, thyroid disease, anemia, medication side effects, chronic pain, and cardiovascular or metabolic conditions can cause similar symptoms.

Symptoms also vary among men. Some notice sexual changes first, while others become concerned about reduced strength, low motivation, or slower recovery. A symptom checklist cannot determine whether the cause is primary hypogonadism, secondary hypogonadism, a temporary hormone change, or another health problem.

A clinician may combine your symptoms, medical history, physical examination, and repeated hormone testing to identify the most likely cause. Persistent changes in sexual function, energy, strength, or recovery deserve professional evaluation rather than self-treatment based on a single test or supplement.

## What Testicular Problems Cause Low Testosterone?

The testicles produce testosterone and sperm, so conditions that damage or limit testicular tissue can reduce hormone production. These problems are known as **primary hypogonadism**, which means the testicles cannot make enough testosterone even when the hypothalamus and pituitary gland send appropriate hormonal signals.

Testicular problems may be present from birth, develop after an infection or injury, or result from surgery, cancer, chemotherapy, or radiation. Some conditions affect one testicle, while others involve both. The effect on testosterone depends on the amount of healthy tissue that remains and whether the condition also disrupts the brain’s hormone signals.

Possible symptoms include lower libido, erectile changes, fatigue, reduced strength, loss of muscle, mood changes, and slower recovery. Symptoms alone cannot identify the cause. Clinicians typically confirm low testosterone with two early-morning blood tests, then measure luteinizing hormone and follicle-stimulating hormone to determine whether the problem begins in the testicles or the brain.

A medical history, physical examination, fertility discussion, and additional testing may be needed. The Cleveland Clinic’s guide to male hypogonadism outlines several testicular and non-testicular causes.

### Klinefelter syndrome and other chromosomal conditions

Klinefelter syndrome occurs when a male is born with an extra X chromosome, most commonly an XXY chromosome pattern. This can interfere with testicular development, resulting in smaller testicles, lower testosterone production, and impaired sperm production. Some men are diagnosed during an infertility evaluation, while others are not identified until adulthood, after symptoms such as low energy, reduced muscle mass, or low libido appear.

Other chromosomal and genetic conditions can also affect testicular function. Because these conditions may influence bone health, body composition, fertility, and sexual function, evaluation often includes hormone testing and a review of reproductive history. Genetic testing may be appropriate when testicular size, fertility findings, or hormone patterns suggest an inherited condition. Testosterone treatment may improve certain symptoms, but it does not restore sperm production in every case.

### Undescended testicles and congenital disorders

An undescended testicle, or cryptorchidism, does not move into the scrotum before birth. A testicle that remains in the abdomen or groin may not develop normally, partly because the scrotum provides a cooler environment. This can affect testosterone production and increase the risk of reduced sperm production later in life.

The condition may affect one or both testicles. If only one testicle is involved, the other may produce enough testosterone for typical development, although fertility can still be affected. If both testicles are underdeveloped or damaged, low testosterone becomes more likely.

Men with a history of undescended testicles may benefit from hormone testing, fertility counseling, and ongoing medical follow-up. Surgery to move a testicle into the scrotum does not always remove long-term risks, particularly when the tissue was already underdeveloped.

### Testicular injury, torsion, surgery, or tissue loss

A severe blow to the testicles can damage the cells that produce testosterone. Testicular torsion is another serious problem. It occurs when the spermatic cord twists and cuts off blood flow, potentially causing permanent injury if treatment is delayed. **Sudden, severe testicular pain requires emergency evaluation**, even if the pain later improves.

Surgery can affect testosterone production when part or all of a testicle must be removed. Removing one testicle often leaves enough functioning tissue for typical hormone production, but the remaining testicle may not compensate fully if it is already impaired. Removing both testicles causes a major loss of testosterone production and requires specialist care.

The American Urological Association explains testicular torsion, including why it requires urgent treatment. Follow-up after an injury or operation may include testosterone testing, semen analysis, and examination of the remaining testicular tissue.

### Mumps orchitis and other infections

Mumps orchitis is inflammation of the testicle that can occur after a mumps infection. Inflammation may damage testosterone-producing cells and the structures involved in sperm production. The risk is higher when both testicles are affected, although inflammation in one testicle can also contribute to fertility problems.

Other infections, including some sexually transmitted infections, may cause inflammation in the testicles or nearby reproductive organs. Symptoms can include pain, swelling, warmth, fever, and tenderness. Prompt evaluation is important because infection, torsion, and other conditions can cause similar symptoms.

Testosterone levels may recover after an acute infection, but severe or prolonged inflammation can leave lasting damage. A clinician may recommend follow-up hormone testing if low libido, fatigue, erectile changes, or fertility concerns continue after the infection resolves.

### Testicular tumors and direct damage

Testicular tumors can affect testosterone production by damaging healthy tissue or replacing part of the testicle. Some tumors produce hormones, while others are discovered because of a lump, swelling, heaviness, or change in testicular size. A painless lump should be evaluated promptly, even when testosterone levels and sexual function seem normal.

Certain noncancerous conditions can also damage testicular tissue. Hemochromatosis, an iron-overload disorder, may interfere with hormone-producing cells. Sarcoidosis and other inflammatory conditions can affect the testicles directly or disrupt the hormonal signals that regulate them.

Evaluation may include a physical examination, blood tests, and scrotal ultrasound. Treatment depends on the cause and may involve a urologist, endocrinologist, oncologist, or another specialist. Testosterone therapy should not begin until an unexplained testicular mass has been evaluated.

### Chemotherapy and radiation-related injury

Cancer treatment can affect testosterone production, particularly when chemotherapy damages testicular cells or radiation reaches the testicles. The degree of injury depends on the treatment type, dose, treatment area, and whether one or both testicles were exposed. Some men experience temporary suppression, while others develop lasting primary hypogonadism.

Chemotherapy and radiation may also reduce sperm production, sometimes before testosterone levels change. Men who may want biological children should discuss fertility preservation before treatment when possible. Options may include sperm banking and referral to a reproductive specialist.

After cancer treatment, persistent fatigue, reduced libido, erectile changes, hot flashes, loss of strength, or reduced exercise tolerance deserve evaluation. Blood testing can show whether testosterone production has recovered. The [National Cancer Institute explains late effects of cancer treatment](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/coping/survivorship/late-effects) and the importance of follow-up care.

### Effects on testosterone, sperm production, and fertility

Testicular problems can affect testosterone and sperm production in different ways. A man may have low testosterone and a very low sperm count, or he may have impaired fertility while his testosterone remains within the expected range. Testosterone supports libido, erections, muscle, bone, mood, and energy. Sperm production follows a separate process regulated by follicle-stimulating hormone and testosterone within the testicles.

Testosterone therapy may improve symptoms of hormone deficiency, but it can also reduce the brain’s signals to the testicles and lower sperm production. Men who want to preserve fertility should discuss this before starting treatment. Depending on the cause, a specialist may consider options that support the body’s own hormone signals.

A complete evaluation may include morning testosterone, LH, FSH, prolactin, semen analysis, and scrotal imaging when indicated. These results help distinguish testicular damage from a pituitary or hypothalamic problem and guide treatment around both symptom relief and reproductive goals.

## What Pituitary and Hypothalamic Problems Cause Low Testosterone?

Low testosterone can result from a problem in the brain’s hormone signaling system rather than from damage to the testicles. This is known as **secondary hypogonadism**, or central hypogonadism. In this situation, the testicles may be capable of producing testosterone, but they do not receive enough hormonal direction from the hypothalamus and pituitary gland.

Potential causes include pituitary tumors, high prolactin levels, genetic conditions, head injuries, radiation exposure, iron overload, severe illness, undernutrition, and significant weight changes. Some causes are temporary, while others require long-term care.

Symptoms may include low libido, erectile changes, fatigue, reduced strength, infertility, or slower recovery. However, symptoms alone cannot identify the source. Clinicians typically compare testosterone with luteinizing hormone (LH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), prolactin, thyroid markers, medication history, and overall health. The Cleveland Clinic’s guide to male hypogonadism outlines several conditions that can interfere with this signaling pathway.

A careful evaluation matters, particularly for men who want to preserve fertility or who have symptoms involving vision, headaches, puberty, or other hormone systems.

### How the hypothalamus, pituitary, LH, and FSH regulate testosterone

The hypothalamus begins the process by releasing gonadotropin-releasing hormone. This prompts the pituitary gland to release LH and FSH. LH travels to the testicles and signals specialized cells to produce testosterone. FSH works with testosterone to support sperm production.

Testosterone then sends feedback to the hypothalamus and pituitary, helping keep hormone production in balance. If the hypothalamus or pituitary does not send enough signals, testosterone may fall even when the testicles are structurally healthy.

Blood tests can help distinguish a central problem from a testicular one. Low testosterone with low or inappropriately normal LH and FSH may suggest secondary hypogonadism, while high LH and FSH more often point toward testicular dysfunction.

### Pituitary tumors and high prolactin

A pituitary tumor can interfere with the cells that release LH and FSH. Some tumors also produce excess prolactin, a hormone that can suppress hypothalamic signaling. As a result, testosterone and sperm production may decline.

Possible symptoms include reduced libido, erectile changes, infertility, headaches, or vision problems. A large tumor may affect peripheral vision because the pituitary sits near the optic nerves.

Clinicians may check prolactin when testosterone is low and LH or FSH levels are low or unexpectedly normal. Persistent prolactin elevation may require additional testing and, when appropriate, an MRI. The [Endocrine Society’s patient information on pituitary tumors](https://www.endocrine.org/patient-engagement/endocrine-library/pituitary-tumors) explains why symptoms and hormone patterns both matter.

### Congenital signaling disorders, including Kallmann syndrome

Some men are born with conditions that affect hypothalamic signaling. **Kallmann syndrome** can prevent the normal release of gonadotropin-releasing hormone, leading to low LH, low FSH, low testosterone, and reduced sperm production.

Clues may include delayed or incomplete puberty, limited facial or body hair, smaller testicles, infertility, or a reduced sense of smell. Some men are not diagnosed until adulthood, particularly when they seek care for fertility concerns.

Other genetic conditions, including Prader-Willi syndrome, can also affect hypothalamic function. A history of delayed puberty, reduced sense of smell, or similar concerns among relatives can help guide testing. Treatment depends on the individual’s hormone levels, symptoms, fertility goals, and broader health history.

### Head injuries, brain surgery, and radiation exposure

The hypothalamus and pituitary gland sit at the base of the brain, where trauma, surgery, or radiation can affect them. A serious head injury may disrupt the pathways that control LH, FSH, and testosterone. Hormone changes can appear soon after the injury or become noticeable months or years later.

Brain surgery and radiation near the pituitary region can also affect other hormone systems. Men may experience low libido, fatigue, reduced strength, fertility problems, or changes in thyroid and adrenal function.

A medical history should include serious falls, concussions, neurosurgery, brain tumors, and cancer treatment. [Harvard Health describes the connection between head trauma, brain surgery, and testosterone production](https://www.health.harvard.edu/mens-health/why-testosterone-levels-drop-and-when-to-consider-treatment).

### Hemochromatosis and other signaling disorders

Hemochromatosis causes the body to store excess iron. Over time, iron can collect in organs such as the liver, pituitary gland, and testicles. When the pituitary is affected, it may release less LH and FSH, which can reduce testosterone and sperm production.

Other conditions can interfere with the hypothalamus or pituitary as well. These include inflammatory diseases, infections, infiltrative disorders, and tumors. The appropriate evaluation depends on symptoms, examination findings, family history, and hormone results.

Possible signs of iron overload include a family history of hemochromatosis, liver disease, diabetes, joint pain, skin darkening, or persistent fatigue. Clinicians may order ferritin and transferrin saturation tests when the hormone pattern or medical history raises concern. The Cleveland Clinic lists hemochromatosis among causes of secondary hypogonadism.

### Severe illness, undernutrition, and major weight changes

Severe illness can temporarily reduce hormone signaling between the brain and reproductive system. This may occur during a serious infection, hospitalization, prolonged inflammation, or major physical stress. Testosterone can also fall when the body does not receive enough calories, protein, or essential nutrients.

Rapid weight loss and restrictive dieting may suppress hypothalamic and pituitary signals. Excess body fat, insulin resistance, and poor metabolic health can also affect testosterone through several overlapping pathways.

A low testosterone result during an acute illness may not represent a lasting deficiency. Clinicians often repeat testing after recovery and review sleep, nutrition, medications, body composition, and chronic conditions before identifying the cause. The [Atlantic Urology Specialists overview of low testosterone causes](https://atlanticurologyclinics.com/blog/9-causes-of-low-t/) discusses illness, nutrition, and weight changes as possible contributors.

### Fertility clues and other reproductive hormone changes

Hypothalamic or pituitary problems can affect both testosterone and sperm production. Low LH reduces the signal needed for testicular testosterone production, while low FSH can interfere with sperm development. Some men may notice smaller testicles, difficulty conceiving, or a history of delayed puberty.

Laboratory patterns provide useful clues. Low testosterone with low or inappropriately normal LH and FSH may suggest a central cause. Elevated LH and FSH more often indicate a problem within the testicles. Prolactin, thyroid hormones, and other reproductive markers can provide additional context.

Discuss fertility plans before starting testosterone therapy. Testosterone treatment can reduce the hormone signals that support sperm production. Men who want to conceive may need a different treatment approach and evaluation by a clinician experienced in reproductive hormone care.

## How Does Aging Affect Testosterone?

Testosterone usually changes gradually with age rather than dropping suddenly. Some men notice little difference, while others develop changes in sexual function, strength, mood, energy, or recovery. These symptoms may become more noticeable when lower testosterone occurs alongside weight gain, poor sleep, chronic illness, medication use, or reduced activity.

Age is one factor, but it does not tell the whole story. Men in their 60s or 70s may have very different testosterone levels and symptoms. A careful evaluation considers a man’s health history, medications, sleep, physical function, symptoms, and properly timed lab results.

### The gradual decline that often starts in the 30s or 40s

Testosterone often begins to decline after age 30, with an average decrease of about 1% per year starting in the late 30s, according to the Cleveland Clinic’s overview of male hypogonadism. This is an average trend, not a fixed schedule. Some men maintain healthy levels for decades, while others experience a more noticeable change earlier in life.

The effects may involve more than sexual health. Lower testosterone can contribute to reduced muscle mass, lower bone density, less energy, depressed mood, and slower recovery after exercise or injury. However, these symptoms can also result from sleep apnea, thyroid disease, depression, diabetes, medication side effects, or other conditions. Aging alone cannot confirm testosterone deficiency.

### Why testosterone levels vary among men

Men of the same age can have very different testosterone levels and symptoms. Genetics, body composition, sleep quality, physical activity, alcohol use, stress, medications, and chronic disease can all affect hormone production. Obesity and Type 2 diabetes are especially common contributors, even when there is no direct problem with the testicles or pituitary gland, as the Cleveland Clinic explains.

Testosterone also follows a daily pattern. Levels are typically highest in the morning and may temporarily fall during acute illness, significant sleep loss, severe calorie restriction, or intense physical stress. Because of this variation, one result may not reflect a man’s usual level. Clinicians interpret lab findings alongside symptoms and may repeat testing before making a diagnosis.

### Total testosterone, free testosterone, and age-related SHBG changes

A testosterone test may measure total testosterone, which includes both protein-bound and unbound testosterone. Free testosterone is the portion that is not tightly attached to a protein and is available for tissues to use. The relationship between these measurements may become more important with age because sex hormone-binding globulin, or SHBG, often increases over time. Higher SHBG can bind more testosterone, leaving less free testosterone even when total testosterone appears acceptable.

A total testosterone level below 300 nanograms per deciliter is a commonly used clinical threshold, but one result does not establish a diagnosis. [Harvard Health explains that low results should be confirmed with two fasting blood tests](https://www.health.harvard.edu/mens-health/why-testosterone-levels-drop-and-when-to-consider-treatment) taken on separate mornings. When total testosterone is borderline or does not match a man’s symptoms, a clinician may assess free testosterone and SHBG using reliable testing methods.

### Body fat, insulin resistance, muscle loss, and slower recovery

Changes in body composition can both accompany and contribute to lower testosterone. Increased abdominal fat may alter hormone signaling and increase the conversion of testosterone into estradiol. Insulin resistance and Type 2 diabetes can also interfere with normal testosterone production. As testosterone falls, some men experience reduced muscle mass and strength, making exercise and everyday activities more demanding.

This can create a difficult cycle. Less muscle and physical activity may lead to further deconditioning, while excess weight can place additional stress on painful joints and limit movement. Addressing metabolic health, preserving muscle with appropriate resistance training, eating enough protein, and treating related medical conditions can support overall hormone health. These steps do not replace medical evaluation when symptoms continue.

### Sleep disorders and chronic disease that worsen age-related changes

Sleep has a close relationship with testosterone production. Hormone levels follow a daily rhythm that depends partly on adequate, uninterrupted sleep. Obstructive sleep apnea, frequent nighttime waking, insomnia, and untreated pain can interfere with this rhythm. Chronic stress may also raise cortisol, which can disrupt the signals involved in testosterone production. [Atlantic Urology Specialists describes stress, sleep problems, and medical conditions as possible contributors to low testosterone](https://atlanticurologyclinics.com/blog/9-causes-of-low-t/).

Kidney disease, liver disease, diabetes, thyroid disorders, and chronic inflammatory conditions may add to age-related changes. Certain medications can also affect hormone levels or sexual function. Treating an underlying condition may improve symptoms and hormone production, so an evaluation should include sleep quality, medications, mental health, metabolic health, and other ongoing health concerns.

### Why age alone cannot confirm testosterone deficiency

Growing older does not automatically mean a man has testosterone deficiency. Diagnosis generally requires both consistent symptoms and repeatedly low testosterone levels measured under appropriate conditions. Fatigue, low motivation, erectile changes, reduced strength, and poor concentration are important clues, but they are not specific to testosterone. Poor sleep, depression, anemia, medication effects, and chronic disease can produce a similar pattern.

Test results can also vary based on the time of day, recent illness, fasting status, laboratory method, and the interval between tests. A clinician may order LH, FSH, prolactin, thyroid markers, blood counts, and metabolic tests to identify the likely cause. As [Harvard Health notes](https://www.health.harvard.edu/mens-health/why-testosterone-levels-drop-and-when-to-consider-treatment), testosterone therapy should not begin based on symptoms alone or a single low result.

### When persistent symptoms after age 50 warrant evaluation

Men over 50 should consider a professional evaluation when symptoms persist, worsen, or interfere with daily life. Concerns may include a sustained loss of sexual desire, erectile changes, fewer spontaneous erections, unexplained fatigue, reduced strength, muscle loss, depressed mood, difficulty concentrating, or slower recovery despite reasonable sleep and activity habits.

An evaluation is especially important when symptoms occur with weight gain, diabetes, sleep apnea, chronic pain, fertility concerns, or long-term opioid or corticosteroid use. Seek urgent medical care for sudden testicular pain, a new testicular lump, severe headache with vision changes, or unexpected nipple discharge. A clinician can determine whether testing is appropriate, identify reversible contributors, and discuss treatment based on symptoms, lab results, fertility goals, prostate health, and overall risk.

## Which Lifestyle Factors Lower Testosterone?

Lifestyle factors can influence testosterone through several connected pathways, including sleep, body composition, metabolic health, stress, exercise, nutrition, and substance use. These factors may contribute to low testosterone, but they do not explain every case. Testicular conditions, pituitary disorders, chronic illness, and medications can also affect hormone production.

For men over 50, the habits that support healthy testosterone often support the same goals that matter most for long-term independence: maintaining strength, protecting bone health, improving mobility, and recovering well after activity. Still, symptoms such as fatigue, reduced libido, erectile changes, loss of muscle, or declining performance should not automatically be blamed on lifestyle or aging.

A single low test result also cannot identify the cause. Testosterone changes with sleep quality, illness, calorie intake, stress, and the time of day. Clinicians generally confirm low testosterone with repeated early-morning testing and may order additional tests to assess the testicles, pituitary gland, thyroid, blood counts, and metabolic health.

The most useful approach is to address modifiable habits while arranging a medical evaluation when symptoms persist. Avoid extreme diets, unregulated supplements, and self-prescribed hormones. A personalized plan can help identify which factors are affecting your health and which changes are safe for your current fitness level and medical history.

### Excess body fat, insulin resistance, and metabolic health

Excess body fat, especially around the abdomen, is associated with lower testosterone in many men. Fat tissue can increase the conversion of testosterone into estradiol, while obesity may reduce the pituitary signals that tell the testicles to produce testosterone. Insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes can further disrupt this hormone system.

The relationship can become self-reinforcing. Lower testosterone may contribute to reduced muscle mass, lower energy, and increased fat gain. At the same time, metabolic problems can make it harder to maintain healthy hormone levels. [Harvard Health explains how obesity, diabetes, and metabolic health can affect testosterone](https://www.health.harvard.edu/mens-health/why-testosterone-levels-drop-and-when-to-consider-treatment).

Improving body composition does not require extreme dieting. Gradual weight loss, resistance training, regular walking, adequate sleep, and treatment for diabetes or sleep apnea can support broader health and may improve hormone function.

### Poor sleep, sleep deprivation, and obstructive sleep apnea

Testosterone production is closely tied to sleep. Levels typically rise during sleep, so consistently sleeping too little or following an irregular schedule may affect hormone production. Poor sleep can also reduce energy, concentration, exercise capacity, and sexual interest, making low testosterone symptoms feel more pronounced.

Obstructive sleep apnea deserves particular attention. Repeated interruptions in breathing can fragment sleep and reduce oxygen levels, even when you spend enough time in bed. Loud snoring, morning headaches, witnessed pauses in breathing, and daytime sleepiness are common reasons to ask about a sleep evaluation.

Aim for a regular sleep schedule and at least seven hours of sleep when possible. If you suspect sleep apnea, do not dismiss it as ordinary snoring. The [National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute explains sleep apnea symptoms and treatment](https://www.nhlbi.nih.gov/health/sleep-apnea), including when testing may be appropriate.

### Chronic stress and inadequate recovery

Short periods of stress are a normal part of life, but ongoing psychological or physical stress can affect the hormone signals involved in testosterone production. Chronic stress raises cortisol, which may interfere with the body’s reproductive hormone system. Poor recovery from work, caregiving, illness, or demanding exercise can add to this strain.

Stress may also affect testosterone indirectly. Men under prolonged pressure may sleep poorly, drink more alcohol, skip exercise, or rely on highly processed foods. These changes can affect metabolic health, body composition, mood, and physical performance.

Recovery does not require a complicated routine. Consistent sleep, planned rest days, moderate exercise, time outdoors, and support for anxiety or depression can help. If stress feels persistent or overwhelming, speak with a mental health professional or primary care clinician. The [American Institute of Stress offers resources for managing chronic stress](https://www.stress.org/).

### Physical inactivity, deconditioning, and muscle loss

Regular movement supports muscle mass, insulin sensitivity, cardiovascular health, mood, and physical function. In contrast, prolonged inactivity can contribute to weight gain, deconditioning, and muscle loss, which may be associated with lower testosterone. Reduced activity can also make everyday tasks more tiring and slow recovery after exertion.

Resistance training is especially useful for preserving strength as men age. A program might include safe, progressive exercises for the legs, hips, back, chest, shoulders, and core. Walking, cycling, swimming, and other aerobic activities can complement strength work.

Start at an appropriate level, particularly if you have joint pain, heart disease, neuropathy, or a long period of inactivity behind you. A physical therapist, exercise specialist, or clinician can help design a plan that improves function without aggravating an existing condition. The [Centers for Disease Control and Prevention provides adult activity guidelines](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/guidelines/adults.html).

### Excessive training without enough calories, protein, or rest

More exercise is not always better. High training loads combined with inadequate calories, insufficient protein, poor sleep, or too few rest days can place the body under significant stress. In some men, this may temporarily suppress reproductive hormone signals and contribute to fatigue, reduced performance, irritability, or lower sexual interest.

This pattern can occur in endurance athletes, people preparing for demanding events, or active adults who increase their training too quickly. It may also affect men who combine frequent workouts with aggressive calorie restriction.

Warning signs include persistent soreness, declining strength, repeated injuries, poor sleep, elevated resting heart rate, and a noticeable drop in motivation. Reducing training volume, eating enough to support activity, spacing demanding sessions, and prioritizing recovery may help. Persistent symptoms still require medical evaluation because they can also reflect anemia, thyroid disease, infection, or hypogonadism.

### Restrictive diets, rapid weight loss, and nutrient deficiencies

Severe calorie restriction and rapid weight loss can temporarily lower testosterone, particularly when the body does not receive enough energy to support normal reproductive function. Diets that exclude major food groups without careful planning may also lead to low intake of protein, healthy fats, zinc, vitamin D, or other nutrients important for health.

This does not mean that every low-carbohydrate, vegetarian, or weight-loss diet harms testosterone. The concern is inadequate nutrition, not one specific eating pattern. Men who are losing weight should aim for a gradual approach that includes sufficient protein, fiber-rich carbohydrates, vegetables, fruit, healthy fats, and fluids.

A clinician or registered dietitian can help identify nutritional gaps, especially after bariatric surgery, during illness, or when appetite is low. The [UNC Men’s Health Program discusses healthy eating, exercise, weight management, and alcohol moderation](https://www.med.unc.edu/urology/mens-health/2018/12/low-testosterone-male-hypogonadism/).

### Heavy alcohol use, nicotine, and recreational substances

Heavy alcohol use can damage Leydig cells, the testicular cells responsible for testosterone production. Alcohol may also worsen sleep, increase body fat, affect liver health, and make it harder to maintain consistent exercise and nutrition habits. Together, these effects can influence hormone health and physical performance.

Nicotine and recreational substances may affect circulation, sleep, mood, sexual function, and overall health. Some substances can interfere directly with hormone signaling, while others may contain unknown ingredients or be combined with drugs that carry additional risks. Anabolic steroid use is a separate concern because it can suppress the body’s own testosterone production after use ends.

Reducing alcohol and seeking support for substance use can benefit health even before hormone levels are checked. Do not abruptly stop heavy alcohol use without medical guidance, since withdrawal can be dangerous. Cleveland Clinic lists sleep, regular exercise, healthy weight management, and limited alcohol use among habits that support testosterone health.

### Practical habits that support healthy hormones and physical performance

A sustainable plan usually works better than a drastic overhaul. Focus on a few habits that support both hormone health and physical function:

* Maintain a healthy weight through gradual, realistic changes.
* Perform resistance training two or more days per week when medically appropriate.
* Add regular walking or another form of aerobic activity.
* Eat enough protein and include vegetables, fruit, whole grains, and healthy fats.
* Keep a consistent sleep schedule and address loud snoring or suspected sleep apnea.
* Limit alcohol and avoid recreational or performance-enhancing substances.
* Schedule recovery between demanding workouts.
* Manage diabetes, high blood pressure, thyroid disease, and other chronic conditions.
* Review medications with a clinician if symptoms begin after a prescription change.

These steps may support healthy testosterone, but they do not diagnose or treat testosterone deficiency on their own. If symptoms continue despite healthier routines, testing can help distinguish lifestyle-related changes from a medical hormone disorder. Do not start testosterone or stop a prescription without professional guidance, especially if fertility, prostate health, sleep apnea, or cardiovascular risk is a concern.

## Which Medical Conditions and Medications Lower Testosterone?

Low testosterone may result from a problem in the testicles, reduced signaling from the hypothalamus or pituitary gland, or a medical condition that affects the body more broadly. Certain medications can also interfere with testosterone production. For men over 50, several factors often overlap, including insulin resistance, poor sleep, chronic pain, weight gain, illness, and long-term prescription use.

A low testosterone result does not reveal the cause on its own. Your clinician may review your symptoms, medical history, medication list, sleep quality, alcohol use, physical activity, and metabolic health. The [Endocrine Society’s clinical guideline](https://www.endocrine.org/clinical-practice-guidelines/testosterone-therapy) recommends confirming low testosterone with consistent symptoms and repeated early-morning testing before discussing treatment.

The conditions below may reduce testosterone directly or disrupt the signals that support its production. They can also cause similar symptoms, such as fatigue, reduced strength, low motivation, erectile changes, and slower recovery. Identifying the underlying cause helps your care team choose the safest and most useful treatment.

### Type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and related hormone changes

Type 2 diabetes and metabolic syndrome are closely associated with low testosterone. Insulin resistance, increased abdominal fat, high blood pressure, and abnormal cholesterol can affect hormone regulation. Excess body fat may reduce the brain signals that stimulate testosterone production. It may also increase the conversion of testosterone into estradiol, a form of estrogen.

Men with diabetes may experience lower testosterone along with reduced libido, erectile dysfunction, fatigue, and loss of strength. These symptoms can also result from nerve damage, vascular disease, sleep apnea, or medication effects. Harvard Health explains how [obesity, insulin resistance, and diabetes affect testosterone](https://www.health.harvard.edu/mens-health/why-testosterone-levels-drop-and-when-to-consider-treatment). Improving blood sugar control, sleep, physical activity, and body composition may support overall hormone health, but these steps should accompany medical evaluation when symptoms persist.

### Chronic kidney disease, liver disease, and inflammation

The kidneys and liver help regulate metabolism, medication breakdown, fluid balance, and hormone activity. Chronic kidney disease may disrupt testosterone production and alter the proteins that carry testosterone through the bloodstream. Advanced liver disease can also affect hormone metabolism and change the balance between testosterone and estradiol.

Long-term inflammation may interfere with communication between the hypothalamus, pituitary gland, and testicles. Conditions such as sarcoidosis and other inflammatory disorders can affect these hormone signals. Chronic illness may also reduce appetite, activity, sleep quality, and muscle mass, which can add to fatigue and weakness.

The Cleveland Clinic’s overview of male hypogonadism lists kidney disease, liver cirrhosis, inflammatory disease, and other systemic illnesses as possible contributors. Men with kidney or liver disease may need careful monitoring because hormone treatment can interact with other health risks and medications.

### HIV, cancer, chronic pain, and other systemic illnesses

HIV and other serious infections may affect testosterone through inflammation, changes in body composition, nutritional deficiencies, and altered pituitary signaling. Some men with HIV also develop testicular or pituitary problems. Others have several contributing factors at once, which makes a complete medical evaluation especially important.

Cancer may lower testosterone when it affects the testicles, pituitary region, or other organs involved in hormone regulation. Chemotherapy, radiation, major surgery, and prolonged illness can also reduce testosterone production. Chronic pain adds another layer because persistent stress, poor sleep, reduced activity, and some pain medicines may affect hormone levels.

Low energy and reduced physical performance should not automatically be blamed on testosterone deficiency. Blood counts, thyroid testing, nutritional assessment, medication review, and hormone testing may help identify the cause. The [Atlantic Urology Specialists guide to low testosterone causes](https://atlanticurologyclinics.com/blog/9-causes-of-low-t/) discusses how systemic illness and chronic disease may contribute.

### Thyroid disorders and other endocrine conditions

The thyroid, adrenal glands, pituitary gland, and testicles operate within a connected hormone system. An underactive thyroid may contribute to low testosterone and can cause fatigue, depressed mood, weight changes, reduced exercise tolerance, and sexual symptoms. Because these complaints overlap with testosterone deficiency, thyroid testing is often part of a broader evaluation.

High prolactin, adrenal disorders, and pituitary disease may also reduce the signals that stimulate testosterone production. Some endocrine conditions affect both testosterone and fertility by changing luteinizing hormone and follicle-stimulating hormone levels. Treating the underlying endocrine problem may improve testosterone without testosterone replacement.

Depending on your symptoms and history, testing may include thyroid-stimulating hormone, free thyroxine, prolactin, luteinizing hormone, and follicle-stimulating hormone. Harvard Health explains how [hypothyroidism may contribute to low testosterone](https://www.health.harvard.edu/mens-health/why-testosterone-levels-drop-and-when-to-consider-treatment), but blood testing is needed to identify the specific problem.

### Opioid pain medications and testosterone suppression

Opioids can reduce the brain signals that stimulate testosterone production. This effect is called opioid-induced androgen deficiency. It may occur with long-term use of oxycodone, hydrocodone, morphine, fentanyl, and other opioid medicines. Risk varies according to the medication, dose, treatment duration, and individual health.

Possible symptoms include low libido, erectile changes, fatigue, depressed mood, reduced muscle mass, and slower recovery. Chronic pain itself can cause many of the same complaints, so evaluation should consider both the condition and the medication. Opioids may also worsen sleep-disordered breathing, which can further affect energy and hormone regulation.

Never reduce or stop an opioid suddenly. Ask the prescribing clinician whether the medicine remains necessary, whether the dose can be adjusted safely, and whether other pain treatments may be appropriate. The Cleveland Clinic explains how illness and medication use can contribute to male hypogonadism.

### Corticosteroids and other hormone-affecting medications

Corticosteroids, including prednisone, can affect the hypothalamus and pituitary signals involved in testosterone production. Risk depends on the dose, treatment duration, route of administration, and overall health. Long-term or high-dose use may also contribute to muscle weakness, body composition changes, poor sleep, mood changes, and reduced exercise capacity.

Other medicines may affect testosterone or create similar symptoms. Certain antidepressants, antipsychotics, blood pressure medicines, chemotherapy drugs, and hormone treatments may influence libido, erections, energy, or reproductive hormones. A medication can contribute to symptoms even when testosterone remains within the laboratory range.

A medication review should include prescriptions, over-the-counter products, injections, topical medicines, and supplements. The [UNC Men’s Health Program discusses corticosteroids and other possible causes of low testosterone](https://www.med.unc.edu/urology/mens-health/2018/12/low-testosterone-male-hypogonadism/). Do not assume a medication is responsible until testing and clinical review support that conclusion.

### Anabolic steroid use and testosterone withdrawal

Anabolic steroids and non-prescribed testosterone products can reduce the body’s natural testosterone production. When external androgens enter the body, the brain may release less luteinizing hormone and follicle-stimulating hormone. The testicles then receive less stimulation and may produce less testosterone and sperm.

After anabolic steroid use ends, some men experience a period of low testosterone. Symptoms may include low libido, erectile dysfunction, fatigue, depressed mood, irritability, reduced strength, and difficulty maintaining muscle. Testicular size and fertility may also change. Recovery varies according to the substances used, dose, duration, and individual health.

Tell your clinician about anabolic steroid use or non-prescribed hormone products. This information helps distinguish withdrawal from testicular disease, pituitary dysfunction, or another condition. Avoid trying to correct the problem with additional hormones or online “post-cycle” products. These products may delay proper care and create additional risks.

### Why you should not stop prescriptions without medical guidance

A medication may contribute to low testosterone, but stopping it without a medical plan can cause more harm than the original side effect. Abruptly stopping corticosteroids can lead to adrenal insufficiency. Suddenly discontinuing opioids may trigger withdrawal and make pain difficult to control. Stopping antidepressants, blood pressure medicines, or other long-term treatments may also cause significant symptoms or allow an underlying condition to worsen.

Ask the prescribing clinician to review the medication, dose, timing, and alternatives. The safest plan may involve a gradual dose reduction, a different medicine, treatment of an underlying condition, or follow-up testosterone testing after a change. In some cases, the medication provides important health benefits that outweigh its possible effect on hormone levels.

Bring an updated medication list to your appointment, including supplements and non-prescribed substances. [Guidance from Atlantic Urology Specialists](https://atlanticurologyclinics.com/blog/9-causes-of-low-t/) also advises patients to speak with a doctor before changing or stopping a medication.

### Medication-related suppression versus testicular or pituitary disease

Medication-related suppression often reduces the brain signals that tell the testicles to produce testosterone. In this situation, luteinizing hormone and follicle-stimulating hormone may be low or inappropriately normal. Testicular disease may cause testosterone to fall while these signals rise because the brain is trying to stimulate the testicles more strongly.

Clinicians use this pattern, along with symptoms and medical history, to distinguish secondary hypogonadism from primary testicular failure. Testing may include repeat morning testosterone, luteinizing hormone, follicle-stimulating hormone, prolactin, thyroid markers, blood counts, and metabolic tests. Further evaluation may be appropriate after testicular injury, infertility, head trauma, severe headaches, or vision changes.

The Cleveland Clinic’s diagnostic information explains that low testosterone may have testicular, pituitary, medication-related, or systemic causes. Identifying the source matters because treatment may involve changing a medication, managing an illness, addressing pituitary disease, protecting fertility, or considering testosterone therapy after reviewing its risks and benefits.

## How Do Clinicians Diagnose and Treat Low Testosterone?

Diagnosing low testosterone takes more than checking one number on a lab report. Clinicians look for a consistent pattern that includes ongoing symptoms, repeatedly low testosterone levels, and clues about what may be affecting hormone production. This matters because fatigue, reduced strength, low mood, erectile changes, and slower recovery can also result from sleep apnea, thyroid disease, depression, medication effects, chronic illness, or inadequate nutrition.

A thorough evaluation helps distinguish hypogonadism from a temporary drop in testosterone. It also helps identify whether the testicles, pituitary gland, medications, sleep, or another health condition may be involved. For some men, treating sleep apnea, diabetes, medication effects, or significant weight changes may improve hormone health without testosterone therapy.

For others, clinician-guided testosterone replacement may be appropriate after reviewing the potential benefits, risks, fertility goals, and long-term monitoring requirements. The Cleveland Clinic’s overview of male hypogonadism explains why both symptoms and laboratory testing are needed.

### Which symptoms warrant testing?

Testing may be appropriate when symptoms persist, interfere with daily life, or appear in combination. Common concerns include reduced sex drive, erectile dysfunction, fewer spontaneous erections, fatigue, reduced muscle strength, and mood changes. Some men also notice less motivation, trouble concentrating, slower recovery after exercise, or a decline in physical performance.

One symptom alone does not prove that testosterone is low. However, persistent sexual symptoms combined with low energy, loss of strength, or changes in body composition deserve medical attention. Men who notice a meaningful decline in function should discuss it with a clinician rather than assuming it is simply a normal part of aging. The [Endocrine Society’s patient guidance](https://www.endocrine.org/patient-engagement/endocrine-library/hypogonadism) outlines symptoms commonly associated with testosterone deficiency.

### Reviewing symptoms, health history, medications, sleep, alcohol use, and physical performance

A clinician will ask when symptoms began, whether they are getting worse, and how they affect relationships, exercise, work, and daily activities. The discussion may cover libido, erections, sleep quality, energy, strength, body weight, mood, and recovery after physical activity.

Your health history also matters. Be prepared to discuss prescriptions, over-the-counter medicines, supplements, alcohol use, nicotine, recreational substances, prior injuries, surgeries, infections, and chronic conditions. Sleep problems, particularly loud snoring or witnessed pauses in breathing, can affect energy and hormone health. A review of physical performance can help separate a hormone concern from deconditioning, pain, or another medical issue.

### Examining body composition, testicular size, body hair, and breast changes

A physical examination can provide clues about the possible cause of low testosterone. The clinician may assess muscle mass, body fat distribution, body hair, testicular size, and breast tissue. They may also check blood pressure, examine the thyroid, look for signs of chronic disease, and evaluate your overall physical condition.

These findings do not confirm a diagnosis by themselves. They help place laboratory results in context. Reduced body hair or smaller testicles may suggest a longer-standing hormone problem, while breast tenderness or enlargement may lead to additional testing. Harvard Health explains how an examination can identify signs associated with testosterone changes in its guide to [testosterone levels and treatment](https://www.health.harvard.edu/mens-health/why-testosterone-levels-drop-and-when-to-consider-treatment).

### Confirming results with two early-morning total testosterone tests

Testosterone levels change during the day and are generally highest in the morning. Clinicians typically order an early-morning blood test, often between 7 and 10 a.m. If the result is low, testing is usually repeated on a different morning before a diagnosis is made.

Many clinicians use a total testosterone level below 300 nanograms per deciliter as a general reference point, but laboratory ranges and testing methods vary. The result must be considered alongside symptoms, age, health status, and other findings. The [American Urological Association guideline](https://www.auanet.org/guidelines-and-quality/guidelines/testosterone-deficiency-guideline) supports using two early-morning measurements because one low result may not reflect your usual level.

### Using free testosterone and SHBG when total testosterone is borderline

Total testosterone measures testosterone in the blood, including testosterone attached to proteins and the smaller amount available to tissues. When the total result is near the lower end of the laboratory range, free testosterone may provide more information. Free testosterone refers to the portion that is not tightly bound to proteins.

Sex hormone-binding globulin, or SHBG, affects how much testosterone remains available. Age, body weight, liver health, thyroid conditions, and other factors can change SHBG levels. When total testosterone and symptoms do not clearly match, a clinician may order SHBG and free testosterone tests. The Cleveland Clinic explains additional hormone testing when total testosterone results are borderline.

### Checking LH and FSH to identify testicular or pituitary causes

Luteinizing hormone, or LH, signals the testicles to produce testosterone. Follicle-stimulating hormone, or FSH, supports sperm production. Measuring these hormones can help show whether low testosterone begins in the testicles or in the hypothalamus and pituitary gland, which coordinate reproductive hormone signals.

Low testosterone with high LH and FSH may point toward a testicular problem. Low or inappropriately normal LH and FSH may suggest reduced signaling from the pituitary or hypothalamus. This distinction guides further testing and treatment. It can also provide important information about fertility, especially for men who may want biological children in the future.

### Evaluating prolactin, thyroid function, iron, blood counts, and metabolic markers

Additional blood tests can identify conditions that cause similar symptoms or interfere with hormone production. Prolactin may be checked when pituitary disease is possible, particularly if low testosterone occurs with erectile problems, breast discharge, headaches, or vision changes.

Thyroid testing can identify an underactive or overactive thyroid. Iron studies may help detect hemochromatosis, which can affect the pituitary and testicles. A complete blood count, glucose or A1C, cholesterol, liver markers, and kidney function tests provide a broader view of health. These results may reveal anemia, diabetes, metabolic disease, or organ problems contributing to fatigue and reduced performance. Harvard Health reviews several possible evaluations in its discussion of [why testosterone levels fall](https://www.health.harvard.edu/mens-health/why-testosterone-levels-drop-and-when-to-consider-treatment).

### Ordering pituitary or testicular imaging when signs indicate it

Imaging is not necessary for every man with low testosterone. A clinician may recommend it when the medical history, physical examination, or blood tests suggest a structural problem. Pituitary imaging may be considered when testosterone is very low, LH and FSH are unusually low, prolactin is elevated, or symptoms include severe headaches or vision changes.

Testicular ultrasound may be appropriate for a lump, swelling, pain, an abnormal examination, or concern about a tumor or other local problem. Imaging decisions should be based on specific findings rather than used routinely for everyone. The [UNC Men’s Health Program explains when imaging may be considered](https://www.med.unc.edu/urology/mens-health/2018/12/low-testosterone-male-hypogonadism/) during an evaluation.

### Accounting for illness, poor sleep, medications, and testing time

A temporary illness can lower testosterone, so tell your clinician if you were sick when the blood sample was taken. Acute infection, major physical stress, poor sleep, inadequate calorie intake, and excessive training can also affect results. Testing after a sleepless night or during severe pain may not represent your usual hormone status.

Medication timing and existing health conditions matter as well. Opioids, corticosteroids, anabolic steroid use, and some other treatments can suppress testosterone production. Untreated sleep apnea, poorly controlled diabetes, and significant weight changes may also affect hormone levels. If a result seems inconsistent with your symptoms, your clinician may repeat testing after temporary factors have been addressed.

### When should men seek professional evaluation?

Consider an evaluation when fatigue, low libido, erectile problems, reduced strength, declining exercise capacity, or mood changes continue for several weeks or begin affecting your independence and quality of life. A clinician can determine whether testosterone testing makes sense and check for other causes that may be treatable.

An assessment is particularly important when several symptoms appear together, when you have a history of testicular or pituitary disease, or when you take medicines known to affect hormone production. Avoid using testosterone obtained without medical supervision. Proper testing helps prevent treatment of the wrong condition and provides a baseline for follow-up. Men with persistent symptoms should seek professional care, as discussed in this [clinical review of low testosterone symptoms](https://drhowardaubert.com/posts/low-testosterone-in-young-men/).

### Urgent signs: sudden testicular pain, severe headache, vision changes, or nipple discharge

Some symptoms require prompt medical attention rather than a routine hormone appointment. Sudden or severe testicular pain can signal testicular torsion, an emergency that needs immediate care. A new testicular lump or rapidly developing swelling also requires timely evaluation.

Severe headache, new vision changes, or nipple discharge may point to a pituitary or another condition requiring prompt assessment. Do not assume these symptoms are caused by low testosterone. Seek emergency care for sudden severe pain, sudden vision loss, confusion, or other rapidly developing symptoms. The Cleveland Clinic lists these warning signs among symptoms that warrant urgent attention.

### Treating underlying causes before or alongside testosterone therapy

Treatment depends on the cause, symptom severity, test results, age, fertility plans, and overall health. If obesity, sleep apnea, diabetes, thyroid disease, poor nutrition, medication effects, or heavy alcohol use contributes to low testosterone, addressing that factor may improve symptoms and hormone function.

Treatment may include improving sleep, managing blood sugar, adjusting a medication with the prescribing clinician, treating sleep apnea, reducing alcohol, or following a structured strength and conditioning plan. These steps can support energy, muscle function, mobility, and recovery whether or not testosterone therapy is prescribed. The [UNC Men’s Health Program notes that conditions such as sleep apnea and diabetes may need treatment](https://www.med.unc.edu/urology/mens-health/2018/12/low-testosterone-male-hypogonadism/) before or alongside testosterone therapy.

### Injections, gels, patches, pellets, nasal gel, and other clinician-guided options

When symptoms and repeated testing support testosterone deficiency, testosterone replacement therapy may be one treatment option. Available forms include injections, topical gels, patches, pellets placed under the skin, and nasal gel. Each option has different dosing schedules, absorption patterns, side effects, costs, and practical considerations.

Injections may cause noticeable changes in testosterone levels between doses. Gels require careful application and precautions to prevent transfer to another person. Pellets provide longer-lasting delivery but require a minor procedure. Your clinician can explain which option fits your health needs, preferences, and ability to follow monitoring instructions. Harvard Health reviews several [testosterone replacement options](https://www.health.harvard.edu/mens-health/why-testosterone-levels-drop-and-when-to-consider-treatment).

### Considering fertility because testosterone therapy can reduce sperm production

Discuss fertility before starting testosterone therapy, even if having children is not an immediate goal. Testosterone supplied from outside the body can reduce the brain’s signals to the testicles, which may lower sperm production. Some men experience a significant decline in sperm count while using therapy.

If you want to preserve fertility, tell your clinician before treatment begins. A reproductive specialist may discuss alternatives or fertility-preserving approaches based on your goals. Do not assume testosterone replacement will improve fertility. In some men, it can work against that goal. Family planning should be part of the initial treatment discussion, as outlined in this [clinical review of testosterone and sperm production](https://drhowardaubert.com/posts/low-testosterone-in-young-men/).

### Monitoring testosterone, hematocrit, prostate health, cardiovascular risk, sleep apnea, and symptoms

Follow-up is an important part of treatment. Clinicians may recheck testosterone levels to confirm that the dose is appropriate and monitor hematocrit, a measure of red blood cell concentration. They may also assess prostate health according to your age, risk factors, and current screening recommendations.

Appointments should include a review of symptoms, sleep, urinary changes, blood pressure, cardiovascular health, side effects, and physical response. Men with untreated or poorly controlled sleep apnea may need additional evaluation because testosterone therapy can complicate breathing-related sleep problems. Regular monitoring allows the treatment plan to be adjusted or stopped when the risks outweigh the benefits. Harvard Health describes the role of [ongoing monitoring during testosterone therapy](https://www.health.harvard.edu/mens-health/why-testosterone-levels-drop-and-when-to-consider-treatment).

## Frequently Asked Questions

**How is low testosterone diagnosed in men?**\
Diagnosis usually requires related symptoms and at least two low early-morning testosterone results taken on separate days. Your clinician may also check free testosterone, SHBG, LH, FSH, prolactin, thyroid function, blood counts, and metabolic markers to identify the cause.

**Can low testosterone be caused by a health condition or medication?**\
Yes. Sleep apnea, obesity, Type 2 diabetes, thyroid disease, chronic kidney or liver disease, pituitary disorders, chronic illness, opioids, corticosteroids, and anabolic steroids may affect testosterone levels. Treating the underlying issue may improve symptoms and hormone function.

**What are the most common symptoms of low testosterone?**\
Symptoms may include reduced libido, erectile changes, fewer spontaneous erections, fatigue, loss of strength or muscle, increased body fat, low mood, poor concentration, reduced body hair, and slower recovery. These concerns can also result from other medical conditions, so testing is important.

**Can lifestyle changes improve testosterone levels?**\
Healthy habits may support hormone health and physical performance. Focus on consistent sleep, resistance training, regular movement, adequate protein and calories, gradual weight management, limited alcohol, and treatment for conditions such as sleep apnea or diabetes. Persistent symptoms still warrant medical evaluation.

**Does testosterone therapy affect fertility?**\
It can. Testosterone therapy may reduce the signals that stimulate sperm production, which can lower sperm count. Discuss fertility plans before treatment begins, even if starting a family is not an immediate goal. A specialist may recommend other options when preserving fertility matters.
