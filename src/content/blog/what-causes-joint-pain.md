People ask what causes joint pain when a knee, hip, hand, or shoulder starts to ache during ordinary movement. The same question can have more than one answer. A joint is simply a place where two bones meet, and pain there can come from the joint itself or from tissue around it. This guide explains common factors and the questions a comprehensive evaluation may consider. It does not diagnose you or tell you which care is right.

## What causes joint pain is not one diagnosis

Joint pain is a symptom, not a single condition. The National Institute of Arthritis and Musculoskeletal and Skin Diseases (NIAMS) notes that the word arthritis means joint inflammation, but people also use it for many disorders that affect joints. There are more than 100 types of arthritis. Some stay in the joints. Others can also affect organs such as the eyes, heart, or skin.

That range is why location alone is a weak clue. Pain in one knee after a twist is a different story from stiffness in both hands that lasts for weeks. Pain after a long walk is different from pain with fever, redness, and sudden swelling. A useful first step is to describe the pattern, not to name a disease from a web page.

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) describes arthritis as a broad term for conditions that affect joints and the tissues around them. The CDC also says there is no cure for arthritis, while appropriate care may help some people reduce pain and stay active. Any plan still has to match the person, the joint, and the findings from an exam.

## How a painful joint is put together

A joint is more than two bone ends. Cartilage covers those ends so they can glide. A thin lining, the synovium, helps make fluid that cushions movement. Ligaments hold the joint steady. Tendons connect muscle to bone. In the knee, the meniscus adds another cushion. Nerves around the joint carry pain signals.

NIAMS explains that osteoarthritis can involve all of these areas, not only worn cartilage. As joint tissues change, pain, swelling, and loss of motion can develop. If pain leads someone to move less, nearby muscles can weaken. Weaker muscles may then place more stress on the joint. That loop is one reason an evaluation looks at strength and daily movement, not only at an X-ray.

Pain can also start outside the joint space. Bursitis involves a small fluid-filled cushion near a joint. Tendinitis involves a tendon. A strain or sprain involves muscle or ligament. Those problems can feel like joint pain even when the joint surfaces are not the main issue. Telling them apart usually takes a history and a hands-on exam.

## Common factors behind joint pain

No list can cover every cause. These are factors clinicians often sort through when an adult asks what is driving the pain. More than one can be present at the same time.

### Osteoarthritis and gradual tissue change

Osteoarthritis is the most common type of arthritis and is more common in older adults. NIAMS describes it as a disease in which joint tissues break down over time. It often affects the hands, knees, hips, neck, and lower back. Symptoms often start slowly. Pain may be worse with use. Stiffness after rest is often brief. Some people also notice swelling after activity or a feeling that the joint is less steady.

Researchers do not know the single trigger that starts that breakdown. Risk factors named by NIAMS include older age, extra body weight, a past joint injury or surgery, repeated joint use, a joint that formed differently, and family history. Osteoarthritis can follow an old injury years later. A badly injured knee in younger life is one example NIAMS gives.

The effect on daily life varies. For some people it stays mild. For others it limits walking, stairs, sleep, or the activities they value. Joint change usually develops over years, though it can worsen faster in some people. An image of arthritis does not, by itself, explain how much pain a person feels.

### Inflammatory and crystal-related arthritis

Some joint pain comes from ongoing inflammation rather than gradual wear. NIAMS describes rheumatoid arthritis as a problem in which the body's defense system does not work as it should. It often affects joints in the hands and feet and can also affect internal organs. People may feel tired or feverish, not only sore.

Other inflammatory patterns have their own clues. Psoriatic arthritis can occur in people who have psoriasis. Ankylosing spondylitis often involves the spine and the joint where the lower spine meets the pelvis. Reactive arthritis is pain or swelling linked with an infection elsewhere in the body. Gout comes from crystals that build up in a joint and often starts in the big toe, though other joints can be involved.

These are descriptions, not a checklist you can complete at home. Inflammatory pain sometimes feels worse after rest and may come with prolonged morning stiffness, warmth, or swelling. Only a clinician can tell whether that pattern fits, and blood tests or imaging are not interpreted in isolation.

### Injury, overuse, and tissues around the joint

A fall, a twist, or a sudden load can sprain a ligament, strain a muscle, or irritate a tendon. Pain that starts at a clear moment, with swelling or a joint that will not bear weight, needs prompt attention. Pain that builds after a new sport, a home project, or a change in work tasks may point more toward overload.

Repeated use does not mean a person should stop moving. It means the current dose of activity may be more than that joint and the muscles around it can tolerate right now. MedlinePlus lists bursitis, tendinitis, sprains, strains, and overuse among reasons for joint pain. Resting in a way your clinician recommends, then returning to movement in a graded way, is a common topic in rehabilitation. The right pace is individual.

### Infection and whole-body illness

An infection inside a joint is uncommon and urgent. NIAMS notes that infection can enter a joint and damage the cushion between the bones. Fever with a hot, very swollen joint is a reason to seek care the same day, especially after a recent procedure, an open wound, or a known infection elsewhere.

Other illnesses can show up as joint pain too. MedlinePlus and Mayo Clinic list examples such as lupus, viral illness, and thyroid disease among many possibilities. Joint pain can also appear with conditions that are not primarily joint diseases, including fibromyalgia. A website cannot sort those apart. The point of naming them is to show why a narrow guess is risky.

### Age, weight, hormones, and daily load

Joints change with age, but age is not a diagnosis. Many adults stay active with joint changes that never become their main limit. Extra body weight can add load to hips and knees and is a recognized risk factor for osteoarthritis. It is not the only factor, and it is not a moral judgment. It is one piece an evaluation may discuss alongside strength, sleep, and other health conditions.

Hormones can matter for some adults. Low testosterone is one pathway that has its own questions and is covered separately in our guide to [low testosterone and joint pain](https://www.sigmacoremedicalcenter.com/blog/low-testosterone-joint-pain-treatment). That article is not a substitute for asking what else could be involved. Thyroid disease and inflammatory arthritis can also overlap with fatigue and stiffness.

Daily load includes work, hobbies, stairs, and time spent in one position. A joint that hurts only during a specific task may be reacting to how that task is done. A joint that hurts at rest, at night, or in several places may need a broader look. Writing down when the pain shows up is often more useful than guessing a single cause.

## One joint or many joints

Clinicians often start by asking how many joints hurt and whether they are on one side or both. Pain in a single joint after an injury leans toward a local problem, such as a sprain, a cartilage injury, or irritation of a nearby tendon or bursa. Pain that jumps from joint to joint, or that appears in matching joints on both sides, raises different questions, including inflammatory arthritis or a whole-body illness.

The joint involved also shapes the conversation. Base-of-thumb or finger pain is a common osteoarthritis pattern. A hot, swollen big toe is a classic gout story, though it is not the only one. Hip or knee pain that grows with walking is often discussed in the context of osteoarthritis, but bursitis, tendon problems, and referred pain from the back can mimic it. Spine-related pain, including nerve irritation, can be felt in a leg or arm and still not start in that limb's joint.

## Questions a comprehensive evaluation may consider

An evaluation is a conversation plus an exam, not a single test. The CDC notes that clinicians may use your history, a physical exam, imaging, and lab tests, and that the mix depends on the suspected type of arthritis. Bring observations. Leave the diagnosis to the clinician who can examine you.

These are questions you can expect, or can ask, during a visit:

1. When did the pain start, and did a specific injury or illness come first?
2. Which joints are involved, and are the same joints affected on both sides of the body?
3. Is stiffness brief after sitting, or does it last more than an hour in the morning?
4. Is the joint swollen, warm, red, or unstable, and have you had a fever?
5. What activities, jobs, or sports load the joint, and what have you already changed?
6. What other conditions, medicines, or family history might be relevant?
7. What can you no longer do that you want to get back, such as walking, lifting, or sleeping through the night?
8. Which findings would change the plan, and when would a referral be appropriate?

Imaging and lab tests answer narrow questions. An X-ray may show joint-space change and still miss a tendon problem. A blood test may support or argue against an inflammatory disease and still not explain today's pain. Ask what a test is meant to decide. Also ask what will happen if the result is normal.

## When joint pain needs prompt care

Joint pain is rarely an emergency, and mild pain is often reviewed at a routine visit. Still, some patterns should not wait for a convenient appointment. Seek care right away if an injury leaves the joint looking out of shape, you cannot use the joint, the pain is severe, or swelling appears suddenly.

Make a prompt appointment if joint pain comes with swelling, redness, tenderness and warmth, or fever. A single joint that is hot and you also feel ill can be a sign of infection. Sudden weakness, numbness, or trouble controlling the bowel or bladder is a separate emergency and is not a routine joint problem.

## What this overview does not decide

Knowing common factors is not the same as knowing your factor. Two people with similar knee pain can have different findings and different reasonable next steps. Guidelines and clinic pages also cannot rank treatments for you. Options such as activity changes, physical therapy, medicines, injections, and surgery each have tradeoffs that belong in a visit.

Be careful with claims that a product or procedure cures arthritis, reverses joint damage, or works for a set percentage of people. The CDC states there is no cure for arthritis. NIAMS says current treatments allow most people with arthritis to lead active lives, which is a different statement from a promise that pain will stop. Ask any clinic, including this one, what is known, what is uncertain, and what happens if you choose to wait.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the most common cause of joint pain?

Arthritis is the most common cause, and osteoarthritis is the most common type of arthritis. That does not mean every ache is osteoarthritis. Injury, bursitis, tendinitis, gout, inflammatory arthritis, and infection are among the other explanations a clinician may need to rule in or out.

### Can joint pain be caused by something other than arthritis?

Yes. Pain can come from a tendon, a bursa, a ligament, a muscle, or a nerve, even when it feels as if it is inside the joint. Whole-body illnesses can also show up as joint pain. An exam is what separates those patterns.

### Why do several joints hurt at once?

Pain in many joints can be a feature of inflammatory arthritis, a crystal disease, a reaction to infection, or another whole-body condition. It can also be more than one local problem at the same time. The number of joints is a clue, not a diagnosis.

### Does joint pain always show up on an X-ray?

No. Early symptoms, tendon problems, and some inflammatory conditions may not match what an X-ray shows. NIAMS also notes that osteoarthritis can affect cartilage, bone, lining, ligaments, and the knee meniscus, so one picture rarely tells the whole story.

### When should I stop waiting and get joint pain checked?

Get urgent care if the joint looks deformed, you cannot use it, pain is severe, or swelling is sudden. Book a prompt visit for warmth, redness, swelling, or fever. For pain that is changing how you walk, work, or sleep, a routine evaluation is a reasonable next step even when none of those warning signs are present.
