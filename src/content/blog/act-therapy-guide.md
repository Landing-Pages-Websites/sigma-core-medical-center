Wanting to stay active while living with chronic pain can bring up tough questions: Should you keep going, change the plan, or rest? ACT therapy can help you approach those moments thoughtfully. Acceptance and Commitment Therapy teaches skills for noticing thoughts and feelings without automatically letting them dictate your next move. It doesn’t promise pain relief or encourage you to push through warning signs. Instead, it can support values-based choices that fit your circumstances and medical guidance. Read on to learn how ACT works, what its benefits and limits may be, and how to explore whether it’s a good fit.

## Key Takeaways

* **ACT supports more flexible responses to challenges**: Practice noticing difficult thoughts and feelings, then choose a considered next step.
* **Let personal values guide realistic goals**: Identify what matters to you and find manageable ways to stay active, connected, and independent.
* **Make therapy part of coordinated care**: Ask a qualified therapist how ACT may fit your needs, how progress will be reviewed, and how it can complement medical treatment.

## What Is ACT Therapy?

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, or ACT, is a form of psychotherapy that helps people respond to difficult thoughts, emotions, and sensations while continuing to take meaningful steps in life. For adults managing chronic pain or changes in mobility, it may offer ways to stay connected to personal goals alongside appropriate medical care.

### What does ACT mean in plain language?

“Acceptance” means making room for thoughts and feelings rather than using all your energy to push them away. “Commitment” means taking practical steps toward what matters to you, even when those steps feel challenging. ACT does not ask you to like pain, give up on treatment, or pretend that a health concern is easy. Instead, it can help you consider how to respond in a way that supports your priorities. For example, someone with joint pain might explore manageable ways to take part in a favorite activity, with guidance from their care team as needed. [Psychology Today’s overview of ACT](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/therapy-types/acceptance-and-commitment-therapy) describes this connection between accepting inner experiences and making positive behavior changes.

### Build psychological flexibility

ACT aims to build psychological flexibility: the ability to notice thoughts and feelings, then choose how to respond rather than reacting automatically. This can be useful when pain, worry, or frustration makes it harder to follow through on daily activities. Flexibility does not mean ignoring symptoms or pushing through without limits. It means pausing to consider what matters and choosing a safe, helpful next step. Someone recovering from an injury, for instance, might acknowledge frustration while following their clinician’s activity guidance and making time for valued connections. [HopeWay explains psychological flexibility](https://hopeway.org/psychotherapies/acceptance-and-commitment-therapy-act) as responding to thoughts and feelings while acting in ways that fit your values.

### Accept without resigning, approving, or giving up

Acceptance in ACT means recognizing what you are experiencing in the present, not approving of it or deciding nothing can change. You can acknowledge that pain is affecting your day and still seek an evaluation, discuss treatment options, or adjust an activity to make it more manageable. ACT does not promise to remove every difficult thought or feeling. It helps people change how they respond to those experiences and make choices that reflect their values. This approach can sit alongside medical treatment, not take its place. [GoodTherapy’s guide to ACT](https://www.goodtherapy.org/learn-about-therapy/types/acceptance-commitment-therapy) explains how rigid responses to difficult thoughts and feelings can interfere with well-being and valued actions.

## How Does ACT Build Psychological Flexibility?

Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) builds psychological flexibility through six connected processes: acceptance, cognitive defusion, present-moment awareness, self-as-context, values clarification, and committed action. Together, these skills can help people notice what they’re thinking and feeling, then choose how to respond instead of reacting automatically or avoiding every difficult experience.

Psychological flexibility doesn’t mean feeling positive all the time or being able to control every thought, emotion, or sensation. It means making room for what’s happening while staying connected to what matters. For someone managing chronic pain, for example, that could mean recognizing worry about an activity, checking in with their body, and deciding whether to adjust the plan, seek support, or take a manageable next step.

ACT doesn’t ask people to ignore pain, push past physical limits, or stop seeking medical care. The approach can help them respond to difficult experiences while continuing to work toward personally meaningful goals. A therapist may introduce the processes through conversation, mindfulness, and practical exercises, adapting the work to each person’s health, preferences, and circumstances. [HopeWay’s guide to ACT](https://hopeway.org/psychotherapies/acceptance-and-commitment-therapy-act) explains how these skills can change a person’s relationship with difficult thoughts and feelings. The processes below often overlap in practice, and people may use different ones at different times.

### Accept difficult thoughts, feelings, and sensations

Acceptance means allowing thoughts, emotions, and sensations to be present without using all your energy to fight or avoid them. It doesn’t mean liking what you feel, approving of a situation, or giving up on treatment. It means noticing your experience and making room to choose how you’ll respond.

For someone living with chronic pain, acceptance might begin with recognizing frustration or fear without criticizing themselves for feeling it. They can still discuss symptoms with a clinician, follow a care plan, and make choices that protect their health. Acceptance is not a reason to ignore new or worsening symptoms or to force activity that feels unsafe. It’s a way to reduce the added strain of struggling with an experience that’s already difficult. [HopeWay describes acceptance as making room for difficult experiences](https://hopeway.org/psychotherapies/acceptance-and-commitment-therapy-act) while continuing to move toward meaningful goals.

### Notice thoughts without treating them as facts

Cognitive defusion is the practice of noticing thoughts without automatically treating them as facts, predictions, or instructions. For example, after a painful day, you might think, “I’ll never be able to enjoy a walk again.” The thought may feel convincing, but defusion can help you notice it as a thought: “I’m having the thought that I’ll never enjoy a walk again.”

That small shift doesn’t require you to prove the thought wrong or replace it with forced optimism. It creates space to consider what you know, what your body needs, and what support might help. You can then make a decision based on the situation in front of you, rather than letting one prediction make the choice for you. [HopeWay explains how cognitive defusion can change responses to distressing thoughts](https://hopeway.org/psychotherapies/acceptance-and-commitment-therapy-act). A therapist can help you practice the skill in ways that feel relevant to your daily life.

### Stay present with what’s happening now

Present-moment awareness means paying attention to what is happening right now, with curiosity and less judgment. You might notice your breathing, the feel of your feet on the floor, or how your body responds as you get ready for an activity. These observations can bring your attention back from worries about what might happen later or regrets about what has already happened.

Being present doesn’t mean ignoring pain or other symptoms. It can help you notice them more clearly and respond thoughtfully. For instance, you might recognize stiffness, worry, and an urge to cancel an outing, then consider whether a shorter visit or extra support would make the plan workable. [Psychology Today explains present-moment awareness as a core part of ACT](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/therapy-types/acceptance-and-commitment-therapy). A therapist can suggest mindfulness practices that fit your comfort level and health needs.

### See yourself as more than your experiences

Self-as-context is the idea that you are more than any one thought, feeling, diagnosis, or life experience. Pain or reduced mobility may affect your routines and choices, but they don’t describe everything about who you are. You may be dealing with uncertainty while also being a friend, partner, parent, neighbor, or person who values learning and connection.

This perspective doesn’t minimize the impact of illness or suggest that you can simply think your way past a limitation. Instead, it offers a broader view of yourself, so a difficult moment doesn’t have to define your whole identity or determine every future choice. In ACT, a therapist might guide you to observe thoughts and feelings as experiences that change over time. This process works alongside acceptance and mindfulness to help you respond with greater perspective, while still taking your needs seriously.

### Clarify what matters to you

Values clarification helps you identify what you want your choices to stand for. Values are not the same as goals: a goal can be completed, while a value can guide ongoing actions. “Have dinner with my family” is a goal; connection or family may be the values behind it.

This distinction can help when pain, low energy, or mobility changes make familiar plans harder. You might ask what matters about an activity and consider other ways to express that value. If gardening represents creativity and time outdoors, tending a few containers could be one option. Values are personal, not rules set by a therapist, family member, or healthcare provider. [HopeWay describes values clarification as a way to guide choices](https://hopeway.org/psychotherapies/acceptance-and-commitment-therapy-act). In therapy, you can explore what feels meaningful to you and how your priorities might shape realistic goals.

### Take values-guided action

Committed action means taking practical steps toward what matters, even when difficult thoughts or feelings are present. The steps can be small, and they can change as your needs change. If independence is important to you, for example, one step might be asking a healthcare professional about safe ways to maintain daily movement. Another might be planning an errand at a time of day when you typically have more energy.

ACT is not about pushing through pain or treating every goal as a test of willpower. A useful action should take your health, abilities, and clinical guidance into account. A therapist can help break a larger goal into manageable steps, consider possible barriers, and review what you learn along the way. [Psychology Today describes committed action as taking practical steps toward value-based goals](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/therapy-types/acceptance-and-commitment-therapy). Progress may involve adjusting the plan, not abandoning what matters.

### See how the six processes work together

The six processes support one another rather than follow a strict sequence. Imagine you’re worried about returning to an activity after a painful experience. You might use defusion to recognize that “I can’t do this” is a thought, not a certainty. Acceptance can help you make room for the worry, while present-moment awareness helps you check what your body is telling you now.

Self-as-context can remind you that this moment of fear isn’t your whole story. Values clarification can help you understand why the activity matters, such as connection, enjoyment, or independence. Then committed action can turn that value into a reasonable next step, perhaps discussing an adapted plan with a clinician. You might revisit any of these processes as circumstances change. ACT brings them together to support more flexible, considered responses, not to guarantee a particular outcome.

## What Happens in ACT Therapy?

ACT therapy is collaborative and practical. You and your therapist explore how you respond to difficult thoughts, feelings, or physical sensations, then connect that work to what matters in your life. Sessions may include conversation, mindfulness, and exercises to practice between appointments. The pace and focus should reflect your needs, health, and goals.

### Use mindfulness, values clarification, and experiential exercises

Your therapist may guide you through a short mindfulness exercise, such as noticing your breath or the sounds around you. The goal isn’t to clear your mind or force yourself to feel calm. It’s to practice paying attention to what’s happening in the present.

You may also clarify your values, or the qualities you want to bring to areas such as relationships, health, and daily life. Experiential exercises help you explore those ideas in practice. For example, you might notice what happens when you try to push away a worry, then compare it with allowing the thought to be present while choosing your next step. This combination of awareness and values work is described in this [overview of ACT](https://fccwellbeing.com/acceptance-and-commitment-therapy-act).

### Practice acceptance, defusion, and activity planning

Acceptance means making room for a difficult thought, feeling, or sensation without letting it dictate every choice. Defusion is the practice of noticing a thought as a thought, rather than automatically treating it as a fact or instruction. Your therapist might help you shift from “I can’t do this” to “I’m noticing the thought that I can’t do this.” The thought may remain, but you can consider how you want to respond.

If pain, fatigue, or another health concern affects your routine, you might plan a small activity that matters to you and adapt it to your abilities. ACT can help you take meaningful steps without waiting for all discomfort to go away. This [guide to ACT and pain](https://www.ziphealthy.com/Blog-ACT-Therapy.html) discusses how acceptance and defusion may support that process.

### Work with discomfort at a manageable pace

ACT doesn’t require you to push through overwhelming situations or ignore your physical limits. You and your therapist can approach difficult emotions, memories, or sensations gradually, checking in about what feels manageable. You can ask questions, pause an exercise, or talk about a different approach.

This care is especially important when you’re living with chronic pain, health changes, or a history of trauma. Acceptance doesn’t mean approving of hardship or giving up on medical care. It means practicing a more considered response when discomfort arises. A [discussion of ACT for older adults](https://www.sailorhealth.com/blog-post/acceptance-commitment-therapy-seniors) notes that acknowledging thoughts and feelings without pressure to change them may feel more approachable for some people.

### Practice between sessions and track progress

Your therapist may suggest trying a skill between appointments, such as a brief mindfulness exercise or noticing when a thought pulls you away from an activity you value. You might also set a small, practical goal, like calling a friend or taking a short walk if it fits your health needs. These activities are opportunities to practice, not tests. At your next session, you can discuss what felt useful, difficult, or unrealistic.

You and your therapist can review progress by looking at changes in your choices and daily life, not only whether symptoms have eased. You can adjust goals as your needs change, too. ACT is used for a range of concerns, but your therapist should shape the work around your circumstances and coordinate with your other care providers when appropriate.

## What Concerns Can ACT Help With?

ACT may help people respond differently to difficult thoughts, emotions, and physical sensations. It is used for a range of concerns, but it does not work the same way for everyone. A qualified mental health professional can help you decide whether it fits your needs and how it might complement other care.

### Address chronic pain and long-term health concerns

Living with chronic pain or a long-term health condition can affect more than your body. It may also shape your routines, relationships, and sense of independence. ACT does not promise to eliminate pain. Instead, it can help you notice difficult thoughts and sensations while finding manageable ways to take part in activities that matter to you. That might mean adapting a walk, returning to a hobby gradually, or making time for people you care about.

Acceptance does not mean ignoring pain or pushing through warning signs. New or worsening symptoms need medical attention, and activity should suit your health and abilities. [ACT’s approach to coping with pain](https://www.ziphealthy.com/Blog-ACT-Therapy.html) focuses on living meaningfully alongside discomfort, not replacing medical care.

### Address anxiety and depression

Anxiety and depression can make everyday tasks and relationships feel harder to manage. ACT may help you notice difficult thoughts and feelings without letting them dictate every choice. With a therapist, you can practice making room for discomfort and taking small steps toward what matters to you, such as calling a friend, attending an appointment, or returning to a valued activity.

This approach may be useful if distress leads you to avoid situations or leaves you feeling stuck in repetitive thoughts. Therapy should move at a pace that feels manageable, and you do not have to dismiss or minimize what you are experiencing. ACT is not a quick fix, and a clinician can help determine whether it belongs alongside other forms of therapy or medical care. Learn more about [ACT’s approach to emotional and mental health challenges](https://www.elevatece.com/post/7-ways-act-helps-clients-overcome-mental-and-emotional-stuck-points).

### Explore OCD, substance use, and other concerns

ACT may be considered as part of care for concerns such as obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), or substance use. It can help people notice distressing thoughts, urges, or memories and practice responding in ways that support their goals. The aim is not to judge those experiences or treat them as personal failures.

Treatment should be tailored to the concern. If you have OCD, ask a qualified mental health professional how ACT may fit with established treatment options. If you are dealing with substance use, ACT should support, not replace, appropriate addiction care. Evidence for ACT in substance use treatment is still developing. The [Recovery Research Institute’s review](https://dev.recoveryanswers.org/research-post/acceptance-commitment-therapy-act-preliminary-evaluation-of-effectiveness) describes preliminary findings and notes the need for further research.

### Recognize that evidence varies by condition and person

ACT has been studied for chronic pain and a range of mental health concerns, but research strength varies by condition. Outcomes may also depend on your circumstances, goals, the therapist’s experience, and whether the approach feels right for you. One person’s progress does not guarantee the same results for someone else.

Research focused specifically on older adults remains limited. A pilot study of an ACT group for older adults called for larger studies with comparison groups to better assess its effectiveness. You can [review the study and its limitations](https://pacja.org.au/api/v1/articles/71197-age-with-act-a-pilot-acceptance-and-commitment-therapy-group-for-older-adults-receiving-a-community-mental-health-service.pdf). Before starting, ask your provider what evidence relates to your concern, what progress might look like, and when you will review whether therapy is helping.

### Combine ACT with medical and mental health care

ACT can be one part of a broader care plan that may also include medical treatment, physical rehabilitation, medication, or other mental health support. This can matter when pain, mobility changes, or ongoing health concerns affect both your physical and emotional well-being. With your permission, your therapist and medical providers may coordinate around practical goals, such as adjusting activity gradually or coping with stress related to treatment.

Share relevant symptoms, diagnoses, medications, and changes in your health with your care team. ACT should not delay medical assessment or replace care recommended by a qualified clinician. If you already work with a physician or therapist, ask how ACT might fit your current plan and how you will track progress. A coordinated approach can keep therapy connected to your priorities while accounting for your health, abilities, and comfort.

## How Can ACT Support Adults Aging With Pain or Mobility Changes?

Pain, stiffness, fatigue, or changes in balance can make everyday routines feel less predictable. Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) does not promise to eliminate these challenges. It can help you respond to difficult thoughts and feelings while choosing actions that matter to you. ACT should complement, not replace, appropriate medical care.

### Make room for difficult experiences while staying engaged in daily life

When pain or limited mobility affects your routine, avoiding activities that might bring discomfort can feel like the safest choice. Over time, though, avoiding more activities may also mean missing social plans, hobbies, or tasks that support your independence. ACT helps you notice what you’re experiencing and consider whether there’s a safe, manageable way to stay involved.

For example, you might join a family meal for part of the time, take a short walk with rest breaks, or ask for help with one step of an activity. The aim isn’t to ignore pain or push through warning signs. It’s to make room for difficult experiences while choosing meaningful actions. [ACT for older adults](https://www.sailorhealth.com/blog-post/acceptance-commitment-therapy-seniors) may be adapted to help people stay engaged as they face age-related changes.

### Set values-based goals for activity, independence, and connection

ACT can help you identify what matters most in your daily life. You might value being present for family, caring for yourself, spending time outdoors, or maintaining independence. These values can guide practical goals, even when pain or mobility changes mean you need to approach an activity differently.

If connection matters to you, a goal could be calling a friend each week or attending a shorter social gathering. If staying active is important, ask your care team about safe ways to include movement in your routine. Values are personal, and goals should reflect your priorities, not someone else’s expectations. [Psychology Today’s overview of ACT](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/therapy-types/acceptance-and-commitment-therapy) describes how personal values can guide choices, even when distress is present.

### Adapt exercises to individual needs, abilities, and health conditions

ACT exercises should fit the person doing them. A therapist might offer a shorter mindfulness practice, spoken instructions, written prompts, or examples based on your daily routine. If sitting on the floor, closing your eyes, or focusing on physical sensations feels uncomfortable, ask about another way to practice. The goal is to make the exercise useful and manageable, not to follow a one-size-fits-all plan.

Tell your therapist about health conditions, sensory needs, mobility concerns, and preferences that could affect your sessions. If an exercise causes distress or physical discomfort, speak up so you can adjust it together. [Guidance on ACT for older adults](https://www.sailorhealth.com/blog-post/acceptance-commitment-therapy-seniors) notes that therapists may adapt their approach to account for age-related needs and individual circumstances.

### Consider culture, preferences, and research limitations

Your beliefs, family roles, language, and experiences with health care can shape what feels helpful. Let your therapist know what matters to you and what you’d prefer to avoid. You may want mindfulness practices that fit your faith, for example, or goals focused on daily function rather than broader ideas about personal growth. Therapy should make room for your perspective.

Research on ACT for older adults is still developing, so findings from one group may not apply to everyone. A pilot program in a community mental health setting called for larger studies with comparison groups and controls. You can read about these [research limitations](https://pacja.org.au/api/v1/articles/71197-age-with-act-a-pilot-acceptance-and-commitment-therapy-group-for-older-adults-receiving-a-community-mental-health-service.pdf). Ask your therapist how you’ll review progress, and discuss pain or mobility changes with your medical team, especially if they affect your safety.

## How Does ACT Differ From CBT?

### Change your relationship with thoughts rather than evaluate them

Cognitive behavioral therapy, or CBT, often helps people notice unhelpful thinking patterns and examine whether their thoughts are accurate or useful. Acceptance and commitment therapy, or ACT, places more emphasis on changing how you respond to thoughts and feelings. Rather than trying to make every difficult thought go away, you practice noticing it without automatically treating it as fact or instruction. [Psychology Today’s overview of ACT](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/therapy-types/acceptance-and-commitment-therapy) describes acceptance as a way to make room for difficult experiences while still taking practical steps.

For example, if pain brings up the thought, “I can’t do anything today,” ACT may help you recognize that thought, check what your body can manage, and choose a safe activity that supports your priorities. Acceptance doesn’t mean ignoring symptoms or pushing through pain.

### See where ACT and CBT overlap

ACT and CBT share important foundations. Both are structured approaches that can help you notice patterns, build coping skills, and take steps toward meaningful goals. A therapist may use exercises, discussion, and practice between sessions in either approach. The main difference is often the focus: traditional CBT may emphasize examining and reframing thoughts, while ACT emphasizes making room for thoughts and feelings and choosing actions based on personal values.

ACT is commonly grouped with “third-wave” behavioral therapies, which include mindfulness and acceptance-based methods. The approaches can also complement each other. Your therapist may draw from ACT, CBT, or both, depending on your concerns and preferences. The [Association for Contextual Behavioral Science’s introduction to ACT](https://contextualscience.org/act) offers more information about ACT’s principles and how they relate to behavioral therapy.

### Choose an approach that fits your needs and preferences

There isn’t one therapy style that works best for everyone. Your choice may depend on your goals, the concerns you want to address, and how comfortable you feel with each approach. If chronic pain, reduced mobility, or other health changes affect your daily life, consider asking whether a therapist has experience supporting people with similar needs. With your permission, they may also coordinate with your medical team.

When considering a therapist, ask what sessions involve, how you’ll track progress, and how the approach can be adapted to your circumstances. You can discuss any concerns about acceptance, mindfulness, or examining thoughts before beginning. ACT has been used for a range of mental health concerns, but results vary by person and condition. A [review of ACT’s potential applications](https://www.elevatece.com/post/7-ways-act-helps-clients-overcome-mental-and-emotional-stuck-points) can help you prepare questions for a qualified mental health professional.

## What Are ACT’s Benefits and Limitations?

### Explore potential benefits for coping, function, and values-based living

Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) may help you respond to difficult thoughts, feelings, and physical sensations while staying connected to the parts of life that matter to you. Progress might mean spending time with family, keeping up with a meaningful activity, or taking steps toward greater independence, even if pain or worry is still present. These goals should be realistic for your health and abilities, and they do not replace guidance from your medical team.

ACT focuses on psychological flexibility: noticing what is happening inside and around you, then choosing how to respond. It has been used for concerns such as depression, PTSD, obsessive-compulsive disorder, and substance use. Results can differ by person and condition, so ACT should not be seen as a guarantee of symptom relief. [Research on ACT’s potential benefits](https://www.elevatece.com/post/7-ways-act-helps-clients-overcome-mental-and-emotional-stuck-points) describes its use across a range of mental health concerns.

### Expect practice to feel challenging when facing difficult emotions

ACT does not ask you to approve of pain, fear, or frustration, or to pretend these experiences are easy. Instead, it helps you notice them without automatically letting them decide what you do next. This can feel unfamiliar, especially if you have spent a long time trying to avoid difficult situations or push uncomfortable thoughts away.

You and your therapist can work at a manageable pace. If an exercise feels overwhelming, say so. Your therapist can adjust it, help you pause, or discuss what support might make the work feel safer. The goal is not to force yourself through distress. It is to practice responding with awareness while respecting your limits. For some people, [acknowledging thoughts without trying to change them](https://www.sailorhealth.com/blog-post/acceptance-commitment-therapy-seniors) can feel like a more compassionate starting point.

### Recognize that ACT works differently for everyone

ACT is not the right fit for every person or every concern. You may find mindfulness, acceptance, or values-based exercises useful, while someone else may prefer a more structured approach to examining thoughts or solving problems. Your goals, health needs, personal preferences, culture, and relationship with your therapist can all shape how helpful therapy feels.

Research findings also vary across conditions, and some areas need further study. For instance, the [Recovery Research Institute’s review of ACT for substance use](https://dev.recoveryanswers.org/research-post/acceptance-commitment-therapy-act-preliminary-evaluation-of-effectiveness) describes the evidence as preliminary. Ask your therapist how ACT may relate to your needs, how you will review progress, and what alternatives are available. If you do not feel that therapy is helping, you can talk about adjusting the approach or exploring another option.

### Don’t use ACT as a substitute for needed medical or mental health care

ACT can be part of a broader care plan, but it does not replace medical evaluation, prescribed treatment, or other mental health care. If pain, weakness, numbness, anxiety, or low mood is affecting your daily life, speak with a qualified health professional about what may be causing it and which treatments may help. Do not stop medication or change a treatment plan without consulting the clinician who prescribed it.

If you begin ACT, tell your therapist about relevant diagnoses, medications, mobility concerns, and other care you receive. With your permission, the therapist can coordinate with your healthcare providers so that therapy goals and exercises fit your needs. For people managing chronic pain or other health changes, emotional coping skills and physical treatment can play complementary roles. [ACT is best considered alongside appropriate care](https://www.ziphealthy.com/Blog-ACT-Therapy.html), rather than as a replacement for it.

## Is ACT a Good Fit for You?

ACT may be worth considering if pain, health changes, or difficult thoughts are getting in the way of activities that matter to you. The best fit depends on your goals, preferences, and willingness to practice new skills. You don’t need a particular diagnosis to explore whether this approach could help.

### Consider your goals, readiness, and preferred therapy style

Start by thinking about what you’d like to do more often. You may want to return to a favorite activity, feel more present with family, or respond to pain with less fear. ACT helps you identify what matters and take steps toward it, even when discomfort or difficult thoughts are part of the picture. It may be a good match if you often avoid activities because of distress or feel stuck in patterns that limit your choices. [ACT may help people address patterns that keep them feeling stuck](https://www.elevatece.com/post/7-ways-act-helps-clients-overcome-mental-and-emotional-stuck-points).

Also consider how you prefer to work with a therapist. ACT can include conversation, mindfulness, and practical exercises, with skills to practice between sessions. Ask what a typical session involves and how your therapist will tailor the approach to your goals.

### Discuss concerns about acceptance, emotional discomfort, and values work

Acceptance can sound like giving in to pain or deciding that a difficult situation is fine. In ACT, it means making room for thoughts, feelings, and sensations without letting them control every choice. You can acknowledge pain while continuing to seek medical care and working toward improved comfort or function.

Some exercises may bring up uncomfortable emotions. Talk with your therapist about what feels manageable and ask to adjust the pace if needed. You don’t have to share everything at once or push through an exercise that feels overwhelming. [ACT for older adults can involve noticing thoughts as they arise without pressuring yourself to change them](https://www.sailorhealth.com/blog-post/acceptance-commitment-therapy-seniors). Values work is personal, too. It helps you identify what matters to you; it isn’t about a therapist deciding what your priorities should be.

### Explore other options if ACT doesn’t feel right

ACT is one therapy option, not a requirement. You may prefer a different approach, or want to combine therapy with medical care, physical rehabilitation, or other support. Tell your therapist what feels useful and what doesn’t. You can ask about alternatives, how progress will be reviewed, and whether the approach can be adapted to your health needs and goals.

ACT is used to support people dealing with concerns such as pain, grief, relationship challenges, and life transitions. That range doesn’t mean it works the same way for everyone. [ACT’s focus on values and meaningful living may be helpful for people facing a range of life challenges](https://www.ziphealthy.com/Blog-ACT-Therapy.html). If pain, mobility changes, or another health concern is part of your situation, discuss therapy options with your healthcare providers so your care supports your overall needs.

## How Do You Find an ACT Therapist?

Look for a therapist whose training and approach match your needs and goals. If you’re managing chronic pain, mobility changes, or another health concern, ask how therapy can fit alongside your medical care and support the activities that matter to you.

### Check credentials, ACT training, and relevant experience

Confirm that the therapist is licensed to provide mental health care where you live. Then ask about their specific training in Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, including relevant coursework, workshops, or supervised experience. Some therapists use mindfulness or acceptance techniques without having extensive ACT training, so it’s reasonable to ask how they apply the approach in sessions.

Consider whether they have experience supporting people with concerns like yours, such as chronic pain, anxiety, or changes in independence. You might ask, “How have you adapted ACT for people managing ongoing health concerns?” The [Psychology Today overview of ACT](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/therapy-types/acceptance-and-commitment-therapy) can help you understand the approach and prepare questions for an initial conversation.

### Ask how the therapist reviews goals and progress

Ask how you and the therapist will choose goals, review progress, and make changes if the plan isn’t helping. In ACT, goals often focus on taking meaningful steps even when difficult thoughts, emotions, or physical sensations are present. You might work toward returning to a valued activity, building a manageable routine, or spending more time with people you care about.

Progress can include changes in how you respond to challenges, not only changes in symptoms. Ask what you’ll track and how often you’ll discuss what is or isn’t working. A collaborative therapist should welcome your feedback and connect therapy goals to your priorities. [GoodTherapy’s guide to ACT](https://www.goodtherapy.org/learn-about-therapy/types/acceptance-commitment-therapy) explains how personal values can help shape those goals.

### Discuss coordination with medical care and other providers

If you receive care for pain, mobility concerns, or another health condition, ask whether the therapist can coordinate with your physician or other providers when it would be helpful. Discuss what information might be shared, how your consent will be handled, and whether you can choose what is communicated. You can also ask how sessions or exercises may be adapted to your health needs, abilities, and energy levels.

Coordination may be especially useful when several professionals are involved in your care. The [Sailor Health overview of ACT for older adults](https://www.sailorhealth.com/blog-post/acceptance-commitment-therapy-seniors) discusses communication between providers in the context of mental and physical health. A therapist should be clear about their role and work with you to keep any coordination focused on your preferences and care goals.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can ACT therapy help with chronic pain?**\
ACT may help you respond to pain-related thoughts and feelings while staying involved in activities that matter to you. It does not promise pain relief or replace medical treatment. A therapist can help shape goals around your abilities and care plan.

**Does acceptance mean I have to live with pain without seeking treatment?**\
No. Acceptance means recognizing what you’re experiencing without letting it dictate every decision. You can still seek an evaluation, discuss treatment options, and follow medical guidance.

**What might I do during an ACT session?**\
Sessions may include conversation, mindfulness exercises, and practical activities focused on your values and goals. Your therapist may also suggest a skill to practice between visits. You can ask to adjust an exercise or slow the pace if it feels uncomfortable.

**How is ACT different from CBT?**\
CBT often focuses on examining thought patterns and considering whether they’re accurate or helpful. ACT focuses more on noticing thoughts without automatically treating them as facts, then choosing actions that reflect your values. A therapist may use elements of both approaches.

**How can I tell whether an ACT therapist is right for me?**\
Ask about their license, ACT training, experience with concerns like yours, and how they’ll track progress. If you have ongoing health needs, discuss how therapy can complement your medical care and be adapted to your abilities and preferences.
