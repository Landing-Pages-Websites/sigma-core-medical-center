Complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) can affect more than the part of the body that hurts. Pain and sensitivity may make movement difficult, while swelling or changes in skin temperature and color can add to the uncertainty. For adults who want to stay active and independent, persistent symptoms can raise understandable concerns about what comes next. CRPS has no single known cause, and its symptoms can resemble those of other conditions. A thoughtful assessment can help clarify possible explanations and guide care. Learn how clinicians evaluate CRPS, what treatment approaches may support movement and comfort, and when persistent or rapidly changing symptoms call for medical attention.

## Key Takeaways

* **CRPS can affect pain, sensation, and movement**: Track changes such as swelling, skin color or temperature differences, touch sensitivity, and stiffness.
* **Diagnosis requires a clinical assessment**: Share when symptoms began and how they affect daily activities; no single test can confirm CRPS.
* **Personalized care can support function**: Ask your care team about suitable options, such as rehabilitation, medication, or psychological support. Seek prompt care for sudden or rapidly worsening symptoms.

## What Is Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS)?

Complex regional pain syndrome, or CRPS, is a condition that causes ongoing pain, usually in an arm, hand, leg, or foot. It often begins after an injury, surgery, or another event affecting a limb, but the pain may persist or feel more intense than expected. CRPS can also involve changes in skin temperature, color, swelling, or movement. Symptoms differ from person to person, and other conditions can cause similar changes, so an assessment by a healthcare professional is important.

### How CRPS affects the nervous system

CRPS is thought to involve changes in both the peripheral nervous system, which carries messages between the body and brain, and the central nervous system, which includes the brain and spinal cord. These systems help process pain and respond to touch, temperature, and movement. When their signaling changes, ordinary contact or movement may feel painful, and the affected limb may respond differently than expected.

Researchers are still learning why CRPS develops. It may involve several factors, and there is no single explanation that applies to everyone. The [Columbus Regional Healthcare System notes that the exact cause is not fully understood](https://crhealthcare.org/conditions/complex-regional-pain-syndrome-crps/). A clinician can review your symptoms and medical history, examine the affected area, and consider other possible causes before recommending care.

### CRPS is real—not “all in your head”

CRPS pain is real, even when imaging or other tests do not show an obvious injury that explains how severe it feels. The nervous system plays a role in how the body experiences pain, and symptoms deserve to be taken seriously. Anxiety, depression, and stress may occur alongside CRPS and can influence the pain experience, but that does not mean the pain is imagined or caused by a person’s emotions.

Care may address physical symptoms as well as emotional well-being. Support from a mental health professional can be useful for coping with persistent pain, alongside appropriate medical care. The Cleveland Clinic explains that psychotherapy may help people with CRPS. If you feel dismissed or unsure about your symptoms, consider discussing your concerns with a clinician experienced in persistent pain.

### Who can develop CRPS?

CRPS can affect adults of different ages, and children can develop it too. It is reported more often in women than in men. The Cleveland Clinic identifies adults as more commonly affected than children, with peak onset around age 40. These are general patterns, not a way to diagnose the condition: age or sex alone cannot confirm or rule out CRPS.

If pain continues after an injury or procedure, or you notice lasting changes in a limb, arrange an assessment with a healthcare professional. This is especially worthwhile if pain makes it harder to use the affected arm or leg in daily activities. A clinician can evaluate the pattern of symptoms, check for other explanations, and discuss suitable next steps.

## What Are the Symptoms of CRPS?

Complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) most often affects one arm or leg. It may develop after an injury or surgery, but symptoms can also appear without a clear trigger. Pain is a common feature, yet CRPS can affect other aspects of the limb too, including skin color, temperature, swelling, sweating, and movement. Symptoms vary from person to person, and no single change is enough to confirm a diagnosis.

The changes can make everyday tasks harder, from getting dressed to walking or sleeping. Keep track of when symptoms began, where they occur, and how they affect your routine. This information can help a clinician assess the full pattern and consider other possible causes. Seek medical advice for persistent pain or ongoing changes in a limb, especially if they limit your ability to move or manage daily activities. The Cleveland Clinic’s CRPS overview describes symptoms that may be considered during an evaluation.

### Ongoing pain and touch sensitivity

CRPS pain may feel burning, aching, throbbing, or sharp. It can be more intense than expected from the original injury and may continue after the injury has healed. Pain can also occur without an obvious trigger.

Some people develop allodynia, meaning gentle contact that would not usually hurt becomes painful. Clothing brushing against the skin, a light touch, or a change in temperature may feel uncomfortable. Increased sensitivity can make it difficult to use the affected limb or tolerate routine care.

Write down what the pain feels like, when it occurs, and what seems to affect it. Share those details with a healthcare professional if pain persists or interferes with daily activities. [Harvard Health describes pain and touch sensitivity associated with CRPS](https://www.health.harvard.edu/pain/complex-regional-pain-syndrome-crps-a-to-z).

### Changes in swelling, skin color, temperature, and sweating

The affected limb may swell or look different from the other side of the body. Skin can appear red, pale, bluish, or mottled, and it may feel warmer or cooler. Some people also notice more or less sweating in the area. These changes may come and go or vary in intensity.

Note when you see a change and whether it occurs alongside pain or difficulty moving. Photos taken at different times can help you describe what you’ve noticed during an appointment, but they cannot diagnose CRPS. A clinician can examine the limb and consider other causes of swelling or changes in skin color and temperature. The Cleveland Clinic lists these possible CRPS symptoms.

### Stiffness, weakness, and movement changes

Pain and sensitivity may make it harder to move the affected arm or leg. You might notice stiffness, weakness, tremors, muscle spasms, or trouble coordinating movement. Everyday actions such as gripping a cup, bending your wrist, or putting weight on your foot may become difficult.

Avoiding movement because it hurts can affect strength and flexibility over time. Tell your clinician which movements are difficult and how symptoms affect your routine. They can assess your function and advise whether guided rehabilitation may be appropriate. Don’t force a painful movement or begin exercises that worsen symptoms without professional advice. [Harvard Health notes movement problems that can occur with CRPS](https://www.health.harvard.edu/pain/complex-regional-pain-syndrome-crps-a-to-z).

### Changes in skin, hair, or nail growth

CRPS may affect the skin and the growth of hair or nails in the affected area. Skin can become dry, thin, tight, or shiny. Hair may grow more or less than usual, while nails may become brittle or change their growth rate. These changes can be subtle and may become noticeable during bathing, dressing, or nail care.

Mention new or ongoing changes during a medical evaluation, particularly if they occur with pain, swelling, or sensitivity. A clinician will consider the overall pattern of symptoms rather than using one skin or nail change alone to identify CRPS. Other health conditions can also affect skin and nail growth. [Harvard Health describes skin, hair, and nail changes linked with CRPS](https://www.health.harvard.edu/pain/complex-regional-pain-syndrome-crps-a-to-z).

### How symptoms can change or spread

CRPS symptoms can vary over time. Pain may persist while swelling, skin color, temperature, or sweating changes come and go. Some people report symptoms beyond the area where they first began, but patterns differ. Symptoms do not follow the same predictable stages for everyone, and a change in symptoms does not by itself mean CRPS has spread.

Keep a simple record of where symptoms occur, when they change, and how they affect movement and daily activities. Share it with your clinician, who can evaluate the changes and check for other possible causes. Seek prompt medical advice if symptoms worsen quickly or you develop new changes that concern you. [Southeast Pain & Spine Care discusses how CRPS symptoms may change over time](https://www.sepainandspinecare.com/exploring-the-stages-of-crps).

## What Are the Types and Possible Causes of CRPS?

Complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) is grouped into two types based on whether a specific nerve injury has been identified. An injury or procedure may precede symptoms, but some people develop CRPS without a clear trigger.

### How CRPS Type 1 differs from Type 2

CRPS Type 1 occurs without a confirmed nerve injury. It was once commonly called reflex sympathetic dystrophy, or RSD. CRPS Type 2 follows a known nerve injury and was previously called causalgia. Both types can cause ongoing pain, sensitivity to touch, swelling, and changes in skin temperature or color. The distinction is whether a nerve injury has been identified, not how severe the symptoms are. The Cleveland Clinic’s CRPS guide explains how the types are classified.

If you have persistent pain or changes in a limb after an injury or procedure, tell your clinician when the symptoms began and how they have changed. This information can help guide an evaluation.

### Injuries, surgery, and other possible triggers

CRPS may develop after an injury or medical procedure. Possible triggers include fractures, especially wrist fractures, surgery, sprains, strains, burns, bruises, and cuts. It can also occur after a limb has been immobilized for a prolonged period. The Cleveland Clinic lists these potential triggers, while noting that the cause is not always clear.

Having one of these experiences does not mean you will develop CRPS. Many people recover without it, and clinicians cannot always explain why it develops in some people but not others. Contact a healthcare provider if pain seems unusually intense, lasts longer than expected, or comes with swelling or skin changes. Note when symptoms started and whether they are improving, staying the same, or worsening.

### When symptoms have no clear cause

CRPS can sometimes begin without an obvious injury or trigger. Its underlying cause is not fully understood. Researchers are studying whether an abnormal inflammatory or immune response involving the nervous system may play a role, but this does not explain every case.

A lack of a clear trigger does not make the symptoms less real. A clinician can assess your symptoms and check for other conditions that may cause similar pain or changes in a limb. Before your appointment, note where you feel pain, when it began, and whether you have noticed changes in movement, skin color or temperature, or swelling. This record can help with the assessment, even when there is no specific event to connect to your symptoms.

## How Do Clinicians Diagnose CRPS?

Diagnosing complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS) involves looking at your symptoms, medical history, and physical exam together. There is no single blood test, scan, or procedure that confirms CRPS. Instead, a clinician assesses how the pain began, whether it seems greater than expected for the original injury, and whether you have changes in sensation, skin, sweating, or movement.

CRPS can look like other conditions, including nerve injuries, circulation problems, infections, and joint disorders. Your clinician may ask about an injury or surgery, but symptoms can also appear without a clear trigger. They may compare the affected limb with the other side, checking for differences in temperature, color, swelling, sensation, and range of motion. Symptoms can change over time, so details about what you have noticed between appointments can be helpful.

The Cleveland Clinic’s CRPS overview explains that diagnosis relies on medical history and physical examination, often with the Budapest Criteria as a guide. Further tests may help investigate other possible causes. If you have persistent pain and changes in a limb, a healthcare professional can assess your symptoms and decide what evaluation is appropriate.

### Review your medical history and examine you

Your clinician may ask when the pain began, whether it followed an injury or procedure, and how it has changed. Describe what you feel, such as burning pain or unusual sensitivity to touch, as well as any swelling, temperature or color changes, sweating, stiffness, or weakness. Mention whether symptoms come and go or affect your sleep and everyday activities.

During the exam, the clinician may compare both limbs and check sensation, strength, movement, swelling, skin temperature, and visible changes to the skin, hair, or nails. A symptom diary or photos can help you describe changes that are not present during the appointment. These notes do not replace an exam, but they can give your clinician a clearer picture of how symptoms vary.

### Assess symptoms and signs using the Budapest criteria

The Budapest Criteria help clinicians assess whether your symptoms and exam findings fit CRPS. They group features into four categories: sensory changes, such as pain sensitivity; changes in skin temperature or color; swelling or sweating changes; and movement problems or changes in skin, hair, or nails.

For the criteria to support a diagnosis, a person must report symptoms in at least three of the four categories, and the clinician must observe signs in at least two categories during the assessment. The clinician must also consider whether another condition better explains the symptoms. The criteria guide a clinical evaluation; they are not a test you can complete on your own. [Stanford Medicine’s CRPS information](https://med.stanford.edu/pain/about/chronic-pain/crps.html) describes the categories clinicians assess.

### Use tests to rule out other causes of pain

Your clinician may recommend tests if your history or exam suggests another possible cause of your symptoms. Depending on what they find, these could include imaging, nerve studies, or blood tests. For example, imaging may help identify a fracture or joint problem, while other tests may help assess nerve damage or look for signs of another condition.

These tests help investigate alternatives; they do not confirm CRPS by themselves. A normal scan, for example, does not necessarily rule out CRPS. The tests your clinician recommends will depend on your symptoms and what they need to clarify. Ask what each test is looking for and how its results may guide your care. The Cleveland Clinic explains that testing may help rule out conditions with similar symptoms.

### Why no single test can confirm CRPS

CRPS is diagnosed by considering several pieces of information together, rather than relying on one definitive test. Pain, swelling, temperature changes, and sensitivity can vary, so some signs may be less noticeable at a particular appointment. Your clinician weighs what you report alongside the signs they observe and considers whether another condition could account for them.

If the cause of your symptoms remains unclear, ask what possibilities your clinician is considering and whether a follow-up exam or referral may be helpful. Keep notes on changes in pain, movement, and limb appearance so you can share specific examples. [Stanford Medicine notes](https://med.stanford.edu/pain/about/chronic-pain/crps.html) that clinicians use the Budapest Criteria to guide diagnosis, while other assessments may be considered in selected cases.

## How Do Clinicians Treat CRPS, and When Should You Seek Care?

CRPS care focuses on easing symptoms, supporting movement, and helping you maintain independence. Because the condition can affect pain, sensation, strength, and daily function, clinicians often combine several approaches rather than relying on one treatment. Your plan may include physical or occupational therapy, medication, psychological support, or procedures for persistent symptoms.

The right approach depends on your symptoms, health history, other medications, and personal goals. For example, you may want to improve your ability to walk, sleep more comfortably, or manage tasks at home. Share those priorities with your care team so you can track progress in ways that matter to you. Treatment may need adjustments over time, and a pain specialist can help coordinate care with your primary clinician and therapists.

Early assessment can be helpful, especially when pain or sensitivity continues after an injury, or when you notice changes in movement, swelling, skin color, or temperature. These symptoms can have causes other than CRPS, so a clinician can evaluate what’s happening and recommend appropriate next steps. Seek urgent care for sudden or rapidly worsening symptoms, particularly when they could signal an infection or another condition requiring prompt attention.

### Use physical and occupational therapy to support movement and independence

Physical and occupational therapy can help you work toward better movement and function while managing CRPS symptoms. A physical therapist may guide gentle exercises to maintain range of motion, strength, and mobility. An occupational therapist can help adapt everyday tasks, such as getting dressed or preparing a meal, to make them more manageable.

Your plan may include gradual exposure to different sensations, mirror therapy, or graded motor imagery. These approaches should be tailored to your needs and comfort. Let your therapist know if an activity feels too intense, and ask how to adjust it rather than pushing through severe pain. The Cleveland Clinic’s guide to CRPS treatment describes rehabilitation approaches clinicians may consider.

### Consider medications and safety for older adults

There isn’t one medication that works for everyone with CRPS, and no medication is specifically approved to treat the condition in the United States. Depending on your symptoms and medical history, your clinician may discuss pain relievers, topical treatments, certain antidepressants or anti-seizure medicines, or other options. The aim is to consider possible relief alongside side effects and interactions.

Bring a complete list of your prescriptions, over-the-counter medicines, and supplements to appointments. Some medications can cause drowsiness or dizziness, which may affect balance and increase fall risk. Others may interact with treatments for existing health conditions. Don’t start, stop, or change a medication without speaking with your clinician. The Cleveland Clinic outlines medication options for CRPS.

### Include psychological support in whole-person care

Persistent pain can affect sleep, mood, relationships, and confidence in your ability to stay active. Psychological support does not mean the pain is imagined. It can help you manage the stress of ongoing symptoms, work through difficult emotions, and develop practical ways to handle daily challenges.

A mental health professional may use psychotherapy to help you build coping strategies and stay connected to routines that matter to you. If pain is affecting your mood or sense of independence, tell your clinician. They can help coordinate support as part of your care plan. The Cleveland Clinic describes psychotherapy as one option in CRPS care.

### Consider nerve blocks and other procedures for selected patients

If pain remains severe or makes rehabilitation difficult, a pain specialist may discuss procedures. Options can include nerve blocks, electrical nerve or spinal cord stimulation, ketamine infusions, or implanted drug pumps. These treatments are intended for selected situations, and potential benefits and risks vary from person to person.

Ask what a procedure is designed to address, how your care team will assess whether it is helping, and what side effects or follow-up may be involved. Discuss how it fits with other parts of your plan, including therapy and daily activity goals. The Cleveland Clinic’s CRPS treatment overview describes procedures that clinicians may consider for persistent symptoms.

### Discuss pacing, home strategies, and complementary approaches with a clinician

Pacing can help you balance activity with rest. Try breaking demanding tasks into smaller steps, taking planned pauses, and increasing activity gradually rather than doing much more on a day when symptoms ease. Your clinician or therapist can help you find a level of movement that supports function without overwhelming you.

Ask your care team about home strategies such as relaxation techniques, sleep routines, or complementary therapies. Their evidence and safety vary, so check before trying a new approach, especially if you take medication or have other health conditions. Keeping a simple record of pain, movement, sleep, and daily activities can help you and your clinician see what may be useful. [Southeast Pain & Spine Care discusses the value of early, comprehensive CRPS care](https://www.sepainandspinecare.com/exploring-the-stages-of-crps).

### Review the evidence on vitamin C and CRPS prevention

Some studies have explored whether vitamin C taken around the time of surgery or an injury might help reduce the risk of CRPS. The evidence does not mean it will prevent CRPS for everyone, and vitamin C is not a treatment for established CRPS. The Cleveland Clinic summarizes research on vitamin C and CRPS prevention.

Before taking a supplement for prevention, ask your surgeon or clinician whether it is appropriate for you. Supplements can interact with medications, and high doses may not be suitable for people with certain health conditions. Don’t delay an assessment of ongoing pain or limb changes while trying a supplement. Your care team can explain what is known, what remains uncertain, and whether there are safety concerns based on your health history.

### Seek medical evaluation for persistent pain or limb changes

Arrange a medical evaluation if pain or sensitivity continues after an injury should be healing, or if you notice swelling, skin color or temperature changes, or declining movement or strength. These symptoms can have several causes. A clinician can assess them and determine whether CRPS or another condition may be involved.

Before your appointment, note when symptoms began, how they have changed, and what makes them better or worse. Mention any impact on walking, sleep, or daily tasks, and bring a list of medicines and relevant health conditions. Timely assessment can help your care team decide on next steps and support your function. [Columbus Regional Healthcare System recommends evaluation for persistent pain or limb changes](https://crhealthcare.org/conditions/complex-regional-pain-syndrome-crps/).

### Get urgent care for new or rapidly worsening symptoms

Seek urgent medical care for sudden, severe pain or rapid changes in an affected limb, especially if it becomes unusually cold, pale, blue, very swollen, or weak. These changes may have causes that need prompt assessment. Call emergency services for symptoms such as chest pain, trouble breathing, fainting, or sudden loss of strength.

Also seek prompt care if you develop a fever, spreading redness or warmth, or drainage near an injury or surgical site, as these can be signs of infection. Don’t assume a new or worsening symptom is part of CRPS. If you’re unsure how quickly you need care, contact your clinician or local urgent medical service and describe what has changed.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can CRPS develop without an injury or surgery?**\
Yes. CRPS sometimes begins without a clear trigger. A clinician can assess your symptoms and check for other conditions that may cause similar pain or limb changes.

**Does CRPS always get worse or spread to other parts of the body?**\
No. Symptoms vary, and they may change over time, but they do not follow the same pattern for everyone. If symptoms appear in a new area or worsen, let your clinician know so they can assess the change.

**Can a scan or blood test confirm CRPS?**\
No single test confirms CRPS. Clinicians use your medical history, symptoms, and exam findings to guide diagnosis. Tests may help rule out other possible causes.

**Is movement safe if I have CRPS?**\
Movement may be part of care, but the right activities and pace depend on your symptoms and health. A physical or occupational therapist can recommend a gradual plan. Don’t force movements that cause severe pain.

**When should I seek urgent care?**\
Get prompt medical attention for sudden, severe pain or rapid changes such as marked swelling, unusual skin color or temperature, or new weakness. Fever, spreading redness, or drainage near an injury or surgical site also needs prompt assessment.
