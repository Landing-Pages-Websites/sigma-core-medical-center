A useful visit starts with your symptoms, the activities you want to keep, your health, and what an evaluation shows. This page explains options to ask about. It does not choose one for you or promise a result.

## What knee osteoarthritis treatment is meant to address

Osteoarthritis is a joint condition in which tissues can change over time. In the knee, that can involve cartilage, bone, the joint lining, ligaments, and the meniscus. The National Institute of Arthritis and Musculoskeletal and Skin Diseases (NIAMS) describes osteoarthritis as the most common type of arthritis, and it is more common with age. Age alone does not decide how much a knee hurts or which care fits.

Symptoms often begin slowly. People may notice pain with use, stiffness after rest, swelling after activity, or a knee that feels less steady. NIAMS notes that morning stiffness with osteoarthritis often lasts less than 30 minutes. Some people stay active with mild symptoms. Others find walking, sleep, work, or recreation much harder. An X-ray can show change and still not match how the knee feels on a given day.

A treatment talk should cover more than a pain score. Knee motion, leg strength, balance, walking distance, stairs, sleep, and the tasks you care about all belong in the conversation. The aim is to understand possible benefits, risks, limits, and alternatives with a qualified clinician. The American Academy of Orthopaedic Surgeons (AAOS) describes nonsurgical care as the first approach for many people with knee arthritis. That does not mean every option fits you, and it does not mean surgery is never appropriate.

## Start with an evaluation, not a treatment name

Knee pain is not always osteoarthritis. A meniscus, ligament, tendon, bursa, hip or back problem, inflammatory arthritis, gout, or an infection can also be involved. A clinician may ask when symptoms started, whether there was an injury, what makes them better or worse, and whether the knee swells, catches, locks, or gives way.

An evaluation may include movement and strength checks, a review of medicines and other conditions, and imaging when the result would change the plan. Ask what a test is meant to show. A test is a tool for a decision, not a decision by itself. Bring a short record of how far you can walk, how stairs feel, what you have already tried, and which tasks matter most.

Some symptoms need prompt care rather than a routine visit. Seek urgent help for a knee that is hot, very swollen, or painful with fever or feeling unwell. After an injury, urgent assessment may be needed if the knee looks deformed, you cannot bear weight, pain is severe, or swelling appears suddenly. A knee that locks or repeatedly gives way also deserves an exam. An article cannot tell you whether your symptoms are urgent.

## How common options differ

Many people discuss more than one option at the same visit. The table below is a way to organize questions. It is not a ranking, a prescription, or a statement that any option is right for you. Evidence and fit vary by person, and a clinician has to match them to your exam, history, and goals.

| Option to discuss.            | What people often hope it supports.                             | Questions worth asking.                                                      |
| ----------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Exercise and rehabilitation.  | Motion, strength, balance, and daily function.                  | What should I start with, and which symptoms mean we should change the plan? |
| Activity changes.             | Staying active while avoiding the tasks that flare the knee.    | Which activities can I keep, modify, or pause for now?                       |
| Weight and whole-health care. | Less load on the knee and attention to related health factors.  | Is this relevant for me, and who should guide it?                            |
| A brace, sleeve, or cane.     | Comfort or confidence with walking for some knee patterns.      | Who should fit it, and how will we know if it helps?                         |
| Topical or oral medicine.     | Short-term symptom relief for some people.                      | Is it safe with my kidneys, stomach, heart, and other medicines?             |
| An injection.                 | Temporary relief for selected people, depending on the product. | What is being injected, what is known, and what are the risks?               |
| A surgical opinion.           | A timed decision when pain and function stay limiting.          | What would make surgery more or less reasonable for me?                      |

Use the rows as a checklist, not as a sequence you must finish. Two people with similar X-rays can leave with different plans because their sleep, medicines, balance, and goals are not the same.

## Movement and rehabilitation

Exercise is one of the options with the strongest support for knee osteoarthritis. The AAOS clinical practice guideline on non-arthroplasty management of knee osteoarthritis recommends supervised exercise, unsupervised exercise, and aquatic exercise over no exercise to improve pain and function. NIAMS also describes physical activity and muscle-strengthening exercise as part of osteoarthritis care. That is a reason to ask about movement. It is not a promise that a program will change your pain by a set amount.

A plan might include strengthening around the knee and hip, motion work, balance practice, and a gradual return to a valued activity. The AAOS review found that the specific mode of exercise may matter less than taking part in a program you can continue. Land exercise and pool exercise can both be discussed. A clinician should match the starting point to your current capacity and other health conditions.

Soreness can happen when someone begins or changes exercise. Concerning pain, swelling, or a knee that feels unstable is a reason to pause and ask for a change, not a reason to guess. Ask how progress will be measured. Walking distance, stair comfort, sleep, and confidence are often more useful than a single pain number. ## Activity changes that still keep you moving

Some people reduce the tasks that reliably flare the knee and substitute a lower-impact option such as cycling, pool work, or another activity a clinician considers appropriate. The point is not to stop using the knee for good. It is to protect strength and mobility while symptoms are being sorted out.

Ask for specifics about stairs, hills, kneeling, lifting, running, and a sport you care about. "Take it easy" is hard to follow. A clearer plan names what to change this week, what to keep, and which symptoms should trigger a reassessment. If fear of pain has already shrunk your day, say so. Avoiding every movement can weaken the leg and make the next step harder.

A short trial is easier to judge than an open-ended restriction. Agree on a time frame and one or two activities you will track. If the knee is calmer but your walking tolerance is falling, the plan needs another look.

## Weight, sleep, and the rest of your health

Body weight is one factor that can change load through the knee. AAOS recommends sustained weight loss to improve pain and function in patients with knee osteoarthritis who have overweight or obesity. The guideline rates that recommendation as moderate, and it notes that diet plus exercise has been studied more favorably than diet alone. This is not a judgment about appearance or effort, and weight is not the only factor in knee pain.

If weight belongs in your visit, ask for an approach that fits your nutrition, mobility, other conditions, and preferences. Sleep, blood sugar, heart health, and mood can also shape what is safe and what you can sustain. NIAMS notes that osteoarthritis pain can affect sleep and energy, which then makes activity harder. Tell the clinician if pain is breaking up your night. That detail can change both rehabilitation and medicine choices.

## Braces, sleeves, and canes

A brace, sleeve, cane, or other support is sometimes discussed when walking confidence, alignment, or comfort is part of the problem. Some braces are meant to support the knee or shift load away from one side of the joint. A device that suits one pattern can be uncomfortable or useless for another. AAOS patient information on knee arthritis describes braces and assistive devices as options some people use, not as care that fits every knee.

Ask who should fit the device, how long to try it, and what change would count as useful. A cane at the wrong height, or held on the wrong side, can create a new ache. Instructions matter as much as the product. If a support lets you walk farther without new pain, that is useful information for the next visit. If it slips, pinches, or changes your limp, bring it back rather than forcing it.

## Medicines to review with a clinician

Some people discuss a topical medicine, acetaminophen, or an anti-inflammatory medicine. The safer choice depends on kidney function, stomach history, blood pressure, heart health, other medicines, and how long it would be used. An over-the-counter label does not make a product risk-free. NIAMS lists medicines among osteoarthritis treatments and stresses that a clinician should guide them.

Ask about interactions, warning symptoms, and whether a topical option could be considered before a pill you would take every day. Do not start, stop, or combine a medicine because of an article. This page gives no dose. Your clinician or pharmacist can check the details against your record. If a medicine helped before, say which one, what it did, and what side effects you noticed.

## Injections and regenerative questions

A clinician may discuss an injection into or around the knee. Corticosteroid injections are one example. Any benefit can be temporary, and repeat use has limits a prescriber should explain. Other injections have different evidence, risks, and roles. Ask what is being proposed, how strong the evidence is for a knee like yours, what side effects are possible, and what you would do if it does not help.

Regenerative options, including platelet-rich plasma (PRP), sometimes come up in joint-care conversations. They are not appropriate for everyone. Evidence varies by condition and by how a product is prepared. Do not treat PRP or a stem cell procedure as a cure for osteoarthritis, and do not assume a procedure is FDA-approved for this use because it is discussed online. AAOS reviews these topics separately from exercise and weight management, which is a signal to ask specific evidence questions rather than to expect a guaranteed change.

## When surgery belongs in the conversation

Knee replacement is one treatment for some people with advanced knee osteoarthritis. An X-ray by itself does not make that decision. AAOS patient guidance frames surgery as an option after a person and their clinician have looked at symptoms, function, and nonsurgical care. A discussion may include pain that limits life, poor sleep, what reasonable nonsurgical care has already been tried, overall health, and your goals.

Asking about surgery does not mean nonsurgical care failed. Continuing nonsurgical care does not mean surgery is off the table forever. Ask what would make surgery more or less reasonable, what recovery asks of you, and which questions should be answered first. A second opinion is reasonable when the choice is unclear.

If you are not ready for that step, you can still leave with a plan. Agree on what to track, which activities to modify, a rehabilitation goal, a time to reassess, and the findings that would change the next step. Write those points down before you leave.

## Questions to bring to the visit

A short list keeps the visit focused on your knee rather than on a generic menu. You do not need to ask every question. Pick the ones that match where you are.

1. What findings support osteoarthritis, and what else is still possible?
2. Does the imaging match the activities I am struggling with?
3. What is each proposed option meant to do?
4. What benefits, risks, and unknowns should I weigh?
5. What would a fair trial of rehabilitation or activity change look like?
6. How should I respond if symptoms increase?
7. Do my other conditions or medicines change the choices?
8. When should I seek a surgical opinion or another evaluation?
9. How will we know whether the plan is helping the activities I care about?

Bring prior images and a current medicine list if you have them. Also note one activity you most want to protect, such as a walk, a hobby, or getting up from a low chair. That single goal gives the visit a target.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a reasonable knee osteoarthritis treatment before surgery?

There is no single best option for every person. A plan may include rehabilitation, activity changes, a support, a medicine discussion, an injection, or a later surgical opinion. The exam, your health, and your goals decide the mix. Ask what each option is meant to do and how you will judge it.

### Can knee osteoarthritis be managed without surgery?

Many people discuss nonsurgical options first, and AAOS describes that as the initial approach for many patients. Whether those options are reasonable for you is an individual decision. They may support function or symptoms. They are not a promise, and they do not forbid a surgical opinion.

### Does an arthritic knee always need replacement?

No. Osteoarthritis on an image does not by itself mean you need a replacement. Pain, function, sleep, exam findings, overall health, and the response to other care all matter. Discuss timing with the appropriate clinician.

### Should I exercise if my knee already hurts?

Supervised, unsupervised, and aquatic exercise are recommended over no exercise for knee osteoarthritis pain and function in the AAOS guideline. The type and dose still have to match you. Ask how to begin and which symptoms mean the plan should change. Do not start an intense program from an article.

### Are PRP or stem cell injections a cure?

No. Nothing on this page is a cure for osteoarthritis. PRP and stem cell procedures differ in evidence, preparation, risk, and regulation. If one is proposed, ask what is known for your situation, what is uncertain, and which alternatives remain.
