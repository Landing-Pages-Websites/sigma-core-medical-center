Many people think osteoporosis only affects older women, but bone loss can affect men and younger adults, too. Certain medications, hormone changes, low body weight, digestive conditions, kidney disease, thyroid problems, and limited physical activity can all play a role. Because osteoporosis often causes no early symptoms, a fracture may be the first warning sign. Prompt evaluation can uncover weakened bones and possible underlying causes before another injury occurs. Osteoporosis treatment may include medication, vitamin and mineral support, resistance exercise, physical therapy, and fall-prevention strategies. A personalized plan can help you protect your ability to move well and remain independent.

## Key Takeaways

* **Screening can reveal hidden bone loss**: Discuss DXA testing and fracture-risk evaluation if you are over 50, have had a minor-impact fracture, or have risk factors such as steroid use or early menopause.
* **Protect bones through a complete care plan**: Combine prescribed treatment with sufficient calcium, vitamin D, protein, resistance exercise, balance training, and practical fall-prevention steps.
* **Review treatment as your health changes**: Keep your clinician informed about new fractures, falls, side effects, dental procedures, kidney concerns, and changes in strength, pain, or mobility.

## What Is Osteoporosis and Why Treat It?

Osteoporosis is a metabolic bone disease that reduces bone strength and increases the risk of fractures. Bone is living tissue that continually breaks down and rebuilds. When breakdown happens faster than new bone formation, bones can become less dense, more porous, and less able to withstand everyday stress.

The condition often develops gradually, especially after menopause and with advancing age, but it is not an unavoidable part of aging. Men and younger adults can also develop osteoporosis when certain health conditions, medications, or lifestyle factors affect bone health. Early evaluation gives you more time to address risk factors, consider treatment, and protect your ability to walk, exercise, travel, and live independently. The [National Institute of Arthritis and Musculoskeletal and Skin Diseases explains how osteoporosis affects bone strength](https://www.niams.nih.gov/health-topics/osteoporosis).

Treatment focuses on more than improving a bone-density result. It aims to reduce fracture risk, preserve mobility, and help you stay active with greater confidence. Your care plan may include medication, nutrition, exercise, fall prevention, and treatment for an underlying condition that contributes to bone loss.

### Bone density, bone quality, and fracture risk

Bone mineral density, or BMD, measures the amount of mineral in a specific area of bone. A lower BMD usually indicates weaker bones and a higher fracture risk. However, density is only one part of bone strength. Bone quality also includes the internal structure of the bone, its ability to repair small areas of damage, and how well it absorbs force.

Osteoporosis develops when bone loss outpaces bone formation. As the internal structure becomes weaker, a bone may fracture during a minor fall or routine activity. A BMD result should therefore be considered alongside your age, fracture history, medications, health conditions, and other risk factors. The Cleveland Clinic explains the connection between bone density and fracture risk.

### Why osteoporosis shows few early symptoms

Osteoporosis often causes no pain or noticeable changes in its early stages. Bone loss may continue for years without affecting your strength, balance, or daily activities. Many people discover they have osteoporosis only after a fracture caused by a fall or movement that would not normally injure a healthy bone.

Later signs may include losing height, developing a rounded posture, or experiencing new back pain. These changes can result from vertebral fractures, which sometimes occur without obvious symptoms. If you are over 50, have broken a bone after a minor injury, or have risk factors for bone loss, ask a healthcare professional whether screening is appropriate. A lack of symptoms does not necessarily mean your bones are strong.

### Fragility fractures of the hip, spine, wrist, and other sites

A fragility fracture occurs when a bone breaks after relatively minor force, such as a fall from standing height. Common locations include the hip, spine, wrist, pelvis, and upper arm. Vertebral fractures may occur during lifting, bending, coughing, or turning, and some cause little immediate pain.

Any fracture after a minor injury deserves attention, even if it heals quickly or does not require surgery. It may signal reduced bone strength and a higher risk of another fracture. A healthcare professional may recommend a bone-density scan, additional imaging, or tests for contributing health conditions. The [Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation describes common osteoporosis-related fractures](https://www.bonehealthandosteoporosis.org/patients/what-is-osteoporosis/).

### How fractures affect pain, mobility, independence, and longevity

A fracture can affect much more than the injured bone. Hip, spinal, and pelvic fractures may cause significant pain, muscle loss, reduced walking ability, and a lengthy rehabilitation period. Some people need help with bathing, dressing, cooking, driving, managing stairs, or other daily tasks while they recover.

Fractures can also affect confidence. Fear of falling may lead you to avoid movement, which can reduce strength, balance, and endurance. Serious fractures, particularly hip fractures, may result in hospitalization, complications, loss of independence, and poorer long-term health. Treating osteoporosis helps protect the physical abilities that support daily life, not just the number recorded on a scan.

### Osteoporosis in men and younger adults with risk factors

Osteoporosis affects men as well as women. It is more common in older adults, but younger people may also develop low bone density when certain factors interfere with bone formation or increase bone loss. These factors include low body weight, smoking, heavy alcohol use, prolonged corticosteroid treatment, reduced hormone levels, digestive conditions that limit nutrient absorption, and some kidney, thyroid, and inflammatory diseases.

A fracture from a minor injury should not be dismissed because of your age or sex. When bone loss seems unexpected, a clinician may look for an underlying cause by reviewing your medical history, medications, nutrition, hormone health, and other symptoms. The [National Osteoporosis Foundation discusses osteoporosis risk in men and younger adults](https://www.bonehealthandosteoporosis.org/patients/what-is-osteoporosis/).

### Treatment goals: prevent fractures and preserve function

The main goal of osteoporosis treatment is to prevent fractures. Depending on your level of risk, your plan may include medication, adequate calcium and vitamin D, resistance and weight-bearing exercise, balance training, fall prevention, and treatment for a medical condition that weakens bone.

Preserving function is just as important. Bone strength supports safer movement, while muscle strength, balance, posture, gait, and confidence help you remain independent. A personalized plan should account for chronic pain, joint problems, previous fractures, fitness level, medications, and your goals. The [Health in Aging guide to osteoporosis treatment](https://www.healthinaging.org/a-z-topic/osteoporosis/care-treatment) reviews lifestyle measures and medication options that can support stronger bones and safer movement.

## How Do Doctors Diagnose Osteoporosis and Assess Fracture Risk?

Osteoporosis often develops without noticeable symptoms, so doctors use more than one type of information to identify it. The evaluation may include a bone-density scan, fracture history, medical conditions, medication review, and an assessment of fall risk. A low bone-density result is important, but it does not tell the whole story. Someone with osteopenia may still face a high fracture risk if they have already broken a bone, use long-term steroids, or have another condition that affects bone strength.

Your clinician may ask about falls, height loss, back pain, menopause, family history, nutrition, exercise, smoking, and alcohol use. They may also review kidney function, thyroid health, vitamin D status, and medications that affect bone remodeling. The [Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation explains common osteoporosis tests](https://www.bonehealthandosteoporosis.org/patients/diagnosis/) and what patients can expect during an evaluation.

The goal is to determine whether osteoporosis is present, estimate the chance of a future fracture, identify treatable causes, and choose care that supports strength, mobility, and independence.

### When to discuss bone-health screening

Ask about bone-health screening if you are 65 or older, have experienced a fracture after a minor fall or routine activity, or have risk factors for bone loss. The [U.S. Preventive Services Task Force recommends osteoporosis screening for women age 65 and older](https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/osteoporosis-screening), as well as younger postmenopausal women whose fracture risk is similar to that of a 65-year-old woman.

Adults over 50 who sustain a fracture should also discuss bone-density testing with a healthcare professional. A fracture from standing height or less may signal weakened bone, even when the injury seemed relatively minor.

Screening for men, younger adults, and people with complex health conditions is often individualized. Long-term steroid use, early menopause, low body weight, rheumatoid arthritis, digestive disorders, thyroid disease, and a parent’s hip fracture may prompt earlier testing. Your clinician can review your history and recommend the right approach.

### DXA scans of the hip and spine

A dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry scan, usually called a DXA or DEXA scan, is the standard test for measuring bone mineral density. It commonly evaluates the hip and lumbar spine, where bone loss can contribute to serious fractures. The scan is quick, painless, and uses a small amount of radiation.

During the test, you lie on a padded table while the scanner passes over your body. You usually remain fully clothed, although clothing without metal is helpful. The appointment may take 10 to 30 minutes, depending on the equipment and whether additional images are needed.

A DXA report can identify normal bone density, osteopenia, or osteoporosis and provide a baseline for future monitoring. Results should be considered alongside your age, fracture history, medications, and overall health. Because readings can vary between machines, follow-up scans are easier to compare when performed on the same device.

### T-scores, Z-scores, and bone-density results

A T-score compares your bone density with the average peak bone density of a healthy young adult of the same sex. Under World Health Organization categories, a T-score of -1.0 or higher is considered normal. A result between -1.0 and -2.5 indicates osteopenia, while a result of -2.5 or lower indicates osteoporosis. Severe osteoporosis generally means a T-score of -2.5 or lower along with a fragility fracture.

T-scores are mainly used for postmenopausal women and men age 50 or older. A Z-score compares your result with people of the same age, sex, and, when applicable, ethnic background. Clinicians commonly use Z-scores for premenopausal women, men under 50, and children.

A low score does not explain why bone density is reduced or determine treatment by itself. Arthritis, spinal changes, previous fractures, and positioning can affect scan results. Your clinician will interpret the full report rather than focusing on a single number.

### FRAX and other fracture-risk factors

The FRAX tool estimates the likelihood of a hip fracture or major osteoporotic fracture over the next 10 years. It considers age, sex, body mass index, smoking, alcohol use, previous fractures, parental hip fracture, rheumatoid arthritis, steroid treatment, secondary osteoporosis, and femoral-neck bone density. You can access the [official FRAX fracture-risk calculator](https://frax.shef.ac.uk/FRAX/) through the University of Sheffield.

FRAX can help clarify risk when a DXA result falls in the osteopenia range. Two people with the same T-score may have very different risks because one has a previous fracture, uses glucocorticoids, or has a strong family history.

FRAX has limits. It may not fully account for multiple fractures, very recent fractures, frequent falls, medication dose, or rapid bone-density changes. A clinician may adjust the result based on these details. FRAX supports a medical decision, but it does not replace a complete evaluation.

### Vertebral fracture assessment and spinal imaging

Spinal fractures can develop gradually and may not cause sudden, severe pain. Some people notice height loss, a rounded upper back, or recurring back discomfort. Others have no clear symptoms. Vertebral fracture assessment, or VFA, can often be performed during a DXA appointment to identify compression fractures that might otherwise go unnoticed.

If VFA shows a possible fracture, your clinician may order a standard spine X-ray, CT scan, or MRI. These tests can help determine whether the finding is new or old and whether another condition may be causing pain. MRI may be useful when the care team needs to assess bone marrow, nerves, or a recent injury.

Tell your healthcare professional about noticeable height loss, posture changes, or new back pain after lifting, coughing, or a minor fall. A previously unrecognized vertebral fracture can change your risk category and affect treatment decisions.

### Why previous hip or vertebral fractures matter

A previous hip or vertebral fracture is one of the strongest warning signs for another fracture. These injuries may indicate fragile bone, even when a DXA scan does not show a T-score in the osteoporosis range. Treatment is often considered after a hip or spine fracture, particularly when the injury occurred after a low-impact event.

Hip fractures can affect walking ability, balance, confidence, and independence. Vertebral fractures may lead to persistent pain, posture changes, reduced physical capacity, and difficulty with everyday movements. They can also increase the chance of additional fractures.

Make sure your clinician knows about fractures that occurred years ago, including injuries you may have dismissed as part of aging. Wrist, arm, pelvis, rib, and other fractures can also provide useful information. The cause of the injury, the force involved, your age at the time, and your other risk factors all shape the assessment.

### Blood and urine tests for secondary osteoporosis

Bone loss may result from more than aging or menopause. Blood and urine tests can help identify secondary causes of osteoporosis, including vitamin D deficiency, overactive parathyroid glands, thyroid disease, kidney problems, low blood counts, and conditions that interfere with nutrient absorption.

Depending on your history, testing may include calcium, phosphate, alkaline phosphatase, vitamin D, kidney function, thyroid-stimulating hormone, blood count, and parathyroid hormone. Your clinician may also request protein studies, sex-hormone testing, or urine measurements when your symptoms or medical history suggest a specific cause.

Testing is not identical for everyone. The evaluation may be more extensive if you are relatively young, have had several fractures, lose bone quickly, or develop osteoporosis without an obvious explanation. Finding an underlying issue can improve bone care and identify another condition that needs treatment. Avoid starting high-dose supplements before discussing them with your healthcare professional.

### Health conditions and medications that weaken bone

Several health conditions can reduce bone strength or increase the chance of falling. Examples include rheumatoid arthritis, celiac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, chronic kidney disease, diabetes, thyroid or parathyroid disorders, low testosterone, early menopause, and conditions that limit mobility. Reduced muscle strength and poor balance can add to fracture risk even when bone density is only moderately low.

Medication history is equally important. Long-term glucocorticoid use, such as prednisone, can cause relatively rapid bone loss. Other medications may affect bone or increase fall risk, including some seizure medicines, aromatase inhibitors, androgen-deprivation therapies, and sedating drugs. Never stop a prescribed medication on your own. Ask whether the dose, duration, alternatives, or bone-protection plan should be reviewed.

Bring an updated medication and supplement list to your appointment. Include occasional steroid treatments, injections, antacids, and over-the-counter products. This review helps your clinician identify reversible contributors and create a plan that protects bone health while supporting overall function.

## Which Lifestyle Changes Support Osteoporosis Treatment?

Medication can reduce fracture risk, but your daily habits also play an important role in protecting bone health. Nutritious meals provide calcium, vitamin D, protein, and other nutrients your body needs. Strength training supports muscles and bones, while balance work and fall prevention can reduce the chance of an injury.

Lifestyle changes should complement, not replace, medical treatment. Your plan should reflect your bone density, fracture history, age, strength, balance, pain level, and other health conditions. For example, walking may be appropriate for one person, while someone with a vertebral fracture may need supervised, spine-safe exercises first.

If you have osteoporosis, chronic pain, or a previous fracture, speak with your healthcare professional before taking supplements or starting a new exercise program. A physical therapist, registered dietitian, or other qualified professional can help you make changes safely. The [Health in Aging guide to osteoporosis treatment](https://www.healthinaging.org/a-z-topic/osteoporosis/care-treatment) also provides an overview of nutrition, exercise, and other supportive care measures.

### Eat calcium-rich foods and use supplements when appropriate

Calcium is a major building block of bones and teeth. Many older adults need about 1,200 milligrams of calcium each day from food and supplements combined. Helpful food sources include milk, yogurt, cheese, sardines with bones, canned salmon with bones, kale, broccoli, and calcium-set tofu.

Try to meet your needs through food whenever possible. A supplement may help if you have dietary restrictions, lactose intolerance, a low appetite, or another condition that makes it difficult to consume enough calcium. Ask your healthcare professional which supplement is appropriate and how to divide the dose. Taking more than recommended does not necessarily provide more protection and may cause problems for some people.

### Test vitamin D levels and supplement safely

Vitamin D helps your body absorb calcium and supports muscle function, which can affect balance and fall risk. Many older adults need about 800 to 1,000 IU daily, although your ideal amount depends on your diet, sun exposure, health history, medications, and blood level.

Ask your healthcare professional whether a vitamin D blood test is appropriate. If your level is low, you may need a higher dose for a specific period, followed by a maintenance amount. Fortified milk, bread, and juice can also provide vitamin D. Avoid taking high doses without medical guidance, since too much can cause health problems. The [Health in Aging osteoporosis treatment guide](https://www.healthinaging.org/a-z-topic/osteoporosis/care-treatment) explains how testing and supplementation may fit into treatment.

### Eat protein, fruits, and vegetables while maintaining a healthy weight

Calcium is only one part of a bone-supportive diet. Protein helps maintain muscle and supports tissue repair, while fruits and vegetables provide nutrients involved in bone health. Include a protein source at each meal, such as eggs, fish, poultry, beans, lentils, Greek yogurt, or tofu.

Maintaining a healthy weight also matters. Being underweight can increase fracture risk and make recovery more difficult after an injury. If you have lost weight without trying, feel full quickly, or struggle to eat enough, ask your healthcare team for help. A registered dietitian can recommend nourishing meals that fit your preferences and medical needs. Focus on consistent, balanced eating rather than restrictive diets.

### Perform resistance and weight-bearing exercise

Weight-bearing and resistance exercises give bones and muscles a reason to stay strong. Walking, climbing stairs, dancing, and low-impact aerobics can place beneficial stress on the bones of your legs and hips. Strength training with weights, resistance bands, or machines can also improve the muscle support around your hips, spine, and other joints.

A reasonable starting goal may be moderate exercise about three times per week, adjusted to your abilities. Begin with manageable sessions and increase the challenge gradually. Proper form matters, especially if you have joint pain, reduced mobility, or a history of fractures. A physical therapist or qualified exercise professional can design a program that supports strength without unnecessary strain. The [Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation exercise guidance](https://www.bonehealthandosteoporosis.org/healthy-bones-guide/) can help you prepare for that conversation.

### Improve balance, posture, gait, and impact exercise safely

Balance training can help reduce your chance of falling. Tai Chi, supported standing exercises, heel-to-toe walking, and physical therapy drills may help you feel steadier. Posture exercises can strengthen the muscles that support an upright position and may reduce excessive rounding of the upper back.

Some people may benefit from carefully selected impact activities, such as brisk walking or gentle step-ups. However, higher-impact exercise is not appropriate for everyone with osteoporosis, particularly people with vertebral fractures or significant pain. Avoid forceful forward bending, twisting, or sudden jolts unless a trained professional has confirmed they are safe. Ask your healthcare team to assess your gait, balance, posture, and exercise technique before increasing intensity.

### Move safely with chronic pain or vertebral fractures

Pain can make movement feel unsafe, but avoiding activity altogether can lead to weaker muscles, poorer balance, and less independence. The goal is to choose movements that support function without placing unnecessary stress on fragile areas.

If you have a vertebral fracture, your clinician or physical therapist may recommend exercises that keep your spine in a neutral position. Use your legs when lifting, keep objects close to your body, and avoid repeated deep forward bends or forceful twisting. Break demanding tasks into smaller steps and rest before pain becomes severe. Arrange a professional assessment before starting a new exercise program, especially if you have ongoing back pain, height loss, numbness, or a recent fracture. The [National Osteoporosis Foundation movement recommendations](https://www.bonehealthandosteoporosis.org/healthy-bones-guide/) can provide a starting point for discussion.

### Prevent falls at home and during daily activities

Falls are a leading cause of hip, wrist, and other fractures in older adults. Begin with a safety review of your home. Remove loose rugs and clutter, secure electrical cords, keep frequently used items within easy reach, and improve lighting in hallways, stairways, and entrances. Install grab bars in the bathroom and use nonslip mats where needed.

Wear supportive shoes with good traction, and ask whether a cane or walker could make walking safer. Have your vision checked regularly, and ask your clinician to review medications that may cause dizziness or drowsiness. Outside the home, use extra caution on wet, uneven, or icy surfaces. The [Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation’s treatment guidance](https://www.bonehealthandosteoporosis.org/patients/treatment/) includes additional fall-prevention recommendations.

### Address smoking, alcohol, and other bone-health risks

Smoking can interfere with bone health and fracture healing. If you smoke, ask your healthcare professional about a cessation plan that fits your routine. Counseling, medication, and structured support may make quitting more manageable.

Excessive alcohol use can also raise fracture risk by affecting bone health, balance, judgment, and nutrition. Limiting alcohol is particularly important if you take medication that causes drowsiness or have fallen before. Other risks may include prolonged inactivity, poor nutrition, untreated vision problems, and medications that cause dizziness. Review these factors during routine care. The Cleveland Clinic overview of osteoporosis explains how smoking and heavy alcohol use can affect bones and fracture risk.

### Use lifestyle changes alongside osteoporosis medication

Healthy habits support osteoporosis medication, but they do not replace treatment when fracture risk is high. Medication works best when your body has adequate calcium and vitamin D, and when you maintain the strength, balance, and coordination needed to prevent falls.

Continue prescribed medication as directed, even if you feel well. Osteoporosis often causes no obvious symptoms, so feeling stronger does not necessarily mean your fracture risk has resolved. Tell your clinician about side effects, missed doses, supplements, and changes in your health. Do not stop or delay certain medicines without medical guidance, since some treatments require a carefully planned transition. The [Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation medication guidance](https://www.bonehealthandosteoporosis.org/patients/treatment/medicationadherence/) explains why nutrition and lifestyle measures remain important parts of a complete treatment plan.

## Which Medications Treat Osteoporosis?

Osteoporosis medications lower fracture risk by slowing bone breakdown, stimulating new bone formation, or combining both effects. The best option depends on your bone-density results, fracture history, age, overall health, kidney function, other medications, and treatment preferences. Someone with low bone density but no fracture may need a different approach from someone who has had a hip or vertebral fracture.

Medication is one part of a complete bone-health plan. Adequate calcium, vitamin D, protein, resistance exercise, balance training, and fall prevention all support stronger bones and safer movement. Your healthcare professional may also look for medical conditions or medications that contribute to bone loss. The [Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation medication guide](https://www.bonehealthandosteoporosis.org/patients/treatment/medicationadherence/) explains common treatment options and important considerations.

### Antiresorptive and bone-building medications

Osteoporosis medications generally fall into two groups. **Antiresorptive medications** slow the activity of cells that break down old bone. This can help preserve bone density and reduce fracture risk. Bisphosphonates, denosumab, raloxifene, and calcitonin are antiresorptive medications, although each works differently.

**Bone-building medications**, also called anabolic or osteoanabolic therapies, stimulate new bone formation. Teriparatide, abaloparatide, and romosozumab may be considered for people at very high fracture risk, including those with multiple fractures, very low bone density, or a recent spine or hip fracture. These medications are typically prescribed for a limited period, followed by an antiresorptive medication to help maintain the gains.

Treatment guidelines from the Endocrine Society emphasize matching medication choice to fracture risk and reassessing treatment over time. No medication eliminates all fracture risk, so your plan should also address muscle strength, balance, nutrition, and safe movement.

### Bisphosphonates: oral pills and intravenous infusions

Bisphosphonates are often among the first medications considered for postmenopausal women and men with elevated fracture risk. Common options include alendronate, risedronate, ibandronate, and zoledronic acid. These medications reduce the activity of cells that break down bone, which can help preserve bone strength and lower the risk of spinal and other fractures.

Oral bisphosphonates must be taken according to specific instructions. You may need to take the tablet after waking with plain water, remain upright, and wait before eating, drinking other beverages, or taking other medications. These steps help the medicine absorb properly and reduce irritation of the esophagus. People with swallowing problems, certain digestive conditions, or difficulty following the dosing schedule may prefer an infusion.

Zoledronic acid is given through an intravenous infusion, often once a year. Temporary flu-like symptoms can occur after the first dose. Before treatment, your clinician may review kidney function, calcium levels, hydration, and dental health. The FDA information on bisphosphonates explains important benefits and safety considerations.

### Denosumab: scheduled injections that slow bone loss

Denosumab is an antiresorptive medication given as an injection under the skin, commonly every six months. It blocks a protein called RANKL, which reduces the formation and activity of bone-resorbing cells. Denosumab can reduce fracture risk and may suit people who cannot take oral bisphosphonates or prefer a scheduled injection.

Your healthcare professional may check calcium and vitamin D levels before treatment, particularly if you have kidney disease or another condition that affects calcium balance. Low calcium can cause muscle cramps, tingling, numbness, or unusual weakness. Discuss your dental health and any planned dental procedures before beginning treatment.

Do not stop or delay denosumab without a medical plan. Bone loss can occur quickly after a missed dose, and some people develop multiple vertebral fractures after discontinuation. A follow-up antiresorptive medication is often needed when denosumab ends. The FDA prescribing information for Prolia outlines dosing, warnings, and monitoring needs.

### Teriparatide and abaloparatide: bone-building options

Teriparatide and abaloparatide are bone-building medications that act on the parathyroid hormone pathway. Instead of only slowing bone breakdown, they stimulate new bone formation. Clinicians generally reserve them for people at very high fracture risk, including those with severe osteoporosis or multiple vertebral fractures.

Both medications are given by injection, usually on a daily schedule, and treatment lasts for a limited period. Possible effects include dizziness, nausea, headache, leg cramps, or a temporary rise in calcium. Your clinician will review your medical history, laboratory results, and fracture risk before deciding whether one of these options is appropriate.

After the bone-building course ends, treatment usually continues with an antiresorptive medication. This helps preserve the new bone formed during therapy. These medications may not be appropriate for people with certain bone cancers, unexplained high calcium levels, or specific radiation exposure. The [BHOF medication guide](https://www.bonehealthandosteoporosis.org/patients/treatment/medicationadherence/) explains where bone-building therapies fit within osteoporosis care.

### Romosozumab for very high fracture risk

Romosozumab is a bone-building medication that also reduces bone resorption. It blocks sclerostin, a protein that limits bone formation. The medication is given as two injections once a month for up to 12 months. It may be considered for postmenopausal women at very high fracture risk, especially after a recent fracture or when other treatments have not provided enough protection.

Romosozumab is not usually a long-term, stand-alone treatment. Once the course is complete, an antiresorptive medication is generally recommended to help preserve the improvement in bone strength. Your clinician will review your history of heart attack, stroke, and other cardiovascular risks before prescribing it.

Report symptoms such as chest pressure, shortness of breath, sudden weakness, facial drooping, or difficulty speaking immediately. The FDA safety information on romosozumab describes its approved use and cardiovascular warning.

### Raloxifene and other selective estrogen receptor modulators

Raloxifene is a selective estrogen receptor modulator, or SERM. It acts like estrogen in some tissues and blocks estrogen activity in others. For postmenopausal women, it can reduce the risk of vertebral fractures and may be considered when spine protection is the main goal. It does not provide the same protection against hip fractures as some other osteoporosis medications.

Raloxifene may also reduce the risk of certain estrogen-sensitive breast cancers, but it is not suitable for everyone. It can increase the risk of blood clots and may cause hot flashes, leg cramps, or other menopausal symptoms. People with a history of deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, or certain stroke risks may need another option.

Discuss prolonged travel, bed rest, and upcoming surgery with your clinician because long periods of immobility can affect clot risk. The FDA prescribing information for raloxifene includes detailed safety information.

### Menopausal hormone therapy for selected patients

Menopausal hormone therapy can slow bone loss and reduce fractures in some postmenopausal women. It may be considered for people who also need treatment for bothersome hot flashes or other menopausal symptoms, particularly those younger than 60 or within 10 years of menopause who have no major contraindications.

Hormone therapy is not the best choice for every person with osteoporosis. Risks may include blood clots, stroke, gallbladder disease, and certain cancers, depending on the type of therapy, dose, duration, and whether estrogen is combined with a progestogen. A history of breast cancer, unexplained vaginal bleeding, liver disease, clotting disorders, or cardiovascular disease may affect eligibility.

Treatment should be individualized rather than prescribed solely to improve a DXA result. The [North American Menopause Society position statement](https://menopause.org/wp-content/uploads/press-release/ht-position-statement-release.pdf) offers guidance for discussing benefits, risks, and alternatives with a qualified clinician.

### Calcitonin, nasal irritation, and its limited role

Calcitonin is a hormone involved in calcium regulation and bone turnover. An injectable or nasal form may provide modest protection against vertebral fractures, but it is not considered a preferred first-line treatment for osteoporosis. Its ability to prevent hip and other nonvertebral fractures is limited.

Calcitonin may be considered for selected postmenopausal women when other treatments are unsuitable or cannot be tolerated. A nasal spray can cause irritation, dryness, crusting, nosebleeds, or changes in smell. Your clinician may recommend checking the nasal passages periodically, especially if you have frequent irritation or a history of nasal problems.

Calcitonin is sometimes used for short-term relief after an acute vertebral compression fracture, although it does not replace a long-term fracture-prevention plan. Because more effective options are available for many patients, discuss its benefits and limitations carefully. The [BHOF treatment guidance](https://www.bonehealthandosteoporosis.org/patients/treatment/medicationadherence/) describes calcitonin’s limited role among current medication choices.

### Calcium and vitamin D: treatment support, not substitutes

Calcium and vitamin D support bone health, but they do not replace osteoporosis medication when medication is indicated. Calcium helps form and maintain bone, while vitamin D helps the body absorb calcium. Your clinician may review your diet, supplement use, vitamin D level, kidney function, and kidney-stone history before recommending an amount.

Food is usually the preferred source of calcium. Options include dairy products, calcium-set tofu, canned salmon or sardines with bones, and fortified plant milks. If your diet does not provide enough, a supplement may help. Taking more than recommended does not make bones stronger and may cause constipation or contribute to kidney stone risk in some people.

Vitamin D supplements should also be used thoughtfully. Excessive intake can lead to high calcium levels and other health problems. Ask your healthcare professional which dose is appropriate, especially if you have kidney disease, malabsorption, or take medications that affect calcium. The [National Institutes of Health calcium and vitamin D fact sheets](https://ods.od.nih.gov/factsheets/Calcium-HealthProfessional/) provide evidence-based intake and safety information.

## What Side Effects and Safety Risks Can Osteoporosis Medications Cause?

Osteoporosis medications can lower fracture risk, but every treatment has potential side effects and safety considerations. The best option depends on your fracture history, bone density, kidney function, calcium and vitamin D levels, dental health, cardiovascular history, other medications, and personal preferences.

Many side effects can be managed with correct dosing, regular monitoring, and clear communication with your healthcare team. Before starting treatment, ask how the medication should be taken, which tests you need, and what symptoms should prompt a call. Keep your clinician informed about new health conditions, dental procedures, falls, and changes to your medications or supplements.

Do not stop osteoporosis treatment without medical guidance. This is especially important with denosumab, since delaying or stopping injections without a follow-up plan can lead to rapid bone loss and a higher risk of vertebral fractures. The [Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation](https://www.bonehealthandosteoporosis.org/patients/treatment/medicationadherence/) provides practical information about medication adherence and safety.

### Oral bisphosphonate digestive effects and dosing rules

Oral bisphosphonates, including alendronate and risedronate, may cause heartburn, indigestion, nausea, stomach discomfort, or irritation of the esophagus. Taking these medications incorrectly can make digestive symptoms more likely.

Most oral bisphosphonates should be taken first thing in the morning, on an empty stomach, with a full glass of plain water. Avoid food, coffee, juice, and other medications for the period listed in your prescription instructions. Remain upright, either sitting or standing, for at least 30 minutes after taking the medication. This helps reduce reflux and esophageal irritation.

Tell your clinician if you have trouble swallowing, severe heartburn, chest pain, or a known esophageal condition. Do not change the dose or timing without asking your healthcare team. The FDA prescribing information for alendronate explains administration instructions and important warnings.

### Intravenous bisphosphonate reactions and kidney checks

Intravenous bisphosphonates, such as zoledronic acid, avoid the digestive tract but may cause short-term flu-like symptoms after an infusion. Fever, chills, muscle aches, joint pain, headache, and fatigue are more common after the first treatment. These reactions are often temporary, but ask your infusion team which measures are safe for you.

Your clinician should check kidney function before treatment. They may also review your hydration, calcium, and vitamin D levels, because kidney disease and mineral deficiencies can increase the risk of complications. Tell the infusion team if you have kidney problems, are dehydrated, or take medications that affect kidney function.

Your care team may give specific instructions about fluids before and after treatment. Do not assume an infusion is appropriate simply because oral bisphosphonates cause digestive symptoms. The National Kidney Foundation explains why osteoporosis treatment requires additional planning for people with chronic kidney disease.

### Denosumab, low calcium, injection-site effects, and missed doses

Denosumab is given by injection on a regular schedule, commonly every six months. Potential side effects include injection-site redness or discomfort, muscle or joint pain, skin reactions, and low blood calcium. The risk of hypocalcemia is higher in people with advanced kidney disease, vitamin D deficiency, or other mineral-balance problems.

Your clinician may check calcium, vitamin D, and kidney health before treatment. Take calcium and vitamin D only as directed, since the right amount depends on your diet, laboratory results, kidney function, and other medications.

Do not delay or stop denosumab without a follow-up plan. Bone loss can return quickly after the medication wears off, and some people develop vertebral fractures after an unplanned interruption. Contact your medical team as soon as possible if you may miss an injection. The FDA safety communication on Prolia outlines serious hypocalcemia risks and monitoring considerations.

### Rare atypical femur fractures and thigh or groin pain

Atypical femur fractures are uncommon but serious breaks that can occur in the thigh bone, sometimes after little or no trauma. They have been reported with long-term bisphosphonate use and with denosumab. Unlike typical hip fractures, these injuries may begin with warning symptoms before a complete break occurs.

Report new, persistent, or unexplained pain in your thigh, hip, or groin to your clinician. Do not wait for the pain to become severe or for difficulty walking to develop. Your clinician may order imaging of the painful leg and, in some cases, the opposite leg because these fractures can affect both femurs.

Do not stop treatment on your own. Your healthcare team can determine whether you need imaging, a medication change, or another approach to fracture prevention. The American Society for Bone and Mineral Research offers expert resources on atypical femur fractures and other bone-health concerns.

### Rare osteonecrosis of the jaw and dental planning

Medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw is rare among people taking osteoporosis doses of bisphosphonates or denosumab. Risk may be higher with cancer treatment doses, invasive dental procedures, gum disease, poor oral hygiene, smoking, and certain medical conditions. The condition involves jawbone that does not heal normally and may cause pain, swelling, drainage, loose teeth, or numbness.

Maintain regular dental care and tell your dentist about every osteoporosis medication you take. If you need an extraction, implant, or another invasive procedure, ask your dentist and prescribing clinician to coordinate your care. They can discuss the procedure, infection prevention, and whether any medication adjustment is appropriate.

Do not stop treatment solely because you need dental work unless your clinician recommends it. Stopping some medications may create other risks, including increased bone loss. The American Dental Association explains dental considerations for people taking osteoporosis medications.

### Romosozumab and cardiovascular precautions

Romosozumab is a bone-building medication used for selected patients with very high fracture risk. It carries a boxed warning about a possible increased risk of heart attack, stroke, and cardiovascular death. It should not be started in someone who has had a heart attack or stroke within the previous year.

Before treatment, discuss high blood pressure, diabetes, high cholesterol, smoking, prior heart disease, stroke, and peripheral artery disease. Your clinician will weigh these risks against the danger of another fracture and consider whether a different medication would be safer.

Seek emergency care for chest pressure, sudden shortness of breath, facial drooping, weakness on one side, or trouble speaking. Romosozumab is generally given for a limited course, after which another osteoporosis medication is usually needed to maintain bone protection. The FDA prescribing information for Evenity includes the cardiovascular warning and treatment information.

### Raloxifene and blood-clot or stroke risks

Raloxifene may reduce vertebral fracture risk in some postmenopausal women, but it can increase the risk of blood clots, including deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism. It may also increase the risk of fatal stroke in women who already have a high risk of stroke. People with a previous blood clot generally should not take raloxifene unless a specialist determines that the benefits outweigh the risks.

Tell your clinician about clotting disorders, stroke, prolonged immobility, smoking, and upcoming surgery. You may receive instructions to temporarily stop raloxifene before a period of extended bed rest or surgery.

Get urgent medical attention for one-sided leg swelling, sudden chest pain, unexplained shortness of breath, or new neurological symptoms. Raloxifene can also cause hot flashes and leg cramps, so mention these effects during follow-up. The [MedlinePlus raloxifene information](https://medlineplus.gov/druginfo/meds/a698007.html) outlines key precautions and warning signs.

### Hormone therapy risks and safety screening

Menopausal hormone therapy may help prevent bone loss in some women, especially when menopause symptoms are also affecting sleep, comfort, or quality of life. It is not appropriate for everyone. Depending on the treatment and a person’s health history, risks may include blood clots, stroke, gallbladder disease, breast cancer, and endometrial cancer.

Estrogen alone and combined estrogen-progestin therapy have different risk profiles. A safety review should include personal and family history of breast or endometrial cancer, unexplained vaginal bleeding, blood clots, stroke, liver disease, and cardiovascular conditions. Your clinician may also review your age, time since menopause, symptoms, and fracture risk.

When hormone therapy is selected, clinicians generally consider the lowest effective dose for an appropriate duration. If bone protection is your main goal, ask whether a nonhormonal osteoporosis medication would be a better fit. The [North American Menopause Society](https://menopause.org/for-women/menopauseflashes/menopause-topics/hormone-therapy) provides patient information about hormone therapy benefits, risks, and eligibility.

### Bone-building medication effects and treatment limits

Teriparatide and abaloparatide stimulate new bone formation and may be prescribed for people at very high fracture risk. Potential side effects include dizziness, nausea, headache, muscle cramps, and temporary increases in blood calcium. Standing up slowly after the first doses may help reduce lightheadedness or fainting.

These medications are used for a limited period, generally up to two years over a person’s lifetime, based on individual treatment history and current guidance. Tell your clinician if you have kidney stones, high blood calcium, or a condition affecting the parathyroid glands.

Bone density may decline after a bone-building course ends. For that reason, clinicians commonly follow treatment with an antiresorptive medication to help preserve the bone gained. Your care team should explain the full sequence before you begin, including the next medication and the timing of that transition. The Endocrine Society guideline reviews medication selection and sequencing for postmenopausal osteoporosis.

### Symptoms that need prompt medical attention

Contact your healthcare team promptly if you develop new thigh, hip, or groin pain, difficulty swallowing, severe heartburn, jaw pain, loose teeth, gum drainage, or an unexplained wound in your mouth. These symptoms do not always indicate a serious medication complication, but they deserve evaluation.

Report muscle spasms, tingling around the mouth, unusual weakness, confusion, or seizures, which can occur with severe low calcium. If you receive denosumab and develop these symptoms, seek medical advice promptly, especially if you have kidney disease or have been told your calcium is low.

Seek emergency care for chest pain, sudden shortness of breath, one-sided weakness, facial drooping, trouble speaking, or swelling and pain in one leg. After a fall, persistent back pain, sudden height loss, or difficulty bearing weight may indicate a fracture. Keep an updated list of medications, injections, supplements, side effects, falls, and dental procedures so your care team can make informed treatment decisions.

## How Do Healthcare Professionals Individualize Osteoporosis Treatment?

Osteoporosis treatment is not a one-size-fits-all plan. Your clinician considers your fracture history, bone density, age, overall health, medications, kidney function, dental needs, and daily routine before recommending treatment. Two people with similar DXA results may need different approaches because one has kidney disease, another has digestive problems, and a third has already experienced a spinal fracture.

Your goals matter, too. You may want to stay independent, return to hiking, lift weights, travel comfortably, or manage pain without giving up the activities you enjoy. These priorities can shape recommendations for medication, physical therapy, exercise, nutrition, and fall prevention.

Treatment decisions also involve practical details. Some medications are taken weekly or monthly, while others require an injection or infusion at a clinic. Reviewing these options together can help you choose a plan that feels manageable and medically appropriate. Research suggests that [shared decision-making can improve long-term commitment to osteoporosis treatment](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00198-022-06453-4).

### Share decisions and discuss treatment preferences

An osteoporosis consultation should be a conversation, not simply a prescription being handed over. Your clinician can explain your fracture risk, treatment choices, expected benefits, possible side effects, and likely treatment duration. You can then discuss concerns about injections, digestive effects, dental procedures, appointment schedules, or medication costs.

Be honest about what you can follow consistently. If taking a weekly tablet feels difficult, an infusion or scheduled injection may be more practical. If you prefer to avoid a particular medication, ask whether another option is safe for you. A treatment plan works best when it reflects both your health needs and your informed preferences.

### Review fracture history, DXA results, and FRAX scores

Your clinician will ask about previous fractures, especially hip or spine fractures that occurred after a minor fall or routine movement. These injuries can indicate fragile bones even when your bone-density result does not meet the usual osteoporosis range. The location, cause, timing, and recovery from each fracture can affect treatment decisions.

A DXA scan measures bone mineral density, usually at the hip and spine. Your clinician may also use the [FRAX fracture-risk calculator](https://www.sheffield.ac.uk/FRAX/) to estimate your 10-year risk of a hip or major osteoporotic fracture. FRAX includes factors such as age, sex, smoking, alcohol use, steroid treatment, previous fractures, and bone density. Your care team considers all of these findings together.

### Consider age, sex, menopause status, and overall health

Age affects fracture risk, fall risk, medication choices, and the potential benefits of long-term treatment. Your clinician may also consider sex, menopause status, body weight, family history, physical activity, and whether your bone density has changed quickly. Men can develop osteoporosis, particularly when certain medical conditions or medications are involved.

Overall health is just as important. Heart disease, kidney problems, digestive disorders, cancer history, dental concerns, and other conditions may make some treatments less suitable. The [Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation explains how age, sex, bone loss, and general health influence medication selection](https://www.bonehealthandosteoporosis.org/patients/treatment/).

### Review kidney function, digestive health, and dental needs

Some osteoporosis medications depend on healthy kidney function, so your clinician may order blood tests before treatment and during follow-up. Kidney function can affect whether a medication is appropriate, how it is given, or whether another option would be safer. Never assume a medication is suitable because it worked for someone else.

Digestive health also matters. Oral bisphosphonates may irritate the esophagus or stomach and require specific instructions, such as taking the tablet with plain water and remaining upright afterward. Tell your care team about planned dental extractions, implants, or other invasive procedures so your medical and dental providers can coordinate care. Reviewing these concerns early supports [safer osteoporosis medication planning](https://www.bonehealthandosteoporosis.org/patients/treatment/medicationadherence/).

### Assess cardiovascular, clotting, and cancer-related risks

Some osteoporosis treatments require a careful review of cardiovascular health. A history of heart attack or stroke may affect whether a particular medication is appropriate. Other treatments can increase blood-clot risk, making a history of deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, or a clotting disorder important to discuss.

Hormone-related treatments also require screening for breast cancer risk, uterine health, stroke, and clotting. Your clinician may ask about abnormal bleeding, cancer history, migraines, smoking, blood pressure, and family history. [Health in Aging outlines safety considerations for osteoporosis treatments](https://www.healthinaging.org/a-z-topic/osteoporosis/care-treatment), but your personal history determines which options are appropriate.

### Review steroid use, hormone changes, and secondary osteoporosis

Long-term corticosteroid use can weaken bone, even when the medication treats a condition unrelated to the skeleton. Tell your clinician about steroid pills, injections, inhalers, and creams, including the dose and how long you have used them. Do not stop a prescribed steroid suddenly. Your clinician can determine whether you need a different dose or additional bone protection.

Your evaluation may also look for secondary osteoporosis, which develops because of another medical condition or medication. Thyroid disorders, low sex hormones, malabsorption, kidney disease, liver disease, inflammatory conditions, and certain cancer treatments can affect bone strength. Identifying an underlying contributor allows your care team to address more than the DXA result.

### Choose treatment for high or very high fracture risk

Medication is commonly recommended when fracture risk is high. This may include people with a hip or vertebral fracture, a DXA T-score in the osteoporosis range, or a high estimated risk based on clinical factors. A recent fracture or multiple fractures may place someone in a very high-risk group and lead to consideration of a bone-building medication.

Treatment sequence can also matter. Some people begin with a medication that builds bone, followed by an antiresorptive medication that helps preserve those gains. Others may start with a bisphosphonate or denosumab based on their health profile and fracture risk. Endocrine Society guidance explains how risk level can guide medication selection and follow-up.

### Match the medication route, schedule, cost, and coverage

The most suitable medication needs to fit both your medical needs and your routine. Oral tablets may work well if you can follow the dosing instructions and have no major digestive concerns. An injection or infusion may be more practical if remembering pills is difficult, swallowing is uncomfortable, or digestive side effects are a concern.

Cost and insurance coverage belong in the discussion, too. Ask about your expected out-of-pocket cost, whether prior authorization is needed, and how often treatment is given. Find out what to do if you miss a dose or cannot attend an appointment. The Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation recommends reviewing [medication coverage and treatment costs](https://www.bonehealthandosteoporosis.org/patients/treatment/) before making a final decision.

### Align treatment with strength, mobility, pain, and independence goals

Preventing a fracture is central, but your plan should also support the life you want to lead. Tell your clinician if you want to return to hiking, play with grandchildren, travel, lift weights, reduce back pain, or remain independent at home. These goals may guide referrals to physical therapy, exercise specialists, pain care, or fall-prevention services.

Medication does not replace movement and functional care. Your plan may include progressive resistance training, safe weight-bearing activity, balance exercises, protein-rich nutrition, and strategies for managing joint or spinal pain. If pain or a previous fracture makes exercise difficult, ask how to begin safely. Your care team can adjust activity as your strength and mobility improve.

### Prepare questions for an osteoporosis consultation

Bring a written question list and an updated medication list to your appointment. Include prescriptions, over-the-counter products, supplements, steroid use, allergies, previous fractures, dental procedures, and relevant family history. Also note recent falls, height loss, new back pain, or changes in walking ability.

Useful questions include:

* What is my current fracture risk?
* Do I need medication, and what alternatives are available?
* Why do you recommend this treatment for me?
* How is it taken, and how long might I use it?
* Which side effects require a call to the office?
* What should I do if I miss a dose?
* Do I need blood tests, dental planning, or a repeat DXA scan?
* Which exercises are safe with my pain or fracture history?
* What happens if this treatment does not work for me?

Ask when you should follow up and who to contact with concerns. Clear instructions make it easier to take an active role in your bone-health plan.

## How Long Should Osteoporosis Treatment Continue?

Osteoporosis treatment does not follow one fixed timeline. The right duration depends on your fracture risk, bone density, age, other health conditions, medication type, and whether you have experienced a fracture during treatment. Some people need ongoing therapy, while others may be candidates for a carefully monitored treatment break after several years of bisphosphonate use.

Your clinician should create a plan with a start date, follow-up schedule, and clear criteria for changing treatment. This plan may include medication reviews, blood tests, repeat DXA scans, and discussions about falls, side effects, and changes in mobility. The [Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation’s treatment guidance](https://www.bonehealthandosteoporosis.org/patients/treatment/) offers an overview of medication options and follow-up considerations.

Treatment duration also depends on how well the medication is working. Stable or improving bone density is encouraging, but it is only one part of the assessment. Your clinician will also consider new fractures, height loss, back pain, falls, kidney function, medication tolerance, and changes in your overall health.

Never stop or delay osteoporosis medication without medical guidance. Some medications may be paused safely in selected patients, while others require a planned transition to another treatment. The sections below explain how clinicians commonly approach these decisions.

### Set initial treatment periods and reassessment dates

Before starting treatment, ask how long the initial course is expected to last and when your progress will be reviewed. The answer may differ if you have a recent fracture, very low bone density, long-term steroid use, or another condition that raises fracture risk.

Your plan may include follow-up visits, blood tests, medication reviews, and a repeat DXA scan after a specific period. Setting these dates in advance makes appointments easier to remember and gives you regular opportunities to discuss side effects, missed doses, falls, and changes in strength or mobility.

Shared decision-making also matters. Research on [supporting patients with osteoporosis treatment](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00198-022-06453-4) suggests that clear information and patient involvement can support long-term commitment. Bring a written list of questions to each appointment, especially if you are unsure whether treatment is working or whether your schedule should change.

### Determine bisphosphonate duration and treatment breaks

Bisphosphonates, including alendronate, risedronate, ibandronate, and zoledronic acid, remain in bone after treatment. Because of this lasting effect, some people with lower or moderate fracture risk may be able to take a monitored break after several years of therapy.

A treatment break, sometimes called a drug holiday, is not the same as ending care. Your clinician may continue to review your fracture history, symptoms, and bone density during the break. If your bone density declines, you experience a new fracture, or your risk increases, treatment may need to restart or change.

People at very high fracture risk may need a longer course or a different medication strategy. Do not decide on a break based only on how you feel, because osteoporosis often causes no symptoms until a fracture occurs. The [Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation’s medication guidance](https://www.bonehealthandosteoporosis.org/patients/treatment/medicationadherence/) explains why treatment breaks require ongoing monitoring.

### Schedule denosumab doses and plan transitions

Denosumab is given as an injection every six months. Keeping each dose on schedule is especially important because bone loss can accelerate when treatment is delayed or stopped without follow-up therapy.

Before receiving denosumab, ask how your appointments will be tracked and what to do if illness, travel, insurance changes, or scheduling problems interfere with your next injection. If you are considering stopping, discuss the next medication before the final dose is due. A transition plan often includes another antiresorptive medication to help protect your bones.

Do not assume that a missed injection can simply be skipped. The [Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation explains denosumab timing and discontinuation concerns](https://www.bonehealthandosteoporosis.org/patients/treatment/medicationadherence/). Contact your healthcare professional promptly if you may miss an appointment.

### Follow bone-building therapy with an antiresorptive

Bone-building medications, such as teriparatide and abaloparatide, are generally prescribed for a defined treatment period. They may be appropriate for people with very high fracture risk, multiple fractures, or substantial bone loss.

When the bone-building course ends, your clinician may recommend an antiresorptive medication. This follow-up treatment helps preserve the bone-density gains made during the first phase. Without a maintenance plan, some of that progress may be lost over time.

Ask when the next medication should begin and whether you need blood tests or other preparation. Your treatment sequence may also depend on previous medications, kidney function, fracture history, and insurance coverage. The Endocrine Society’s osteoporosis guideline describes the importance of continued treatment and follow-up for people with elevated fracture risk.

### Reassess after a new fracture or continued bone loss

A fracture during treatment does not automatically mean the medication failed, but it does require a careful review. Your clinician may check whether doses were taken correctly, whether any were missed, and whether another health condition or medication is weakening your bones.

The location and severity of the fracture also matter. A hip or vertebral fracture may place you in a higher-risk category and lead to a different treatment approach. Continued bone loss on follow-up DXA testing, particularly alongside fractures or other risk factors, also deserves attention.

Report new back pain, noticeable height loss, a fall, or pain in the hip, thigh, or groin. Regular [osteoporosis follow-up and bone-density monitoring](https://www.bonehealthandosteoporosis.org/patients/treatment/medicationadherence/) can help your care team identify when treatment needs to continue, change, or become more intensive.

### Coordinate treatment with dental work or surgery

Tell your dentist, oral surgeon, and medical team about your osteoporosis medication before an extraction, implant, or other invasive dental procedure. Antiresorptive medications have been associated with a rare jaw complication called medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw. Good dental care is a sensible precaution.

Do not stop medication on your own before dental work. The decision depends on the medication, treatment duration, fracture risk, type of procedure, and your dental health. In many cases, the risk of an osteoporotic fracture from stopping treatment may outweigh the dental risk.

Schedule routine dental checkups, address infections promptly, and maintain careful oral hygiene. The [American Dental Association’s guidance on osteoporosis medications](https://www.ada.org/resources/ada-library/oral-health-topics/osteoporosis-medications) explains the main considerations, but your dentist and prescribing clinician should make recommendations for your situation.

### Make medication changes with medical supervision

Your healthcare professional should guide decisions about starting, stopping, switching, or pausing osteoporosis medication. A pharmacist can also help you review dosing instructions, medication interactions, side effects, and practical ways to remember each dose.

Before your appointment, write down every prescription, supplement, and over-the-counter medication you take. Include missed doses, new symptoms, falls, dental procedures, and changes in kidney or digestive health. Ask which symptoms should prompt a call and what to do if you cannot take a medication as scheduled.

Treatment changes should account for both fracture protection and your daily goals. If side effects, cost, travel, or difficulty swallowing make a medication hard to continue, tell your care team. Another medication, route, or schedule may fit better while continuing to protect your bones.

## How Do You Monitor Bone Health and Osteoporosis Treatment?

Monitoring osteoporosis involves more than checking whether your bone density has changed. Your healthcare team may also review fractures, falls, height, back symptoms, laboratory results, medication use, strength, balance, and daily function. Together, these details provide a clearer picture of whether treatment is reducing fracture risk and helping you remain active and independent.

A DXA scan is an important part of follow-up care, but it does not tell the whole story. Someone may have stable bone density and still experience a fracture. Another person may show a small change that falls within the normal variation of the test. Your results should be considered alongside your fracture history, health conditions, medications, and movement goals. The Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation offers resources to help you prepare for appointments.

Regular monitoring can also identify concerns early. A missed dose, low vitamin D level, change in kidney function, new fall, or symptom such as thigh pain may affect your care plan. Keep an updated list of medications, supplements, fractures, falls, and questions. Share this information at each visit, and contact your healthcare professional if you develop a new fracture or concerning symptom.

### Schedule follow-up DXA scans and compare the same machine

Your clinician may recommend follow-up DXA scans to measure bone mineral density at the hip and lumbar spine. The timing depends on your fracture risk, treatment, age, and previous results. Some people need an earlier scan, while others can wait longer. Your healthcare professional can recommend a schedule based on your individual needs.

When possible, have follow-up scans performed on the same machine and at the same facility. Different scanners may produce slightly different results, making comparisons less reliable. Ask whether your report includes the least significant change, which helps determine whether a difference is likely to represent a true change rather than normal testing variation. A DXA scan overview from Cleveland Clinic explains what the test measures and how it is performed.

### Compare bone density with actual fracture outcomes

A DXA result is only one part of fracture-risk monitoring. Your clinician will ask whether you have broken a bone since your last visit, especially after a fall from standing height or another minor injury. A new fracture may indicate that your current treatment needs review, even when your bone density has remained stable.

Your care team may also use the [FRAX fracture-risk assessment tool](https://frax.shef.ac.uk/FRAX/) to estimate your 10-year probability of a hip fracture or major osteoporotic fracture. FRAX considers factors such as age, sex, previous fractures, smoking, alcohol use, rheumatoid arthritis, steroid exposure, and femoral-neck bone density. The estimate supports clinical judgment, but it does not replace a personalized evaluation.

### Track new fractures, height loss, and back pain

Tell your clinician about any new fracture, even if it followed an everyday activity or seemed minor. Hip, wrist, upper-arm, and vertebral fractures can change your risk assessment and may call for a different treatment approach. Do not assume a fracture is unrelated to osteoporosis because the injury did not seem severe.

Also report noticeable height loss, a newly stooped posture, or ongoing back pain. Vertebral compression fractures can cause mild symptoms or no sudden pain, so they may go unnoticed. Losing about an inch or more in height can be a warning sign, particularly when combined with back discomfort. Cleveland Clinic lists height loss, posture changes, and fractures after minor injuries among signs worth discussing with a healthcare professional.

### Monitor calcium, vitamin D, and kidney function

Calcium and vitamin D support bone maintenance and help some osteoporosis medications work safely. Your clinician may review your diet, supplements, vitamin D level, blood calcium, and kidney function. The right supplement dose depends on your diet, laboratory results, kidney health, and other medications. Taking more than recommended is not automatically better.

Kidney testing is especially important before certain treatments, including intravenous bisphosphonates. Your clinician may also check calcium before medications that can lower blood calcium in some patients. Ask whether you need to separate calcium supplements from other medicines, since calcium can affect the absorption of certain drugs. [HealthInAging.org’s osteoporosis guidance](https://www.healthinaging.org/a-z-topic/osteoporosis/care-treatment) covers calcium, vitamin D, exercise, and treatment support.

### Use bone-turnover markers when helpful

Bone-turnover markers are blood or urine tests that provide information about how quickly bone is being broken down and rebuilt. They are not necessary for everyone with osteoporosis, and they do not replace DXA scans or fracture-risk assessment. However, your clinician may use them when the treatment response is unclear or medication adherence is a concern.

These tests may show whether bone breakdown has slowed after an antiresorptive medication or whether bone formation has increased with a bone-building treatment. Results can vary based on timing, food intake, kidney function, and the test used. Your care team may repeat the same test under similar conditions to make results easier to compare. This review of [bone-turnover markers in osteoporosis care](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9679236/) explains how they may support treatment decisions.

### Follow medication schedules and administration instructions

Taking osteoporosis medication correctly matters. Oral bisphosphonates, for example, are generally taken in the morning on an empty stomach with a full glass of plain water. You may need to remain upright and wait before eating, drinking anything besides water, or taking other medications. Follow the instructions for your specific prescription because directions can differ.

Some treatments require an injection or infusion on a fixed schedule. Do not delay or stop medication without medical guidance, particularly denosumab. Missing or stopping denosumab without a follow-up plan can cause bone breakdown to rise quickly and may increase fracture risk. If you cannot keep an appointment or are unsure what to do after a missed dose, contact your healthcare team promptly. The [Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation medication guide](https://www.bonehealthandosteoporosis.org/patients/treatment/medicationadherence/) offers guidance on adherence.

### Record side effects, missed doses, falls, and questions

A simple health record can make follow-up visits more useful. Write down each medication, dose, administration date, missed dose, side effect, fall, new pain, and change in mobility. Include vitamins, supplements, and nonprescription medicines because they may affect treatment or laboratory results.

For each fall, note where it happened, whether you felt dizzy, and whether you were injured. Record new jaw symptoms, swallowing problems, digestive symptoms, thigh or groin pain, or unusual weakness. These symptoms can have several causes, but sharing them helps your clinician decide whether further evaluation is needed. Bring your list to every appointment and ask which symptoms require a prompt call or urgent care.

### Assess strength, balance, gait, and fall risk

Osteoporosis care should include steps that reduce the chance of falling. Your clinician or physical therapist may assess leg strength, walking pattern, posture, balance, footwear, vision, and how safely you move from sitting to standing. These checks can identify risks that a DXA scan cannot show.

A tailored exercise plan may include resistance training, weight-bearing activity, balance work, and posture exercises. If you have significant pain, a vertebral fracture, joint disease, or a history of falls, seek guidance before adding impact exercise or heavier weights. A physical therapist can modify movements for your abilities and teach safer ways to bend, turn, climb stairs, and carry items. [HealthInAging.org recommends balance and strengthening activities](https://www.healthinaging.org/a-z-topic/osteoporosis/care-treatment) as part of osteoporosis care and fall prevention.

### Adjust treatment when monitoring shows a need

Your treatment plan may change if monitoring identifies a new fracture, continued bone loss, medication side effects, poor tolerance, kidney-function changes, or difficulty following the dosing schedule. Your clinician may repeat imaging, investigate an underlying condition, change the medication route, or recommend another type of osteoporosis treatment.

The decision should reflect your fracture risk, age, overall health, kidney function, other conditions, medication preferences, cost, and ability to follow the schedule. If you experience a new hip or vertebral fracture while receiving treatment, contact your healthcare team rather than waiting for a routine appointment. This [review of osteoporosis treatment options](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5768298/) explains why treatment decisions should be individualized.

## How Can Osteoporosis Treatment Help You Stay Active and Independent?

Osteoporosis treatment is about more than improving a bone-density result. Its broader purpose is to reduce fracture risk while helping you preserve strength, mobility, confidence, and control over daily life. A complete plan may include medication, nutrition, safe exercise, physical therapy, fall prevention, and regular follow-up. Together, these strategies can support the abilities you rely on for walking, climbing stairs, traveling, working, and spending time with family and friends.

Staying active with osteoporosis requires the right type and amount of movement. Exercise should reflect your fracture risk, current strength, joint health, balance, pain level, and medical history. The [American Bone Health exercise guidance](https://www.americanbonehealth.org/exercise/) highlights the value of resistance, balance, posture, and weight-bearing activities, while also noting that certain movements may need to be modified. Your healthcare team can help you build a plan that challenges your body safely and supports your long-term goals.

### Build strength, weight-bearing capacity, balance, and posture

Resistance and weight-bearing exercise can support the muscles and bones needed for everyday movement. Walking, stair climbing, and strength training may help you rise from a chair, carry groceries, and move confidently around your home. Balance and posture exercises are also important because better body control can reduce fall risk and help you respond if you lose your footing.

A common goal is moderate exercise several times per week, but the appropriate intensity depends on your fracture risk and physical condition. Start with professional guidance if you have significant bone loss, a recent fracture, severe pain, or balance concerns. [HealthInAging.org recommends weight-bearing activity and strength training](https://www.healthinaging.org/a-z-topic/osteoporosis/care-treatment), but your program should be adjusted to your abilities and treatment goals.

### Modify exercise for joint problems, chronic pain, or fractures

Osteoporosis and arthritis often occur together, and chronic pain can make standard exercise routines difficult. You may need lower-impact options, shorter sessions, extra rest, or equipment that reduces stress on painful joints. Stationary cycling, water exercise, supported walking, and carefully selected resistance movements may work well for some people. A clinician or physical therapist can help identify the safest choices for you.

Certain movements may increase fracture risk when bone loss is advanced, particularly forceful bending, twisting, or repeated rounding of the spine. Yoga and Pilates are not automatically unsafe, but some positions may need to be changed or avoided. [HealthInAging.org advises people with major bone loss to seek professional advice](https://www.healthinaging.org/a-z-topic/osteoporosis/care-treatment) before starting these activities. New, severe, or persistent pain should be evaluated rather than dismissed as a normal response to exercise.

### Use physical therapy to improve gait, balance, and movement

A physical therapist can assess how you walk, stand, turn, reach, and recover your balance. This evaluation may identify movement patterns that increase fall risk or place extra strain on your spine, hips, or knees. Your therapist can then recommend exercises to improve leg and core strength, coordination, posture, and movement control.

Physical therapy may also teach you how to use a cane, walker, brace, or other support correctly. Treatment can address weakness after a fracture, fear of falling, reduced endurance, and difficulty with household tasks. The Bone Health and Osteoporosis Foundation explains physical therapy’s role in supporting posture, balance, and safe movement. Ask whether an in-person assessment is appropriate before increasing exercise independently.

### Protect the spine during daily activities

Spinal compression fractures can cause sudden back pain, height loss, a stooped posture, and difficulty standing upright. Even without a diagnosed fracture, safer movement habits can reduce strain on the spine. Bend through your hips and knees instead of rounding your back, keep objects close to your body, and avoid lifting loads that feel too heavy or awkward.

A physical therapist can show you safer ways to get out of bed, reach into cabinets, carry laundry, and work in the garden. Appropriate strengthening and posture training may also make standing and walking more comfortable. Contact a healthcare professional if you develop sudden back pain, a noticeable posture change, or loss of height. These symptoms can signal a vertebral fracture, even without a major accident.

### Rehabilitate after hip, spine, or wrist fractures

A fracture can affect much more than the injured bone. Hip fractures may reduce walking ability and independence. Wrist fractures can make dressing, cooking, driving, and using a cane or walker more difficult. Vertebral fractures may limit standing, lifting, sleeping, and participation in activities you once enjoyed. Rehabilitation should address healing along with the practical skills needed to return home and resume daily routines.

Recovery often includes gradual strengthening, balance work, mobility training, pain management, and fall prevention. Your medical team may also review osteoporosis treatment to reduce the risk of another fracture. Cleveland Clinic describes follow-up visits and repeat bone-density testing as part of ongoing care. Continue rehabilitation even after pain improves if weakness, stiffness, or balance problems remain.

### Return safely to hobbies, travel, work, and daily activities

Osteoporosis treatment can support a gradual return to meaningful activities, but recovery should depend on function rather than a calendar alone. Before resuming golf, hiking, gardening, skiing, long-distance travel, or physically demanding work, ask your healthcare team how to adjust lifting, bending, carrying, and impact. You may benefit from a phased plan with shorter sessions, supportive footwear, rest breaks, or assistance with heavier tasks.

Travel planning can help you stay active without taking unnecessary risks. Arrange help with luggage, choose stable walking routes, and keep medications in their original containers. If you use a cane or walker, make sure it is fitted correctly and easy to bring with you. Discuss [fall prevention and bone-density testing with your provider](https://www.healthinaging.org/a-z-topic/osteoporosis/care-treatment), particularly if you have had a previous fracture or feel less steady than usual.

### Recognize urgent symptoms after a fall or during treatment

Seek prompt medical advice after a fall if you have severe pain, difficulty standing or walking, visible deformity, new weakness, numbness, or pain in the hip, back, wrist, or ribs. A fracture can occur after a minor injury, and some spinal fractures cause little immediate pain. Avoid trying to walk normally if movement feels unsafe or your symptoms are getting worse.

During treatment, report new or unusual symptoms such as persistent thigh or groin pain, jaw pain or swelling, muscle spasms, confusion, or signs of an allergic reaction. The appropriate response depends on your medication and health history. Cleveland Clinic recommends medical evaluation after a fracture caused by a minor injury. If symptoms are severe, sudden, or life-threatening, call emergency services.

### Know when to seek a comprehensive bone-health evaluation

Consider a comprehensive evaluation if you are over 50 and have not discussed fracture risk, have experienced a fracture after a minor fall, or have lost noticeable height. Long-term steroid use, early menopause, low body weight, thyroid or digestive disorders, kidney disease, and a family history of hip fractures can also increase concern. Some medications may weaken bone over time, even when they are necessary for another condition.

A thorough assessment may include a medical history, medication review, fracture-risk calculation, DXA scan, laboratory testing, and an evaluation of strength and balance. [Express Scripts recommends discussing osteoporosis screening and treatment](https://www.express-scripts.com/pharmacy/blog/treatment-options-for-osteoporosis) after age 50 or following a fracture. Bring a list of medications, supplements, previous fractures, falls, and activity goals so your care team can create a plan that protects your bones while supporting strength, mobility, and independence.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is osteoporosis, and why does treatment matter?**\
Osteoporosis weakens bones and makes fractures more likely, often without causing early symptoms. Treatment can reduce fracture risk, preserve mobility, and help you stay independent for activities such as walking, traveling, exercising, and managing daily tasks.

**Who should be evaluated for osteoporosis?**\
Adults 65 and older, people over 50 who have had a fracture after a minor injury, and those with risk factors should discuss screening with a healthcare professional. Earlier evaluation may be appropriate if you use long-term steroids, have low body weight, early menopause, thyroid or digestive conditions, kidney disease, low hormone levels, or a family history of hip fracture.

**How is osteoporosis diagnosed?**\
Diagnosis may include a DXA scan of the hip and spine, fracture history, FRAX risk assessment, medication review, and blood or urine tests. Your clinician may also recommend spinal imaging if you have height loss, posture changes, or new back pain. Bone density is important, but your overall fracture risk determines whether treatment may be appropriate.

**Can exercise and nutrition help manage osteoporosis?**\
Yes. Adequate calcium, vitamin D, protein, resistance training, weight-bearing activity, balance exercises, and fall-prevention measures all support bone and muscle health. If you have a fracture, chronic pain, or significant bone loss, work with a qualified healthcare professional or physical therapist to choose safe exercises and modify movements that strain the spine or painful joints.

**How long do osteoporosis medications need to be taken?**\
Treatment duration depends on your medication, fracture risk, bone-density results, side effects, kidney health, and whether you experience a new fracture. Some people may take a supervised break from certain bisphosphonates, while denosumab requires careful scheduling and a planned transition if it is stopped. Do not start, stop, or delay treatment without discussing it with your healthcare professional.
