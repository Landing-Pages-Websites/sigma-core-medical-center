## What Can a Recovery and Rehabilitation Clinic in Richmond, VA Treat?

“Recovery and rehabilitation clinic” can refer to different kinds of care. Some Richmond providers treat substance use disorders, while others focus on physical medicine and rehabilitation for pain, mobility, and daily function. A clinic may offer one specialty or coordinate care across several services, so confirm what it provides before your first visit.

Physical rehabilitation may be appropriate for adults managing chronic pain, joint problems, neuropathy, weakness, or changes in balance and mobility. Depending on your needs, care may focus on improving strength, movement, or the ability to handle everyday activities. Addiction treatment addresses different concerns, such as substance use and withdrawal risks, and may include outpatient or more structured programs.

Some people also need help with mental health concerns alongside substance use. In that case, it’s worth asking whether the clinic can assess both needs or coordinate with other providers. Addiction programs and physical rehabilitation clinics have different goals, and one type of clinic may not provide the other. Ask about the clinic’s specialties, available levels of care, and referral options so you can find support that fits your situation.

### Addiction to alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, stimulants, and other substances

Addiction treatment programs may support people experiencing problems with alcohol, opioids, benzodiazepines, stimulants, or other substances. These may include prescription medications, such as some pain or anxiety medicines, as well as substances like cocaine, methamphetamine, or fentanyl. The services and substances treated vary by provider, and programs may offer partial hospitalization, intensive outpatient, or outpatient care.

A clinician can help assess a person’s substance use, health history, and safety needs to recommend an appropriate level of care. Some people may need medical supervision during withdrawal; others may be able to attend outpatient treatment while living at home. When contacting a program, ask which substances it treats, whether it offers medical support, and how it decides which care option may be appropriate.

### Chronic pain, joint problems, neuropathy, and limited mobility

Physical rehabilitation may help people address chronic pain, joint problems, [neuropathy](/services/neuropathy), weakness, or difficulty with everyday movement. After assessing your needs, a care team may recommend a plan focused on strength, balance, range of motion, coordination, or practical tasks such as walking and using your hands. Treatment goals should reflect your health, abilities, and daily activities.

Services differ between clinics, so ask whether the team has experience with your specific concern and what types of care it provides. If pain or mobility changes are new, severe, or getting worse, consult a qualified medical professional about an evaluation before starting a new exercise routine.

### Co-occurring substance use and mental health needs

Some people experience substance use concerns alongside mental health conditions, such as anxiety, depression, or trauma-related symptoms. When both are present, coordinated care may help providers consider the full picture instead of addressing each concern in isolation. An assessment can clarify what support may be useful and whether additional specialists or services should be involved.

Richmond providers vary in the conditions they treat and the services they offer. When you call a clinic, ask whether its clinicians work with both concerns, how they coordinate treatment, and what options are available if you need more support than the program can provide.

### How addiction care differs from physical rehabilitation

Addiction care and physical rehabilitation address different needs. Addiction treatment may focus on substance use, withdrawal risks, counseling, and ongoing recovery support. Depending on a person’s health and circumstances, care may take place in an outpatient program or a more structured setting. The appropriate level of care should be determined with qualified professionals.

Physical rehabilitation typically focuses on movement, strength, pain-related function, and everyday activities. It may involve an assessment followed by a plan that includes guided exercise, hands-on care, or other non-surgical treatment. A clinic that offers one type of care may not provide the other, though some people need support from multiple providers. Ask what services are available on-site and whether the clinic coordinates referrals.

## Which Care Options May Fit Your Needs?

The right care depends on your symptoms, health history, daily responsibilities, and the support available to you. Addiction treatment and physical rehabilitation address different needs, but some people may benefit from coordinated care. Services vary by provider, so ask what each clinic offers and whether its clinicians can address your specific concerns.

### Addiction care: detox, residential, partial hospitalization, intensive outpatient, and outpatient

Addiction care can include medically supervised detox, residential treatment, partial hospitalization (PHP), intensive outpatient (IOP), and outpatient services. These options differ in how often you receive care and whether you stay at a treatment facility. A clinician can recommend a level of care based on withdrawal risk, substance use history, overall health, and your situation at home. Withdrawal from alcohol or certain medications can be dangerous, so seek medical guidance before stopping or reducing use.

Richmond-area programs offer several levels of treatment. Contact programs directly to confirm current services, eligibility, and whether they can meet your medical needs.

### Medical supervision, medication-assisted treatment, counseling, and trauma-informed care

An addiction care plan may include medical supervision, medication, counseling, and ongoing support. Medication-assisted treatment may help some people manage cravings or withdrawal as part of a broader treatment plan. A clinician should review your health conditions and medications before recommending care, especially if you also manage chronic pain or other ongoing health concerns.

Counseling can help you identify triggers, develop coping strategies, and address challenges that affect recovery. Some providers offer approaches such as cognitive behavioral therapy, motivational interviewing, and trauma-informed care. Ask which services are available, who provides them, and how the care team will account for your medical history.

### Personalized care for pain, mobility, recovery, and physical function

If pain, joint problems, neuropathy, or limited mobility affect your daily life, ask about care tailored to your needs and goals. An assessment may consider your symptoms, movement, strength, balance, medical history, and current medications. You might want to walk more comfortably, improve balance, return to a valued activity, or manage everyday tasks with greater ease.

Rehabilitation services vary, but may include [non-surgical pain relief](/services/pain-relief), supervised exercise, and help building functional skills. Ask how the team tracks progress, adjusts your plan, and adapts activities to your abilities and health conditions.

### Coordinated addiction, mental health, and medical care

When substance use occurs alongside depression, anxiety, trauma, chronic pain, or other health concerns, coordinated care can help providers consider the whole person. With your permission, members of your care team may share relevant information, review medications, and align treatment goals. Ask whether a clinic works with mental health professionals, primary care providers, rehabilitation specialists, or addiction programs, and how referrals are arranged.

People with complex or severe injuries may need specialized, more intensive rehabilitation than an outpatient clinic provides. Your primary care provider can help you find the right setting and confirm eligibility.

## How Can You Choose the Right Clinic?

The right clinic should offer care that fits your health needs, goals, and daily life. Some clinics focus on addiction treatment, while others provide physical rehabilitation or medical care for pain and mobility. If you need support in more than one area, ask how the clinic coordinates care or connects patients with other providers. These questions can help you compare options in Richmond and choose a setting where you feel informed, respected, and supported.

### Verify licensing, clinician credentials, specialties, and levels of care

Confirm that the clinic is licensed to provide the services you need. Ask who will assess and treat you, what credentials they hold, and whether they have experience with concerns such as substance use, chronic pain, joint problems, or reduced mobility. Make sure the clinic offers the right level of care: addiction services may range from outpatient counseling to residential treatment, while physical rehabilitation may involve medical evaluation and supervised exercise. These services are different, so ask what the clinic provides and when it refers patients elsewhere. If you’re considering medication-assisted treatment, ask which medications are available and who manages them.

### Ask how assessments guide and update care plans

A care plan should reflect your health history, needs, and goals, rather than follow a fixed template. Ask what the first assessment covers, who reviews the findings, and how your preferences inform recommendations. For physical concerns, the discussion may include pain, mobility, medications, medical history, and the activities you hope to return to. Addiction care may also assess mental health, safety, substance use, and support at home. Find out how often the team reviews your progress and what happens if a treatment isn’t helping or your needs change. Ask whether clinicians coordinate recommendations when you have multiple concerns, and whether planning is collaborative and individualized.

### Check the setting, accessibility, privacy, and medical supervision

Think about whether the clinic’s location and environment will make attending care manageable. Ask about wheelchair access, parking, public transit, visit times, and whether a support person can accompany you. For residential care, find out about rooms, shared spaces, visiting policies, and privacy. If treatment involves withdrawal, medication changes, significant health conditions, or intensive rehabilitation, ask what medical supervision is available and how urgent concerns are handled. You should know whom to contact if your symptoms change between visits. Ask how any clinic you’re considering can accommodate your needs.

### Compare insurance, costs, payment options, and transportation

Ask for a clear explanation of costs before starting care. Confirm whether the clinic accepts your insurance and whether your recommended services are covered. Copays, deductibles, and coverage can vary by provider and level of care, so check details with both the clinic and your insurer. If you’ll pay out of pocket, request written rates and ask about payment plans. Remember to consider practical expenses too, including transportation, parking, and time away from work or caregiving. If getting to visits is a concern, ask whether the clinic can suggest local transportation resources. Many programs share information about insurance and services online, but contact any clinic directly to confirm its current policies.

### Ask about family involvement, cultural responsiveness, and referral partnerships

If you’d like a family member or trusted person involved, ask how the clinic includes them and what consent is needed before staff can share your information. Family education or counseling may help, but participation should reflect your preferences. Ask how the clinic supports people with different cultural backgrounds, languages, beliefs, and accessibility needs. If you’re managing more than one concern, such as substance use alongside chronic pain or depression, find out how the clinic coordinates with other medical and behavioral health providers. Ask what happens when you need a service the clinic doesn’t offer, and whether staff can help arrange a referral. Use conversations with prospective clinics to understand how they’ll support you.

## What Happens When You Contact a Clinic?

Your first call is a chance to explain what kind of support you’re looking for and find out whether the clinic may be a fit. You may speak with an admissions coordinator or another staff member before meeting with a clinician. You don’t need to have every answer ready. Take notes, ask questions, and share information at a pace that feels manageable.

### Call or get a referral to confirm available services

Call the clinic directly or ask your healthcare provider for a referral. Briefly describe your main concerns, such as substance use, mental health, pain, or mobility, and ask which services are available. Programs differ: a clinic may offer outpatient counseling, for example, but not detox or residential treatment. Ask whether staff can assess your needs and explain where they’ll refer you if the clinic doesn’t provide the right level of care. A referral may also come from a hospital, family member, or treatment provider. Confirm current services, eligibility, and location directly with the clinic before making plans.

### Prepare for a screening, assessment, and care recommendations

A first conversation may include a brief screening about your health, substance use, immediate needs, and safety. If the clinic may be able to help, staff can describe the next steps for a more complete assessment. A clinician may ask about symptoms, medical history, previous treatment, and what you hope to accomplish. Answer as openly as you can; the information helps guide recommendations, not assign blame. Ask who will review the assessment with you and how the clinic decides which level of care may be appropriate. You can also ask whether recommendations may change as your health, goals, or circumstances change.

### Gather health history, medication details, insurance information, and questions

Before your visit, make a short list of health conditions, allergies, previous treatment, and medications. Include prescriptions, over-the-counter medicines, and supplements, with doses if you know them. Bringing medication containers or a current medication list can make this easier. Have your insurance card and coverage details ready, or ask about self-pay rates and other payment options if you don’t have insurance. Write down questions about confidentiality, medical supervision, family involvement, and what support is available between visits. This information helps the clinic understand your situation and explain possible next steps. If you’re unsure what to bring, call ahead and ask.

### Ask about admission, scheduling, and what to bring

Before you finish the call, make sure you understand what happens next. Ask whether you need a referral, how soon an assessment can take place, and whether visits are in person or remote. If admission is recommended, confirm the process, expected timing, and what you should bring. You may also want to ask about transportation, accessibility, visiting hours, and whether a family member or support person can attend. Find out whom to contact if you need to change your visit. Admission at some programs may begin with a referral or phone screening. Follow the specific instructions from the clinic, since procedures and requirements vary.

### Get urgent medical help for severe withdrawal symptoms; don’t attempt risky withdrawal alone

Withdrawal from alcohol or certain medications, including benzodiazepines, can cause serious medical problems. A seizure, trouble breathing, fainting, severe confusion, or hallucinations calls for urgent medical attention. Call 911 or go to an emergency department rather than waiting for a routine visit. Don’t try to manage severe withdrawal alone or stop alcohol or medication suddenly without medical guidance. Contact a healthcare professional or clinic to discuss a safe plan. If you’re experiencing suicidal thoughts or an emotional crisis, call or text [988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline](https://988lifeline.org/). For immediate physical danger, call 911.

## What Support Helps Recovery Continue?

Recovery often involves more than attending visits. A useful support plan can address substance use, mental health, physical function, and the practical demands of daily life. The right mix depends on the person’s needs and should be planned with qualified clinicians.

### Use family education, counseling, practical support, and healthy boundaries

Family members can offer support by learning about substance use and recovery, listening without judgment, and helping with practical needs such as transportation or daily tasks. Counseling can give individuals and families a structured place to work through communication challenges and agree on boundaries. Support does not mean taking responsibility for someone else’s choices or covering up harmful behavior. It means offering care while protecting everyone’s well-being.

Ask the treatment team whether family education or counseling is available, and whether the person in recovery wants relatives involved.

### Plan for aftercare, follow-up, relapse prevention, and peer support

Before a treatment program ends, ask what support will continue afterward. A transition plan may include follow-up visits, counseling, medication management when appropriate, peer groups, and steps for responding to cravings or a return to substance use. Write down who to contact, where care will take place, and what to do if a planned support option is unavailable.

Peer support can connect people with others who understand recovery firsthand, while professional follow-up helps keep care responsive to changing needs. Some programs also offer alumni services to continue support after treatment. Ask providers which services are available and how they fit the person’s care plan.

### Explore Richmond resources and recovery housing, when appropriate

Local services may help fill gaps between levels of care. Depending on a person’s situation, options can include outpatient counseling, peer support, community services, or recovery housing. Recovery housing may provide structure and a substance-free living environment, but it does not replace medical or behavioral health treatment. Ask about eligibility, house expectations, costs, accessibility, and how residents connect with clinical care.

A clinician or case manager can help identify resources that match the person’s treatment plan, health needs, and living situation, and can help confirm current programs, requirements, and availability.

### Continue care for pain, mobility, and physical function

Substance use recovery and physical rehabilitation may involve different providers, but both can matter to overall health. Ongoing pain, joint problems, neuropathy, or limited mobility can affect sleep, mood, and daily routines. Share these concerns with the care team, particularly if pain treatment or medications are changing. A clinician can help arrange an appropriate evaluation and discuss options that account for the person’s health history and recovery goals.

When recommended by a qualified professional, guided rehabilitation exercise and non-surgical care may support movement and everyday function. Ask any provider which services are offered and whether a referral, insurance authorization, or accessibility arrangements are needed.

### Plan for costs, cravings, triggers, stigma, and limited support

Practical barriers can make it harder to stay connected with care. Before treatment begins, ask about insurance coverage, out-of-pocket costs, payment options, transportation, and community assistance. Cost can be a significant obstacle, so review common barriers to treatment and ask providers what resources may be available.

It can also help to identify situations, people, or places linked to cravings, then plan whom to contact when they arise. Everyday triggers can be a challenge during outpatient care. Ask the treatment team about coping strategies and added support. If stigma or limited social support makes asking for help difficult, consider confidential counseling or peer services. Share urgent safety concerns with a clinician promptly.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does a recovery and rehabilitation clinic in Richmond treat physical conditions or substance use?**\
It depends on the clinic. Some providers focus on addiction treatment, while others offer physical rehabilitation for pain, weakness, or mobility challenges. Call ahead to confirm the services and specialties available.

**Can I get help for chronic pain and substance use at the same clinic?**\
Some clinics coordinate with other providers, but they may not treat both concerns on-site. Ask whether the team can work with your medical and behavioral health providers or help arrange a referral.

**How do I know what level of addiction care may be appropriate?**\
A qualified clinician can assess your health, substance use, withdrawal risks, and circumstances to recommend next steps. Do not stop alcohol or certain medications suddenly without medical advice, since withdrawal can be dangerous.

**What should I bring to my first visit?**\
Bring a list of your health conditions, medications, allergies, and previous treatment, along with your insurance information if you have it. Write down questions about costs, services, privacy, and follow-up care.

**When should I seek urgent medical help instead of waiting for a visit?**\
Call 911 or go to an emergency department for severe withdrawal symptoms, trouble breathing, fainting, seizures, severe confusion, or hallucinations. For a mental health crisis or suicidal thoughts, call or text 988.
